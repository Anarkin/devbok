Angular

study · Angular 22.1 · released 2026-06-03

Angular 22 (3 June 2026) is the release where the signal-first rewrite of the framework stopped being a preview and became the default: OnPush is now the default change detection strategy for new components, zoneless apps are what ng new generates, and Signal Forms, the Resource API (resource, rxResource, httpResource) and @angular/aria all graduated to stable. It also raises the floor hard — TypeScript 6 and Node 22 are required, strictTemplates is on by default, and paramsInheritanceStrategy silently flips to 'always' — so an upgrade is a real piece of work rather than a version bump. The single most quotable recent change is governance, not code: from v22 onward Angular moves to one major per year, dropping each June (v23 in June 2027), with support stretched from 18 to 24 months (12 active + 12 LTS) — the answer to a decade of enterprise complaints about the upgrade treadmill. Meanwhile the legacy surface is shrinking on a published timetable: @angular/animations was deprecated in v20.2 and is slated for removal in v23, and NgModules, *ngIf/*ngFor, constructor injection and Karma are all still everywhere in real codebases while being nobody's recommendation. Assumption: "Angular" here means the modern framework documented at angular.dev (v2 through v22), not AngularJS 1.x, which reached end of life on 31 December 2021.

01Angular today: releases and upgrades

Angular is not one framework with a long history; it is two frameworks that share a name, plus a third one hiding inside the second. AngularJS (1.x, 2010) was a dirty-checking, scope-based library that died on 31 December 2021. Angular 2+ (2016) was a full rewrite: TypeScript, a compiler, NgModules, RxJS, zone-based change detection. And since roughly v16 a third Angular has been growing inside it — standalone components, signals, no zones, no NgModules, no RxJS requirement — to the point where an app written today shares almost no idioms with one written in 2019 even though both compile with the same CLI. Every serious Angular interview is, underneath, a test of which of those three you actually know, and whether you can navigate a codebase that contains all three at once.

The mental model to carry into the room: Angular's public surface is a set of deliberately staged migrations, each with a version where it became available, a version where it became the default, and a version where the old thing goes away. If you can name those three numbers for the four or five migrations that matter, you sound current without memorising a changelog. If you can also say why each one happened — what the old design cost — you sound like a lead.

The cadence change — the thing to mention out loud

Through v22 Angular shipped a major every six months with 18 months of support (6 active + 12 LTS). Starting after v22 that becomes one major per year, every June: v23 in June 2027, v24 in June 2028, with 24 months of support per major (12 months active + 12 months LTS) and four to six minors roughly every eight weeks in between. v22 itself is stretched across the full year — 22.1 landed in August 2026, and 22.2–22.5 will carry the new features that would previously have needed a major.

This matters more than it looks. The single loudest enterprise complaint about Angular for a decade was "we finish an upgrade and the next one is already out". A yearly major with two years of support means an organisation can now be one major behind and still be fully supported, which changes the upgrade policy you should propose in an interview: stay on the latest major but adopt it in the first minor (x.1), not on release day; never let yourself fall two majors behind, because ng update only supports one major hop at a time.

How upgrades actually work

Angular's upgrade story is unusually good and candidates undersell it. ng update runs schematics — codemods shipped with the framework that rewrite your source. Every breaking change the team can automate, they do automate. The interview-relevant consequence: an Angular upgrade is mostly mechanical, and the risk lives in the handful of behavioural changes that cannot be codemodded because they depend on runtime data.

Reading the "which Angular is this?" signals in a codebase

A useful five-second triage you can narrate in a code-review question. Open any component file and look for these tells, in order:

  • @NgModule anywhere → pre-v19 architecture; expect declarations, SharedModule, and lazy loading via loadChildren pointing at modules.
  • constructor(private http: HttpClient) → pre-v14 idiom; inject() is the modern form and is required by @Service.
  • *ngIf / *ngFor / ngSwitch → pre-v17 templates. Still supported, but @if/@for are the recommendation and @for requires track.
  • @Input() / @Output() decorators → pre-v19; the signal forms are input(), input.required(), output(), model().
  • zone.js in polyfills and ChangeDetectorRef.detectChanges() calls → zone-based app.
  • animations: [trigger(...)] in a component decorator → deprecated since 20.2, removed in v23.
  • karma.conf.js → pre-v21 testing; Vitest is the default now.

Interview trap

Saying "Angular is the one with two-way binding and dirty checking". That is AngularJS. Interviewers use this to separate people who used Angular from people who read about it in 2015. Modern Angular is one-way by default, uses a compiler that generates template instructions ahead of time, and since v22 does fine-grained, signal-driven change detection with no dirty checking at all in a zoneless app. The second half of the same trap is the opposite error: claiming zone.js is gone. It is not — it is no longer installed by default in new apps (v21) and no longer needed, but every existing app still ships it until someone runs the zoneless migration.

Gotcha

"We'll skip a major and do them both at once." ng update only supports one major version hop at a time, because the schematics for version N assume the codebase already looks like version N−1. Skipping v21 to go from v20 to v22 means running ng update twice anyway, and doing them in one commit means you cannot bisect the breakage. The correct plan is one PR per major, each one green in CI, and the intermediate commit is genuinely runnable. The official update guide generates the exact step list for any version pair.

Try it — pin the timeline to something you can run

1. Scaffold a v22 app and read what you get for free: npx @angular/cli@latest new tour --style=css --ssr=false. Open app.config.ts, app.ts and package.json and list what is absent compared to a v17 app you have seen — no app.module.ts, no zone.js in the dependencies, no karma.conf.js, and no changeDetection line on the component (because OnPush is now implied).

2. Break the upgrade path on purpose: in a v20 app, run ng update @angular/core@22 directly and read the error. Then do it properly through v21.

Expected outcome

The direct hop is refused: the CLI reports an incompatible peer/major jump and tells you to update to the intermediate major first. Doing it in two steps succeeds, and after the v22 step you will find changeDetection: ChangeDetectionStrategy.Eager stamped onto components that never declared a strategy — that annotation is the migration's honest record of "this component relied on the old default", and removing them one at a time is the modernisation backlog.

Interview Q&A

What is the current Angular release and support policy, and how would you set an upgrade policy for a large estate?

Angular 22 shipped on 3 June 2026 and is the last release of the six-month cadence — from v23 onward there is one major per year each June, with 24 months of support per major, split into 12 months of active support and 12 months of LTS, plus four to six minors every eight weeks or so. For an estate I would set the policy as: track the latest major but adopt at the first minor rather than on release day, so the ecosystem has caught up; never allow an app to fall more than one major behind, because ng update only hops one major at a time and being two behind doubles the work; and run the upgrade as one PR per major with CI green at each step. I would also budget explicitly for the non-automated changes, because the automated ones are basically free — for v22 that is strictTemplates, the paramsInheritanceStrategy default flip, and the Node/TypeScript floor. The yearly cadence changes the calculus for people who were deliberately staying on LTS: with 24 months of support, being one major behind is now a supported steady state rather than a slow-motion emergency.

A team says "we can't upgrade, we're on Angular 14 with 400 components". How do you get them current?

I would separate the version bump from the modernisation, because conflating them is what makes these projects fail. Step one is purely mechanical: run ng update one major at a time, v14 to v15 to v16 and so on, accepting every schematic, with no refactoring in those PRs. That gets them onto a supported version in a few weeks of mostly-CI work; the real cost centres are usually third-party libraries that stopped being maintained and any code touching internal Angular APIs. Step two is modernisation, and it is optional and incremental: the standalone migration, the control-flow migration, the signals migration, and the zoneless migration are all separate schematics you can run months apart. Step three is the cultural part — new code uses the new idioms, and old code gets converted when it is touched, enforced by lint rules rather than by a migration sprint. The one thing I would not do is a rewrite: Angular's schematics exist precisely so you never have to, and a rewrite on a 400-component app is a year of no feature work.

Why did the Angular team move to a yearly major release, and what does it cost?

The six-month cadence was designed to keep breaking changes small and frequent, which is genuinely better than a giant migration every three years, but it collided with how enterprises actually schedule work: a six-month major with 18 months of support meant that by the time an upgrade cleared change management, the next one was out. Moving to yearly majors with 24 months of support means an organisation can be one version behind and still fully supported, which is what most of them wanted. The cost is that breaking changes now batch up — a yearly major has twice the delta of a six-month one, so the individual upgrade is bigger even if the annual total is the same. The team is mitigating that by shipping more in minors: features that used to wait for a major now land in 22.1 through 22.5 as long as they are not breaking. The other cost is optics: a framework that ships majors more slowly can look less alive, so expect more of the visible progress to appear in minors and in the docs.

What actually breaks when you go from Angular 21 to 22?

Four things, and only one of them is quiet. The loud ones are the platform floor — TypeScript 6, dropping 5.9, and Node 22, dropping Node 20 — which mostly hurts through third-party libraries and CI images rather than your own code. strictTemplates becoming the default is loud but automated: the migration writes your old setting into tsconfig so nothing breaks, and turning it on is then a deliberate piece of work. OnPush becoming the default is also automated, by stamping ChangeDetectionStrategy.Eager onto components that did not declare a strategy. The quiet one is paramsInheritanceStrategy defaulting to 'always', which has no migration: child routes now inherit parent params and route data. Nine times out of ten that fixes bugs, but if any code branched on data['x'] being undefined in a child, it now silently takes the other branch. That is the one I would grep for and cover with a test before upgrading.

What is the difference between an Angular deprecation and a removal, and how do you track them?

Angular's deprecation policy is that a deprecated API keeps working for at least two major versions, is documented in the deprecations list with the version it was deprecated in, and ships with a migration where one is possible. @angular/animations is the current worked example: deprecated in 20.2 in favour of the template-level animate.enter and animate.leave bindings plus plain CSS, and scheduled for removal in v23. That gives roughly two years of notice. I track this with three mechanisms rather than by reading blogs: the deprecations page as the source of truth, build warnings in CI treated as errors on a schedule (not immediately, or nobody upgrades), and a dependency dashboard so the third-party libraries hit their own deadlines. The lead-level point is that the deprecation window is a planning input, not a warning to silence: a package removed in v23 means the work must land inside the v22 year, not in the v23 upgrade PR.

How does Angular's opinionated, batteries-included model compare to React's, and when is that the wrong choice?

Angular ships the router, HTTP client, forms, testing, i18n, SSR, build system and now accessibility primitives as one versioned unit, with codemods that upgrade all of them together. That is a decisive advantage for long-lived, many-team applications: the architecture question "which router, which state library, which forms library" is already answered, new joiners are productive across teams, and upgrades are one coordinated event instead of twelve independent ones. The cost is ceiling and agility — the framework's opinion is the one you get, the bundle floor is higher than a minimal React app, and adopting a genuinely different pattern means fighting the grain. I would choose Angular for a product with a five-year horizon and many contributors, a large forms and data-grid surface, or a strict accessibility and i18n requirement. I would not choose it for a marketing site, a small team optimising for hiring speed in a React-heavy market, or anything where the last 30KB of bundle is the business. And I would be honest in the interview that since signals and standalone components landed, the two ecosystems converged a lot: the day-to-day authoring experience is far more similar than it was in 2019.

A candidate says "we use AngularJS". What do you ask next?

I would clarify which one they mean, without making it a gotcha, because plenty of people say AngularJS when they mean Angular 15. If they genuinely mean 1.x, the follow-up questions are about the migration position: AngularJS hit end of life on 31 December 2021, so what is the plan — commercial extended support, a hybrid app running both frameworks under ngUpgrade, a strangler-fig rewrite behind a reverse proxy, or a deliberate freeze? Each of those is a legitimate answer and each says something different about the organisation. I would also probe whether they understand what actually changed between the two, since it is the clearest test of whether they know why modern Angular is built the way it is: scopes and dirty checking versus a compiler and a component tree, $digest loops versus zones versus signals, and DI by string name versus DI by token.

What are Angular schematics and when have you written one?

Schematics are Angular's codemod and code-generation system: a TypeScript program that takes a virtual file tree, applies rules to it, and writes the result atomically, so a failed schematic leaves no partial state. Angular ships three kinds — generation schematics behind ng generate, update schematics that ng update runs automatically on version bumps, and library schematics behind ng add. The interesting ones for a lead are the update schematics, because they are why Angular upgrades are cheap: the team's rule is that if a breaking change can be automated, it must be. In a platform team I would write two kinds. The first is a generation schematic for the house pattern — a feature folder with the route, a smart component, a store and the test file already wired the way we want, which is worth far more than a documented convention nobody follows. The second is a migration schematic when we do our own breaking change in an internal library, so consuming teams run ng update @acme/ui instead of reading a changelog. They use the TypeScript AST, so writing one is a real but small skill, and it pays for itself the second time a rename touches fifty repositories.

Is Angular still worth learning given how much has changed? What would you tell a skeptical senior React engineer?

I would show them the delta rather than defend the history. A modern Angular component is a class with input(), output() and signal() fields and a template with @if/@for — that is conceptually the same model as a React function component with props and useState, minus the hook rules, the dependency arrays, and the memoisation ceremony, because signals are pull-based and glitch-free by construction. Zoneless change detection means Angular now updates precisely the views that read a changed signal, which is closer to Solid's model than to React's re-render-and-diff. The things Angular still does that React does not are the ones an enterprise cares about: one versioned package set with automated upgrades, DI as a first-class architectural tool, and a forms and accessibility story in the box. The honest downsides I would name: the ecosystem is smaller, the framework carries a decade of legacy idioms you will meet in real code, and TypeScript is mandatory rather than optional. The pitch is not "Angular is better", it is "Angular is now a signal-based framework with an unusually good migration story, and the 2016 objections are mostly answered".

How do you decide when to adopt an experimental or developer-preview Angular API?

Angular's labels are meaningful and I use them as the decision rule. Experimental means the API may change or be removed in any release, including a minor — I use it in a spike or behind a feature flag in a non-critical surface, never in a shared library. Developer preview means the shape is close to final and the team wants feedback, but it is still outside the semver guarantee; I will use it in application code if the blast radius is one feature and I am willing to do a rename. Stable means it is covered by the deprecation policy and I will build a shared abstraction on it. The practical test I apply on top of that is: how many files would a rename touch, and is there a codemod-shaped escape? Signal Forms is the perfect case study — it was experimental in v21 and I would have kept it to one greenfield feature; it went stable in v22 and is now a defensible default for new forms. WebMCP tooling in v22 is explicitly experimental, so it belongs in a prototype, not in the checkout flow.

02Components, templates, control flow

A component in Angular is a class decorated with metadata that the Angular compiler reads at build time and turns into an imperative render function. That last part is the piece candidates skip, and it explains almost everything else. Angular does not interpret your template at runtime; the compiler parses it, type-checks it against your class, and emits a template instruction stream — create-mode instructions that build DOM once, and update-mode instructions that write into it. Understanding that "the template is compiled code, not a string" is what makes the rest of the framework make sense: why the template can only see what the compiler can prove it can see, why imports is mandatory, why strictTemplates can catch a type error inside an interpolation, and why control flow was eventually moved from structural directives into first-class syntax.

The running example for this unit: a TicketCard in a support-desk app. It takes a ticket, projects a footer, emits a status change, exposes a signal input for density, and renders a list of comments. Everything below is a variation on it.

The modern component, end to end

import { Component, input, output, model, computed, viewChild, ElementRef } from '@angular/core';

@Component({
  selector: 'ticket-card',
  // No `standalone: true` — standalone is the default since v19.
  // No `changeDetection` line — OnPush is the default since v22.
  imports: [PriorityBadge, RelativeTime],
  template: `
    <article class="card" [class.dense]="density() === 'dense'">
      <header>
        <h3>{{ ticket().title }}</h3>
        <priority-badge [level]="ticket().priority" />
      </header>

      @if (ticket().assignee; as assignee) {
        <p>Assigned to {{ assignee.name }}</p>
      } @else {
        <p class="warn">Unassigned</p>
      }

      @let recent = ticket().comments.slice(-3);
      @for (c of recent; track c.id) {
        <p><b>{{ c.author }}</b> <relative-time [at]="c.at" /></p>
      } @empty {
        <p class="muted">No comments yet</p>
      }

      <button #resolve (click)="status.set('resolved')">Resolve</button>
      <ng-content select="[footer]" />
    </article>
  `,
})
export class TicketCard {
  // Signal inputs (v17.2 dev preview, stable v19). Read them like functions.
  readonly ticket = input.required<Ticket>();
  readonly density = input<'dense' | 'comfortable'>('comfortable');

  // Two-way binding: [(status)] on the parent.
  readonly status = model<TicketStatus>('open');

  // Output: a function-style API, no decorator, not an Observable.
  readonly escalated = output<string>();

  // Signal query — resolves before ngAfterViewInit, and is itself reactive.
  private readonly resolveBtn = viewChild.required<ElementRef<HTMLButtonElement>>('resolve');

  readonly isStale = computed(() => Date.now() - this.ticket().updatedAt > 7 * 864e5);
}

Built-in control flow, and the bits people get wrong

The block syntax became stable in v17 and is compiler syntax, not directives — which is why it needs no imports entry and why it type-narrows properly. Three details carry most of the interview value:

  • track is mandatory on the loop block. *ngFor defaulted to identity tracking and silently destroyed and rebuilt every DOM node when a list was reassigned; the block form forces you to state the identity key. Use a stable business id. track $index is correct only for primitive lists that are appended to, never for lists that reorder — reordering with $index re-renders every row and drops focus and input state.
  • The @empty block replaces the *ngIf="list.length === 0" sibling, and it evaluates the collection once instead of twice.
  • @let (stable v18.1) declares a template-local variable, which finally removes the *ngIf="expensive() as value" hack people used to memoise a value in a template.

Selectors, and the selectorless question

Angular's component model is selector-based: a component declares a CSS selector, you list it in imports, and the compiler matches it against your template. That indirection is the source of a specific class of bug — you forget the imports entry, and <ticket-card> silently renders as an unknown element with no error, because in a non-strict template an unrecognised tag is just an unknown HTML element.

Selectorless components — importing the class and writing <TicketCard /> directly, so a missing import is a compile error — have been prototyped by the Angular team since 2025 and are widely written about as a v22 feature. Be careful here: they are not on the official roadmap's completed list and are not in the v22 release notes, so the accurate interview answer is "it is an RFC-stage direction, landing incrementally, and I would not build on it yet". Knowing the difference between a shipped feature and a well-publicised prototype is itself a signal. The shipping fix for the same bug class today is strictTemplates (default in v22), which turns an unknown element into a compile error, plus the new v22 compiler diagnostics for duplicate input/output names and for a tag matched by two components.

Interview trap

Calling a signal input inside a field initialiser. readonly upper = this.name().toUpperCase(); throws, because signal inputs have no value until the parent binds them — the class field runs first. The fix is computed(() => this.name().toUpperCase()), which defers the read. This is the single most common error when a team first converts @Input() to input(), and interviewers like it because getting it right requires you to understand that a signal input is a reactive cell that is filled later, not a property assigned during construction. The same trap in reverse: input.required() read inside ngOnInit is fine, read inside the constructor is not.

Gotcha

track $index on a reorderable list. It compiles, it looks harmless, and it destroys your users' state. With track $index, moving row 3 to position 1 means Angular thinks item 1 changed value rather than that a node moved, so it re-renders every row in between: focus is lost, input values inside the rows are rebuilt, CSS transitions restart, and any child component state is thrown away. Use track item.id. If the collection genuinely has no id — a list of strings you only ever append to — $index is fine, and saying that out loud is the answer that scores.

Try it — break tracking and watch the DOM

1. Render a list of 5 objects with a text input inside each row and track $index. Type into the third input, then click a button that reverses the array. Now change to track item.id and repeat.

2. Convert a component from @Input() plus ngOnChanges to input() plus computed(), then deliberately move the computed body into a plain field initialiser and read the error.

Expected outcome

With $index, reversing wipes the typed value and the focus ring, and Chrome DevTools' paint flashing shows every row repainting. With track item.id, Angular moves the existing DOM nodes: the typed text follows its row, focus survives, and only the moved nodes flash. Exercise 2 throws NG0950: Input is required but no value is available yet (or a plain "cannot read properties of undefined" for an optional input) — proof that inputs are populated after construction, which is exactly why computed exists.

Interview Q&A

Why did Angular replace structural directives with built-in control flow, and what did it actually buy?

*ngIf and *ngFor were ordinary directives, which meant every conditional in the app paid for directive instantiation, an embedded view container and a differ, and the compiler could not reason about the control flow at all. Moving the block syntax into the compiler in v17 bought four things. Performance: the team measured roughly 30% faster rendering on list-heavy benchmarks, because the loop block uses a purpose-built diffing algorithm instead of the general IterableDiffer. Ergonomics: no CommonModule import, no ng-template gymnastics for an else branch. Type safety: the compiler narrows types across a conditional boundary properly, and in 21.2 the @default never; form gives real exhaustiveness checking on a union. And correctness by construction: track is mandatory, which turns the single most common Angular performance bug into a compile error. The old directives still work and the migration is a one-command schematic, so in a code review I would treat leftover *ngIf as debt rather than a bug.

What is the difference between input(), model() and output(), and when do you reach for model()?

input() creates a read-only signal that the parent writes and the child only reads; output() creates an emitter the child calls and the parent listens to; model() is a writable signal that is both, giving the parent the two-way binding syntax. Mechanically model() is exactly an input() plus an output named valueChange, declared once. I reach for model() only for genuine two-way UI state — a form control wrapper, a date picker's selected date, a dialog's open flag — where the child legitimately owns the interaction and the parent legitimately owns the value. I avoid it for anything where the change should be a semantic event rather than a value assignment: "the user resolved this ticket" is an output(), not a model(), because the parent may want to validate, confirm or reject it. The failure mode of over-using model() is that state ownership becomes ambiguous and you end up with two sources of truth that fight, which is exactly the problem one-way data flow was invented to solve.

How do signal queries differ from @ViewChild, and why does the timing change matter?

@ViewChild was resolved by the framework and written onto the property, so you had to know whether it was available in ngOnInit — only with static: true, and only if it was not inside a conditional — or in ngAfterViewInit, and it silently stayed undefined if you guessed wrong. viewChild() returns a signal, so there is no timing question at the API level: you read it when you need it, and if it is inside a false conditional you get undefined, or an error from viewChild.required(). The important consequence is that it is reactive — a computed or effect that reads viewChildren() re-runs when the set of matched elements changes, which used to require QueryList.changes and a subscription. The pattern this unlocks is DOM measurement without lifecycle hooks: an afterRenderEffect that reads a viewChild re-runs automatically when the element is replaced. I would still reach for ElementRef sparingly — direct DOM access breaks SSR unless it is inside afterNextRender.

Someone reports "my component renders but nothing shows and there's no error". Walk me through it.

The classic cause is a missing imports entry on a standalone component: the tag matches no known selector, so the browser treats it as an unknown element and renders an empty inline box. With strictTemplates — the default in v22 — this is now a compile error, so my first question is whether the project has it on; if not, turning it on is the fix and probably surfaces a dozen more. The other candidates, in the order I would check them: the selector does not match what the template writes, usually a hyphen or case mismatch or an attribute selector used as an element; the component is declared but the conditional around it is false because a signal was read without calling it, since @if (visible) rather than @if (visible()) is always truthy — a signal is a function object; the content is projected into a select slot that does not match; or the host element has zero height from CSS. In v22 I would also check the new duplicate-match diagnostic, which now errors when two components claim the same tag instead of silently picking one.

What is ViewEncapsulation and when would you change it?

By default Angular uses ViewEncapsulation.Emulated: the compiler rewrites your component CSS to add a generated attribute selector so styles cannot leak out. It is not the Shadow DOM — it is a build-time transformation, which is why global styles can still leak in, and why ::ng-deep works at all. ShadowDom uses the real platform feature: true isolation both ways, but you inherit its constraints — global stylesheets and design-token custom properties need explicit plumbing, form and accessibility semantics cross the boundary awkwardly, and third-party libraries that query the document break. None disables encapsulation entirely and dumps your CSS into the document. I change it in exactly two situations: ShadowDom when shipping a component that will be embedded in someone else's page as a custom element, and None for a top-level layout or theme component where the styles are genuinely global. In application code I treat ::ng-deep as a smell — it is deprecated, it is not scoped despite appearances, and the right answer is almost always a CSS custom property exposed by the child.

How does content projection work, and how does a projected node differ from the child's own template?

ng-content is a compile-time instruction that tells the renderer where to place nodes the parent already created. The key mental model is that projected content belongs to the parent, not the child: it is created in the parent's view, bound against the parent's context, and change-detected as part of the parent's view. That has three practical consequences. First, a projected node can use the parent's template variables and cannot use the child's. Second, if the child is OnPush and the parent is not, the projected content still updates on the parent's schedule — which is why "my OnPush component's projected content updates but its own template doesn't" is a normal observation rather than a bug. Third, ng-content is not a runtime API: you cannot loop over it, and putting it inside a conditional does not make the nodes lazy — they are created regardless. If you need lazy or repeated projection you need ng-template with ngTemplateOutlet, or an input taking a TemplateRef. Since v18, ng-content also supports fallback content for when nothing is projected, which removed a common wrapper-component hack.

When would you write a directive instead of a component, and what does hostDirectives change?

A component owns a template and therefore owns DOM; a directive attaches behaviour to DOM someone else owns. So the rule I use is: if the answer to "what markup does this produce?" is "none, it just makes existing markup behave differently", it is a directive. Tooltips, drag handles, autofocus, permission-based hiding, analytics instrumentation, and anything that must work on an arbitrary host element are all directives. hostDirectives, added in v15, changed this from a trade-off into composition: previously, sharing behaviour between components meant either inheritance — which does not compose, since you get one base class — or a wrapper element that pollutes the DOM and breaks flex and grid layouts. Now a component can declare a list of directives applied to its own host, choose which inputs and outputs to re-expose, and get their host bindings merged in. That is how the accessibility package and Angular Material build their primitives: the behaviour and ARIA wiring live in directives, and the styled component composes them. In a design system this is the mechanism I would standardise on, because it makes accessibility behaviour something you inherit by declaration rather than something each component re-implements.

What does strictTemplates actually check, and how would you introduce it to a large legacy app?

strictTemplates turns on the full set of template type-checking flags: input bindings are checked against the input's declared type rather than treated as any, DOM property bindings are checked against the DOM typings, the event object in an output binding is typed, template reference variables get their real types, safe navigation and pipes are checked, and unknown elements and properties become errors instead of warnings. It became the default in v22, with a migration that writes your existing setting into tsconfig.json so an upgrade does not break — meaning adopting it is now an explicit decision. In a large app I would not flip it globally; I would stage it with the intermediate flags. strictTemplates is a bundle, and its individual members — strictInputTypes, strictNullInputTypes, strictDomLocalRefTypes, strictAttributeTypes and the rest — can be enabled one at a time, so you land the cheap high-value ones first, fix the fallout in normal-sized PRs, and finish with the expensive ones. The two that generate the most noise in legacy code are strictNullInputTypes, because the async pipe produces T | null, and strictAttributeTypes, because a static attribute is always a string. Both are real bugs surfacing, which is the argument I would make to the team.

What are selectorless components and should we adopt them?

Selectorless is a proposed direction where you import the component class and use it in the template as a tag bound to that symbol, rather than declaring a CSS selector string and listing the class in imports. The motivation is real: selectors are a global namespace with prefix collisions in large monorepos, a forgotten imports entry fails silently, and the indirection is invisible to editors and refactoring tools. My honest answer on adoption is no, not yet. The team has been prototyping it publicly since 2025 with an RFC, and a lot of blog coverage attached it to v22, but it is not in the v22 release notes and it is not listed as completed on the official roadmap — so anything relying on it today is relying on an unreleased or experimental surface. What I would do instead, right now, is turn on strictTemplates so an unmatched tag is a compile error, and adopt the v22 compiler diagnostics for duplicate selectors and duplicate input names, which fix the same failure modes with shipped features. Being able to draw that line — between a direction the team is pursuing and an API I can put in production — is the point.

In code review, what makes you reject an Angular component?

Five things, roughly in order of how often I see them. A function call in a template binding, which re-runs on every change detection pass of that view; the fix is a computed or a pure pipe. track $index on a list that can reorder, which silently destroys user state. A subscription without takeUntilDestroyed() or an async pipe, because manual subscribe in a component is where leaks live. Business logic in the component instead of a service — anything I could not test after deleting the template is misplaced. And a component with more than about five inputs, which is almost always two components pretending to be one. Beyond those I look for the things that are cheap now and expensive later: a leftover ChangeDetectionStrategy.Eager annotation from the v22 migration, direct DOM access outside afterNextRender which will break the moment SSR is enabled, and any in a template-facing type, since strictTemplates cannot help you through an any.

03Signals: the reactivity graph

Signals are the single most important thing to understand about modern Angular, because every other 2024–2026 change is downstream of them: zoneless change detection, OnPush by default, the Resource API, Signal Forms, signal queries, and the DevTools signal graph all exist because the framework finally has a way to know exactly which views depend on which state.

The mental model: a signal is a node in a dependency graph that remembers who read it. When you read count() inside a reactive context — a computed, an effect, or a template — Angular records an edge from count to that consumer. When you write count.set(4), Angular does not recompute anything; it walks the edges and marks every downstream node dirty. Nothing is evaluated until someone reads it. That is the difference between signals and every event-emitter-based reactive system you have used: signals are pull-based with push-based invalidation. Push-based systems (RxJS, event emitters, Vue 2 watchers) recompute eagerly and can produce intermediate wrong values; pull-based systems compute lazily and can never be observed in an inconsistent state.

The three primitives, and the fourth

import { signal, computed, effect, linkedSignal, untracked } from '@angular/core';

// 1. Writable state
const query = signal('');
const page  = signal(1);
query.set('angular');
page.update(p => p + 1);

// 2. Derived state: lazy, memoised, glitch-free
const cacheKey = computed(() => `${query()}::${page()}`);

// 3. Side effects at the edge of the system
effect(() => {
  // Runs after the value settles, once per change detection pass, not once per write.
  localStorage.setItem('lastQuery', query());
});

// 4. Writable state DERIVED from other state (stable v20)
const selectedId = linkedSignal<Row[], string | null>({
  source: () => rows(),
  computation: (rows, prev) =>
    rows.some(r => r.id === prev?.value) ? prev!.value : rows[0]?.id ?? null,
});
// The user can override it: selectedId.set('r7')
// ...but when `rows` changes it re-derives, keeping the selection if it survived.

linkedSignal is the one people miss, and it is the answer to a genuinely common problem: local state that must reset when its source changes, but is user-writable in between. Before v19 you wrote that as an effect that called .set() on another signal, which is exactly the anti-pattern the team warns about — it creates a write-during-read cycle, runs a tick late, and is hard to test. If you find yourself writing effect(() => other.set(source())), the answer is almost always linkedSignal.

Glitch-free, and why it matters

The canonical demonstration. With plain callbacks or naive push reactivity, an observer of total can see an impossible intermediate value while the two upstream writes are applied one at a time. Signals cannot produce that, because total is not computed until it is read, and by then both writes have landed.

const price = signal(100);
const qty   = signal(2);
const total = computed(() => price() * qty());

effect(() => console.log('total', total()));   // logs 200

// A "sale": half price, double quantity. Total should never change.
price.set(50);
qty.set(4);
// A push-based system may log 100 (50 * 2) in between. Signals log 200 once.

Mechanically this works through version counters. Every producer has a version; every consumer records the versions it last saw. A write bumps the producer version and marks consumers possibly dirty rather than dirty. On read, a consumer walks its producers and only recomputes if one genuinely changed value — which means a write that sets the same value, or a chain where an intermediate computed produces an equal result, stops the propagation dead. That is equality-based short-circuiting, and it is why a computed that maps a large array to arr.length will not invalidate the view when the array changes but the length does not.

Equality is the sharpest edge

const rows = signal<Row[]>([]);

// WRONG: mutates in place. The reference is unchanged, so nothing invalidates.
rows().push(newRow);

// RIGHT: a new reference.
rows.update(r => [...r, newRow]);

// Or opt into a deeper comparison when you genuinely need it:
const filters = signal(defaultFilters, { equal: (a, b) => deepEqual(a, b) });

Signals compare with Object.is by default. Primitives behave the way everyone expects. Objects and arrays do not: mutating one and setting it back is a no-op, because the reference is identical. The two disciplined answers are immutable updates (what most teams do) or a custom equal. A custom equal is also a performance tool in the other direction — if a service re-fetches and produces a structurally identical object every 30 seconds, a deep equal stops that from invalidating half the UI.

When effect is the wrong tool — the important part

The Angular team's guidance is unusually blunt: most of the time you should not use effect. An effect is for pushing state out of the reactive graph into something that is not reactive. Everything that stays inside the graph should be a computed or a linkedSignal.

Interview trap

"Signals are just RxJS BehaviorSubjects." They are not, and the differences are exactly what an interviewer is fishing for. A BehaviorSubject is push-based and eager, so a chain of combineLatest can emit intermediate glitch values; signals are pull-based and glitch-free. A BehaviorSubject has no automatic dependency tracking — you must declare dependencies in a combineLatest and keep that list correct; signals discover dependencies by execution, and the dependency set can differ per run (a computed with an early return genuinely has fewer dependencies on that pass). A BehaviorSubject needs unsubscription; a signal has no subscription to leak. And signals have no notion of time — no debounceTime, no retry, no switchMap — which is precisely why RxJS is still in the framework for event streams. The one-line version: signals model state, observables model events over time.

Gotcha

Reactive context ends at the first await. Dependency tracking is synchronous only. In effect(async () => { const a = first(); await x(); const b = second(); }), the read of first() is tracked and the read of second() is not — silently. You get an effect that re-runs for some of its inputs and not others, which is one of the nastiest bugs in the model because it looks correct. Read every signal you depend on synchronously at the top, or use resource(), which is designed for exactly this shape. The same rule bites in a computed: a computed must be synchronous and pure, full stop.

Try it — see the graph work and then break it

1. Build the price/quantity/total example above with an effect that logs. Apply both writes in one function and count the logs. Then wrap each write in setTimeout and count again.

2. Create const rows = signal([{id:1}]) bound to a list in a template. Add a button that does rows().push({id:2}) and another that does rows.update(r => [...r, {id:2}]). Click each.

3. Write effect(async () => { const a = sigA(); await delay(10); console.log(sigB()); }), then change sigB and watch nothing happen.

Expected outcome

(1) Both writes in one function produce one log with the correct final value — effects are batched to the change detection pass, not run per write. Separated by setTimeout, you get two logs, including the intermediate 100, because they are now genuinely two states of the world rather than one. (2) The push button changes nothing on screen — the array reference did not change, so no consumer was invalidated; the immutable button updates immediately. If you then click the immutable button, the pushed row suddenly appears too, which is the confusing symptom teams report as "signals are flaky". (3) Changing sigB never re-runs the effect, because the read happened after an await and was outside the reactive context.

Interview Q&A

Explain signals to someone who knows RxJS. What problem do they solve that observables did not?

Observables are a general model of values over time, and they are excellent for that: events, streams, cancellation, retries, backpressure. What they are bad at is representing current state, because to know the current value you either subscribe or reach for a BehaviorSubject, and the moment you combine several of them you have to declare the dependency list yourself and you can observe glitches — intermediate emissions where the combination is momentarily inconsistent. Signals are the state half of that split: pull-based, so nothing computes until it is read; auto-tracking, so the dependency graph is discovered by execution rather than declared; glitch-free, because a read always sees a settled value; and subscription-free, so there is no unsubscribe and no leak. The framework benefit is the real prize: because Angular knows exactly which template read which signal, change detection can target the specific views that need updating instead of walking the component tree. That is what made zoneless possible. In practice I use both — signals for state and derivations, RxJS for anything with a time dimension — and toSignal/toObservable at the boundary.

What is computed laziness and memoisation, and how would you prove it in a code review?

A computed does not run when its dependencies change; it is marked dirty and runs on the next read. If nobody reads it, it never runs — so a computed feeding a component behind a false conditional costs nothing. It also memoises: repeated reads with unchanged dependencies return the cached value without re-executing. On top of that there is equality short-circuiting — if a recomputation produces a value equal by Object.is to the previous one, downstream consumers are not invalidated at all. The way to prove it is a console.log or a counter inside the computation, then read it three times in a row and change an unrelated signal in between; you see exactly one execution. The reason this matters in review is that it changes what "expensive" means. An expensive derivation in a computed is fine as long as it is pure and synchronous; the same expression inlined into a template binding is a genuine problem, because a template expression is re-evaluated on every check of that view with no memoisation. So "move it into a computed" is a real performance fix, not a style preference.

When is effect the right answer, and what are the failure modes when it is not?

effect is for the boundary between the reactive graph and the non-reactive world: writing to localStorage, updating document.title, feeding a third-party chart or map instance, firing analytics. Inside the graph it is almost always the wrong tool, and the framework docs say so. The three failure modes I look for: an effect that calls .set() on another signal, which is a derivation written imperatively — it runs a tick late, can create a cycle, and should be a computed or a linkedSignal; an async effect, where everything after the first await is silently untracked, so the effect responds to some inputs and not others; and an effect doing data fetching, which has no cancellation and will happily apply a stale response over a fresh one when two requests race — that is what resource() and httpResource() exist for. The one legitimate write-from-effect case is bridging to something you do not control, and even then I would wrap the reads I do not want to depend on in untracked so the dependency set is explicit.

What is linkedSignal and what did people do before it?

linkedSignal, stable in v20, is writable state that re-derives from a source. The canonical case is a selection: a table has rows and a selected row, the user can change the selection freely, but when the rows are replaced by a new search the selection must reset — except it should survive if the previously selected row is still present. That is neither pure derived state nor pure local state, which is why computed and signal both fail at it. The API takes a source and a computation that receives the new source value and the previous state, so you can decide whether to keep or reset. Before v19 the pattern was an effect that watched the source and called set() on a plain signal, which is exactly the write-inside-effect anti-pattern: it ran a change detection tick late so the UI flickered through a stale selection, it was easy to turn into a loop, and it was hard to unit-test because you had to flush effects. Other people did it in ngOnChanges, which only worked for values that arrived as inputs. Whenever I see an effect whose only job is to set another signal, linkedSignal is the fix.

A junior says "I set the signal but the view didn't update". What do you check?

In order: did they mutate instead of replace? rows().push(x) keeps the same array reference, so Object.is says nothing changed and no consumer is invalidated. That is the cause about eighty percent of the time, and the fix is update with a new reference, or a custom equal if immutability is genuinely impractical. Second: did they read the signal in the template without calling it? @if (visible) tests a function object, which is always truthy, and {{ count }} renders the function's source. Third: are they inside a zoneless app doing the update from a callback the framework never sees — which, importantly, is not a problem for signals, since a signal write schedules change detection itself; but if they are updating a plain field rather than a signal, in a zoneless app nothing will ever refresh it. Fourth: is the value being set equal to the previous one, so the short-circuit correctly does nothing? And fifth, the subtle one: is there a computed in between whose output did not change — for example mapping to list.length — in which case the graph is behaving exactly as designed. Angular DevTools' signal graph makes all of this visible in a way that guessing does not.

How do you decide between a signal in a service, a signal in a component, and a state library?

My default is: component state lives in the component, and anything two components need lives in a service exposing read-only signals plus explicit methods. The pattern is a small class with private writable signals, public asReadonly() accessors or computed projections, and methods that are the only way to mutate — which gives you the discipline of a store with none of the boilerplate, and it is testable with plain function calls. I stay there until one of three things happens: the state machine gets genuinely complex and I want explicit transitions rather than scattered set calls; I need time-travel debugging or an event log for a domain where "how did we get here" matters; or several teams need a shared contract and I want the extra ceremony as a coordination tool. At that point NgRx SignalStore is the natural step, because it is signals underneath and the migration is mechanical. The mistake I have seen most often is reaching for a global store on day one for a CRUD app, which buys a lot of indirection and no benefit — and the opposite mistake, letting a signal service grow to forty fields with no transitions, which is a store with the safety removed.

What is untracked for, and give a real case where omitting it caused a bug.

untracked reads a signal without creating a dependency edge. The typical need is an effect or computation that should react to some of its inputs but merely consult others. The real case I have hit: an analytics effect that fired a search event whenever the query changed, and also read the current user for the payload. Without untracked, the user signal was a tracked dependency, so a token refresh that replaced the user object re-fired the search event with the unchanged query — duplicate events, inflated metrics, and a very confusing dashboard. Wrapping the user read in untracked fixed it in one line. The second common case is breaking a would-be cycle: an effect that both reads and writes related state, where the read of the thing you are about to write must not create a dependency. That said, a cycle is usually a design smell, so if I need untracked to prevent an infinite loop I take it as a signal to reach for linkedSignal or restructure, not as a fix.

How do signals interact with immutability and large data structures?

Signals compare with Object.is, so the default contract is "replace, don't mutate", and that is the right default because it makes invalidation exact and cheap. The cost shows up with large structures: updating one row in a ten-thousand-row array means allocating a new array, and if a naive computed then re-derives the whole thing you have made an O(1) edit cost O(n) twice. Three levers. First, normalise: hold a map keyed by id and a separate array of ids, so a row edit replaces one map entry and the id array is untouched — most consumers that only care about ordering never invalidate. Second, split the signal: many small signals beat one big one, because invalidation is per-signal and the graph does the filtering for you. Third, use a custom equal where a cheap structural check can stop propagation, or reach for a persistent-data-structure library if the domain genuinely warrants it. What I would not do is turn off immutability and mutate in place with a manual "bump a version counter" signal — it works, but you have re-created dirty checking by hand and lost the property that made signals worth adopting.

What does "glitch-free" mean and can you demonstrate it?

A glitch is an observable intermediate state that is logically impossible. The demonstration is a price signal, a quantity signal and a total computed from both; you then halve the price and double the quantity in the same synchronous block. The total should be identical before and after, and with signals it is — an observer sees the value once, correctly, because total is not evaluated on write, only marked dirty, and by the time anything reads it both writes have landed. A push-based system that recomputes on each emission will publish the intermediate value. Angular gets this from three mechanisms together: pull-based evaluation, so nothing runs until read; topological ordering, so a consumer never reads a producer that is still stale; and version-based short-circuiting, so a recomputation that yields an equal value stops there. The practical payoff is not academic — it is why you never need a "debounce until things settle" hack around derived UI state, and why effects fire once per settled change rather than once per write.

How would you introduce signals to a team with a large RxJS codebase, without a rewrite?

I would draw the line at the boundary rather than migrate feature by feature. The rule I would write down: state is signals, streams are RxJS, and the conversion happens once, at the edge of the service. Concretely, services keep whatever RxJS they already have internally, and expose toSignal(stream$, { initialValue }) so components consume signals; components that need to feed a stream use toObservable(sig). Templates lose the async pipe and the | async as gymnastics immediately, which is the visible win that gets people on board. Then new code gets written signal-first, and the existing combineLatest chains get converted only when they are being changed anyway. I would run the official ng generate @angular/core:signals migration for inputs, outputs and queries early, because that is mechanical and touches everything. The two things I would explicitly warn the team about are the ones that generate the "signals are broken" bug reports: mutation instead of replacement, and effects used as derivations. And I would keep RxJS firmly in place for what it is good at — debouncing a search box, cancelling in-flight requests, websockets, retry with backoff — because "we removed RxJS" is not a goal worth having.

04Change detection: zones to zoneless

Change detection is Angular's answer to one question: after something happened, which parts of the DOM might be stale? The framework has now answered it three different ways, and an interview at lead level will expect you to explain all three and the reasoning that moved between them.

Answer 1 — zones (2016 to about 2024). zone.js monkey-patches every asynchronous browser API before your app boots: setTimeout, addEventListener, XMLHttpRequest, Promise, requestAnimationFrame, and dozens more. When any patched callback finishes, the zone notifies Angular, and Angular runs change detection over the entire component tree, top to bottom, re-evaluating every template binding and comparing the result with the last value. This is genuinely brilliant as ergonomics — you never have to tell Angular anything — and genuinely expensive as engineering: a mousemove handler that does nothing triggers a full-tree check, and the framework has no idea which component actually changed.

Answer 2 — OnPush (2016, but optional until 2026). An OnPush component is skipped during a check unless something marked it dirty: one of its inputs changed by reference, an event fired from its own template, an async pipe in its template emitted, or someone called markForCheck(). Marking is what matters — it walks up the tree setting a dirty flag on ancestors, so the next check descends only along dirty paths. OnPush turned "check everything" into "check the paths that plausibly changed", at the cost of a discipline most teams applied inconsistently.

Answer 3 — signals and zoneless (stable v20.2, default v21, completed in v22). When a template reads a signal, the framework records that the view depends on it. A signal write marks exactly those views dirty and schedules a check. No zone, no patched globals, no full-tree walk. In v22 the last piece landed: OnPush became the default strategy for new components, and the old Default was renamed Eager and deprecated. The framework's default posture is now "nothing is checked unless something said so".

Interview trap

Claiming OnPush means "only checks when inputs change". That is the half-answer that gets you marked down, because it misses the three other triggers and the ancestor walk. The full answer: an OnPush view is checked when it is dirty, and it becomes dirty from an input changing by reference, an event bound in its own template firing, an async pipe in its template emitting, a signal it read changing, or an explicit markForCheck(). And the crucial mechanical detail: marking propagates upwards to the root, so the next pass can descend to it — a dirty component deep in the tree does not get found by magic. The corollary that catches people: mutating an input object in the parent without changing the reference will never update an OnPush child, which is why OnPush and immutability are a package deal.

Gotcha

NG0100 ExpressionChangedAfterItHasBeenCheckedError only appears in development. Candidates often say "we suppressed it" or "it only happens in dev so we ignored it". Both are wrong answers. The error means a binding's value changed during rendering — typically a parent reading a value the child sets in ngAfterViewInit, or an effect writing a signal that a template above it reads. In production the second pass does not run, so the error disappears but the bug does not: the UI renders the stale value and only corrects itself on the next unrelated tick, which users see as a flicker or a value that is one interaction behind. The fixes, in order of preference: restructure so the value is derived (computed) rather than assigned; move the write into the parent; or, as a last resort, defer the write to a microtask. Reaching for detectChanges() to silence it usually makes it worse.

Try it — feel the difference between the three answers

1. In a zone-based app, add constructor() { console.count('CD'); }… actually put the counter in a getter used by a template binding, then move the mouse over the page with a (mousemove) listener attached anywhere. Watch the counter climb. Now wrap the listener in runOutsideAngular.

2. Take the same app, add provideZonelessChangeDetection(), and find what stops working. Fix each one by converting the state to a signal.

3. Cause NG0100 on purpose: in a child, set a parent-bound value inside ngAfterViewInit.

Expected outcome

(1) Every mousemove runs a full-tree check — the template binding is re-evaluated hundreds of times a second even though nothing changed. Outside the zone, the counter stops entirely, and the handler's own state changes stop showing up too, which is the trade you are making. (2) The things that break are exactly the ones that wrote plain fields from async callbacks; the ones that already used signals or the async pipe keep working untouched. That contrast is the whole argument for signals in one experiment. (3) You get the error in dev and a silent one-tick-stale value in a production build — run ng build --configuration production and serve it to see the flicker.

Interview Q&A

Explain how Angular change detection works, from zones to zoneless.

Historically Angular used zone.js, which monkey-patches the async browser APIs — timers, event listeners, XHR, promises — so that when any of those callbacks completes, the framework is told "something might have happened" and runs a synchronous check over the whole component tree, re-evaluating every binding and writing the DOM where a value changed. It is ergonomically wonderful and computationally blunt: the framework knows something happened but not what, so it checks everything. OnPush was the first refinement, letting a component opt out unless it was marked dirty by an input reference change, its own template's event, an async pipe emission, or an explicit markForCheck — with the important mechanic that marking walks up to the root so the next pass can find it. Signals are the completion of that idea: because the framework records which views read which signals, a write can mark precisely those views, and the tree walk becomes unnecessary. That is what made zoneless viable — stable in 20.2, the default for new apps in v21 — and in v22 OnPush became the default strategy with the old Default renamed Eager and deprecated. The end state is a framework that updates only what depends on what changed.

What breaks when you turn on zoneless, and how do you find it?

Anything that mutated non-signal state from a callback the framework can no longer observe. Concretely: a setTimeout or setInterval that assigned to a component field; a manual subscribe whose handler wrote a property instead of feeding an async pipe; and third-party callbacks — a charting library, a map SDK, a websocket client, a payment iframe — that call back into your code outside any Angular machinery. In a zone app all of those triggered a tick as a side effect, and the UI updated by luck. Zoneless removes the luck. Finding them is the work: I would enable zoneless in a branch and drive the app through its main journeys, because the symptom is always "the data is right in the debugger but the screen is stale", which is easy to recognise once you know to look for it. Angular ships help here — there is a zoneless migration tool exposed through the CLI's MCP server, and I would also grep for runOutsideAngular, detectChanges, NgZone and bare subscribe( as a hit list. Each fix is one of three: make the state a signal, put an async pipe on the stream, or call markForCheck(). The first is the one that leaves the codebase better.

What is ExpressionChangedAfterItHasBeenCheckedError, really?

In development Angular runs change detection twice per pass and compares. If a binding produces a different value on the second run, the render was not a pure function of the state at the start of the pass — something wrote state during rendering — and Angular throws NG0100. The classic shapes are a child writing to a value the parent binds, from ngAfterViewInit or ngAfterContentChecked; a getter in a template that creates a new object or array every call, so the reference differs every time; and an effect or subscription that writes a signal read higher in the tree. It matters because production does not run the second pass: the error goes away but the stale render does not, and users see a value that is one tick behind, often as a flicker on first paint. My fix order is: make the value derived rather than assigned, so it cannot change during a pass — a computed reading the same inputs; if it must be assigned, move the assignment up to the owner of the state; if it genuinely must happen after render, use afterNextRender so it lands outside the pass. Calling detectChanges() to make the message stop is the one move I would push back on in review, because it hides the bug and doubles the work.

Why did OnPush become the default in v22, and what does the migration do?

Because with signals, OnPush is no longer a discipline you have to maintain — it is just correct. The historical objection to OnPush was that it required immutability everywhere and a good understanding of the marking rules, and a single team member getting it wrong produced a component that mysteriously did not update. Signals remove that: a view that reads a changed signal is refreshed regardless, so the main failure mode of OnPush disappears. Making it the default means new code gets the fast path without an annotation, and the framework's default posture becomes "check nothing unless told". The migration is deliberately conservative: ng update writes changeDetection: ChangeDetectionStrategy.Eager — the renamed and now deprecated old default — onto every existing component that did not declare a strategy, so behaviour is preserved exactly. Those annotations then serve as a to-do list: each one is a component that has not been verified under OnPush. I would treat clearing them as ongoing work tied to whatever feature touches the component, not as a migration sprint, and I would add a lint rule so no new component acquires one.

When would you still use NgZone.runOutsideAngular?

Only in an app that still has zones, and only for high-frequency work that must not trigger a tick: a requestAnimationFrame render loop, drag-and-drop or resize handlers firing at pointer rate, scroll listeners, canvas or WebGL work, or a third-party library that runs its own loop. The pattern is to do the work outside, then re-enter with ngZone.run() only at the moments where application state actually changes — for example on drag end rather than on every drag move. If you profile a zone app and see change detection running hundreds of times a second while the user drags something, this is the fix and it is often a ten-times improvement. In a zoneless app the question mostly evaporates: nothing triggers change detection except signal writes and explicit marking, so a mousemove handler that updates a local variable costs nothing by construction. That is worth saying explicitly in an interview, because it reframes zoneless from "a performance micro-optimisation" to "a change that deletes a whole category of performance workarounds".

How do you profile a change detection problem?

I start with Angular DevTools' profiler, because it attributes time to components rather than to stack frames: you record an interaction and get a flame chart of which components were checked and how long each took, plus why they were checked. That usually answers the question in one pass — either one component is expensive, or hundreds of cheap components are being checked when they should not be. If it is the latter, the cause is nearly always a zone app without OnPush, or a high-frequency event source inside the zone. If it is the former, I look at the component's bindings for function calls and getters, because those re-run on every check with no memoisation, and for pipes that are marked impure. Chrome's performance panel is the second tool, for anything that is not Angular's fault — layout thrashing from reading offsetHeight in a loop, or a third-party library's work. In v22 there is also the DevTools signal-graph integration, which shows the producer/consumer relationships directly, so "why did this view refresh" becomes a question you can answer by looking rather than by reasoning. The metric I care about is the number of views checked per interaction; getting that from "the whole tree" to "the three that changed" is the goal, and signals plus OnPush get you there without hand-tuning.

A template binding calls a method. Why is that a problem, and when is it not?

It is a problem because a template expression is re-evaluated on every check of that view, with no memoisation whatsoever. In a zone-based app without OnPush, that means every timer, every keystroke, every mousemove anywhere in the app re-runs your function — I have seen a formatting helper called sixty thousand times during a scroll. The second problem is that if the function returns a new object or array each call, every downstream binding sees a changed reference and re-renders, and in dev you get NG0100. The fixes are a computed for anything derived from signals, a pure pipe for a transformation that depends only on its arguments, or a plain field assigned when the inputs change. It is not a problem when the function is genuinely trivial and the view is rarely checked — a signal-driven OnPush component that refreshes only when its own state changes can call a cheap function without measurable cost, and I would not flag that in review. What I do flag unconditionally is a function call in a binding inside a loop, because there the cost is multiplied by the row count.

What is the difference between markForCheck and detectChanges?

markForCheck is a declaration of intent: it flags this view and every ancestor as dirty so that the next scheduled change detection pass will visit them. It is asynchronous with respect to rendering, it batches naturally with everything else happening in the same tick, and it is almost always the right call. detectChanges is imperative: it runs change detection on this view and its subtree immediately and synchronously. That has three consequences people underestimate — it does not check ancestors, so a value the parent owns can stay stale; it runs outside the normal batching, so calling it in a loop is quadratic; and because it renders in the middle of whatever else is happening, it is a reliable way to produce NG0100. The legitimate uses are narrow: forcing a render before measuring the DOM synchronously, and driving a detached view. In tests fixture.detectChanges() is normal and expected, which is where most people first meet it and where the habit comes from. In application code, if I see detectChanges I ask what state is not reactive, because that is usually the real bug.

Does zoneless mean zone.js is gone from Angular?

Not gone — no longer required, and no longer installed by default. Since v21, ng new produces an app with no zone.js dependency and zoneless change detection configured, and v22 completes the picture by defaulting components to OnPush. But every existing app still ships zone.js until someone migrates, provideZoneChangeDetection() is still supported, and there are still legitimate reasons to keep it for a while — chiefly third-party libraries that assume it, and test suites written with fakeAsync/tick, which depend on zone patching. Angular 22 acknowledges that last one directly by shipping a zone.js Vitest patch so fakeAsync, flush and waitForAsync keep working under the new test runner. So the accurate statement for an interview is: zoneless is the default for new applications and the direction of the framework, zone.js is a supported opt-in for existing ones, and the migration is a real but bounded piece of work whose size is proportional to how much non-signal state your app mutates from async callbacks.

How would you convince a team to migrate a large app to zoneless?

I would not lead with performance, because for many apps the framework overhead is not their bottleneck and the argument collapses under a profile. I would lead with three things that are true regardless of app size. First, debugging: stack traces in a zone app are polluted by zone frames and an error's origin is often unrecoverable; zoneless traces are ordinary JavaScript traces, and that alone saves real time. Second, bundle and startup: zone.js is roughly 30KB gzipped of patching that runs before your app does, on every page load, on mobile. Third, direction: it is the default for new apps, the ecosystem is converging on it, and the cost only grows as the app does. Then I would de-risk it: turn it on in a branch, drive the top ten user journeys, and produce a concrete list of broken spots — in my experience it is a surprisingly short list, concentrated in a few third-party integrations. I would sequence it after the signals migration rather than before, because converting state to signals fixes most of the breakage as a side effect, and I would ship it behind a flag by environment so it can be reverted in a deploy rather than a revert.

05Dependency injection

Dependency injection is the part of Angular that has changed least and matters most. It is also the clearest differentiator from React: Angular has a real, hierarchical, runtime DI container, and using it well is an architectural skill rather than an API skill. If you can explain why an injector tree exists at all, you are already ahead of most candidates.

The mental model: Angular maintains a tree of injectors that mirrors, but is not identical to, your component tree. When something asks for a dependency, Angular walks up that tree from the requesting node until it finds a provider for the token, and the instance it finds is shared by everything below that point. Two trees, actually — the element injector tree (from component and directive providers) and the environment injector tree (from application config, route-level providers, and lazy-loaded scopes). The element tree is searched first; when it is exhausted, the search continues in the environment tree.

The modern shape

Interview trap

"providedIn: 'root' means a singleton." Almost — and the exceptions are where the interesting questions live. It means one instance per environment injector chain that resolves it. Under SSR, every request gets its own application instance and therefore its own "singletons", which is exactly why putting request-specific state in a root service is a cross-request data-leak bug rather than a style issue. In a micro-frontend page with two bootstrapped Angular apps, you get two. In a test, each TestBed gets its own. And a lazy-loaded route with its own providers entry for the same token gets a second instance, shadowing the root one for that subtree — which is a legitimate technique and also a classic accidental bug when someone copies a providers array. The precise sentence to use is "one instance per injector that provides it, and root means the application-level environment injector".

Gotcha

inject() only works in an injection context. That means a constructor, a field initialiser, a factory function, or inside runInInjectionContext(). Call it from a method, a promise callback, or a setTimeout and you get NG0203: inject() must be called from an injection context. This bites hardest when converting constructor injection to inject() in a base class or a helper function, and when writing a utility that wants to inject lazily. The two fixes: capture the dependency at field-initialiser time and use it later, or capture const injector = inject(Injector) and later call runInInjectionContext(injector, () => ...). In v22 there is a third answer for the "I do not want to pay for it upfront" case — injectAsync(), which defers both the import and the instantiation without leaving the injection context.

Try it — make the injector tree visible

2. Put a service in a lazy route's providers, log a random id in its constructor, and navigate away and back.

3. Call inject(HttpClient) inside a setTimeout in a component method and read the error. Fix it with runInInjectionContext.

Expected outcome

(1) With root, the two siblings share a counter; with component-level providers they each get their own, and this one experiment is the whole concept. (2) The constructor logs a new id every time you enter the route: route-scoped providers are destroyed on leaving, which is exactly the lifetime you want for feature state and exactly why root-scoped feature state shows stale values. (3) You get NG0203. Capturing const injector = inject(Injector) at field level and wrapping the deferred call in runInInjectionContext(injector, ...) fixes it — and if you now use Angular DevTools' injector tree view you can see the resolution path for any node.

Interview Q&A

Explain Angular's hierarchical injector. Why two trees?

There are two parallel hierarchies. The element injector tree is created by providers on components and directives and mirrors the DOM structure of your views; the environment injector tree is created by the application bootstrap, route-level providers and lazily loaded scopes, and mirrors the routing and module structure. A resolution starts at the requesting element injector, walks up through its ancestors, and when that runs out it continues into the environment injector chain, ending at the root and then at the platform injector; if nothing provides the token you get NG0201. The two trees exist because they answer different questions: element injectors let a component give its subtree a private instance — a form group, a grid's selection model, a theme — which is inherently a view-shaped lifetime; environment injectors handle application- and feature-shaped lifetimes, which do not map onto the DOM at all, especially for lazy-loaded features. The practical payoff of understanding the split is that it tells you where to put a provider: if the lifetime you want is "this widget", it belongs on the component; if it is "this feature area", it belongs on the route.

Constructor injection or inject()? Defend your answer.

inject(), and v22 makes it more than a preference — the new @Service decorator requires it. The substantive arguments are that inject() works outside constructors, which is what makes functional guards, functional interceptors, resolvers and reusable inject-helpers possible at all; that it composes with inheritance without the base-class constructor-parameter chain, which is a real pain in component hierarchies; and that it infers types without decorator metadata, so it does not depend on emitDecoratorMetadata. It also enables a pattern I use a lot: a small exported function that injects several services and returns a composed API, which is the Angular analogue of a custom hook and is far cleaner than a base class. The counter-arguments are worth acknowledging honestly: constructor injection is more familiar to people coming from other DI frameworks, it makes dependencies visible in one signature, and it plays better with hand-rolled instantiation in tests — though TestBed makes that mostly moot. The one real constraint is that inject() only works in an injection context, so a class that is genuinely constructed with new outside Angular cannot use it.

What is @Service in v22 and why add it?

@Service() is a v22 decorator that is shorthand for @Injectable({ providedIn: 'root' }), with autoProvided: false as the escape hatch when you want to provide it somewhere specific instead. The motivation is that root-provided was already what nearly everyone wanted nearly all of the time, and @Injectable with an options object was ceremony around the common case — plus @Injectable without options is a genuine footgun, because it makes the class injectable but provides it nowhere, producing a runtime NG0201 that beginners find baffling. @Service removes that failure mode by making the common case the default. The one behavioural constraint is that it requires inject() rather than constructor parameters, which is deliberate — it pushes the codebase toward the API that works everywhere. In terms of adoption I would use it for new services, and I would not bulk-rewrite existing ones, because @Injectable is not deprecated and the churn buys nothing.

What is injectAsync and when is it worth the complexity?

injectAsync(), new in v22, takes a lambda returning a dynamic import and defers both the module load and the service instantiation until the dependency is first used; you await the result at the call site. It also takes a prefetch option — onIdle being the obvious one — so the chunk can be fetched during idle time and be warm by the time the user needs it. The value is code splitting at the service level rather than only at the route level. The concrete cases I would use it for are heavy, rarely-used libraries hanging off a service: a PDF generator, a spreadsheet exporter, a charting engine, a rich-text editor, an OCR or crypto library. Those are often several hundred kilobytes sitting in the main bundle for a feature five percent of users touch. It is worth the complexity exactly when the chunk is big and the usage is conditional; it is not worth it for a small service, because you have made every call site async for no gain, and turning a synchronous API into a promise-returning one is contagious. I would also be careful about using it on anything in a critical path where the extra round trip shows up as latency — that is what prefetch is for, and it is worth measuring rather than assuming.

How do you swap an implementation for a test, or per environment?

The token is the seam. If code depends on a concrete class, the class is the token and you override it with { provide: RealApi, useClass: FakeApi }, which works but couples the consumer to the real class's shape. Cleaner is to depend on an abstraction — an abstract class or an InjectionToken typed to an interface — and provide the implementation at the composition root, so the consumer never sees the concrete type at all. For environment differences I use a factory provider that reads configuration and returns the right implementation, or I provide different values in different bootstrap configurations; I avoid if (isDevMode()) scattered through services, because that puts the decision in a hundred places instead of one. In tests, TestBed.configureTestingModule({ providers: [...] }) overrides at the environment level and TestBed.overrideComponent at the element level, which matters when the thing you want to fake is provided by the component itself. One lead-level nuance: an abstract class used as a token is often better than an InjectionToken<Interface>, because it gives you a type and a runtime value in one declaration, and it can carry default method implementations.

What does multi: true do and where does Angular use it?

A multi-provider contributes a value to an array associated with a token, rather than replacing whatever else was registered. Injecting the token gives you every contribution, in registration order. Angular uses it for every extension point where "many things participate" is the right model: HTTP interceptors, application initializers, router features, form validators, and error handlers. The reason it matters architecturally is that it lets independent parts of an application, including libraries, register behaviour without knowing about each other or coordinating a central list — a library can provide an interceptor from its own provideX() function and it composes with yours. The trap is ordering: interceptors run in registration order for the request and reverse order for the response, so an auth interceptor registered after a logging interceptor logs the request before the token is attached. Modern Angular has been moving away from raw multi-tokens toward explicit feature functions like withInterceptors([...]), which make the order visible as an array in one place instead of implicit in provider registration order — that is a strictly better design and I would prefer it in new code.

What is NG0201 and what are the non-obvious causes?

NG0201: No provider for X means the resolution walked the element injector chain, then the environment chain, and found nothing. The obvious cause is a forgotten provider. The non-obvious ones are more interesting. A service decorated with bare @Injectable() and no providedIn is injectable but provided nowhere — the fix is @Service() or providedIn: 'root'. A dependency requested with self: true or host: true deliberately stops the walk early, so a provider that exists higher up is invisible by design. A service provided only in a lazy route's injector but injected from a component outside that subtree will fail even though the class is clearly "registered somewhere". Two copies of the same library in node_modules produce two distinct class objects, so the token you provide is genuinely not the token you inject — this is the one that costs a whole afternoon, and the tell is that the error names a class you can see is provided. And in a monorepo, importing a token through two different path aliases has exactly the same effect. Angular DevTools' injector tree view answers most of these in seconds by showing the actual resolution path.

Where should state live: a root service, a route provider, or a component provider?

Match the provider scope to the lifetime the state should have, and the bugs mostly disappear. Root is right for things that genuinely live as long as the session: the authenticated user, feature flags, a websocket connection, a cache you want to survive navigation. Route scope is right for feature state that should reset when the user leaves the feature — a multi-step wizard, a filter set, a draft — because the injector is destroyed on leaving and re-created on entry, which gives you correct reset semantics for free instead of an ngOnDestroy that clears fields by hand. Component scope is right for anything one instance owns: a data grid's selection and sort state, a dialog's controller, a form's coordination object; two grids on one page must not share, and component providers guarantee that structurally. The failure mode I see most often is per-page state in a root service — the user navigates away, comes back, and sees the previous page's data for a frame, or worse, a stale filter silently applied. The second is the opposite: putting the auth token in a component provider and then wondering why a sibling cannot see it. In v22 route-level providers also got automatic injector cleanup behind an experimental router feature, which tightens the route-scope story further.

How does DI behave under SSR, and what can go wrong?

On the server, each incoming request bootstraps its own application instance with its own environment injector, so "root singletons" are per-request, not per-process. Understanding that is the difference between a safe SSR app and a data leak. The classic bug is a service that caches something request-specific — the current user, a tenant id, an auth token — in a way that outlives the request: a module-level variable, a static field, or anything registered on the platform injector rather than the application injector. Those are shared across requests, and the symptom is one user seeing another user's data under load, which is about the worst bug an application can have and is almost impossible to reproduce locally with a single request. The related trap is browser globals: window, document and localStorage do not exist on the server, so anything touching them must be inside afterNextRender or guarded, and injecting DOCUMENT rather than reaching for the global is the pattern that keeps code portable. Angular 22 also tightened this area from the security side — HTTP transfer caching now excludes credential-bearing requests by default, precisely so a cached authenticated response does not get serialised into the HTML sent to a different user.

Is Angular's DI worth the complexity compared to just importing modules?

Mostly yes, and the honest answer names the cases where it is not. What DI buys you is a substitution seam at every boundary without any code changing on either side: tests replace a provider instead of mocking a module, an enterprise app swaps an implementation per tenant or per environment from one place, and libraries expose configurable behaviour without a global registry. It also gives you lifetimes as a first-class concept — the difference between per-app, per-route and per-component instances is declarative rather than something you hand-roll with factories and maps. And it makes composition explicit, which is genuinely valuable when twenty teams share a codebase. What it costs is indirection: the thing you get is not always the thing you can see, resolution errors are runtime rather than compile-time, and a newcomer has to learn a tree-walking algorithm to debug a missing provider. For a small app with one implementation of everything, a plain module import is simpler and I would not argue otherwise. My rule is that DI earns its keep the moment a dependency has more than one implementation, has a lifetime that is not "forever", or crosses a team boundary — and in a long-lived enterprise application that is nearly everything.

06RxJS and signals together

For most of Angular's life, RxJS was not optional: HTTP returned observables, the router exposed observables, forms exposed observables, and EventEmitter was an observable. That produced a generation of Angular developers who were fluent in switchMap and a generation who quietly copy-pasted it. Signals changed the balance, and the interview question is no longer "do you know RxJS" but "where do you draw the line, and why".

The line that holds up under questioning: signals model state — a value that exists right now and can be read; observables model events — things that happen over time, possibly zero times, possibly forever, possibly needing cancellation. A user's selected filter is state. Keystrokes in a search box are events. The result of the search is state again. So a well-built feature usually crosses the boundary twice, and the interesting engineering is at the crossings.

The two conversion functions

import { toSignal, toObservable, takeUntilDestroyed } from '@angular/core/rxjs-interop';

export class SearchPage {
  // State
  readonly query = signal('');
  readonly page  = signal(1);

  // State → stream, so we can use the time operators RxJS is good at
  private readonly results$ = toObservable(this.query).pipe(
    debounceTime(300),
    distinctUntilChanged(),
    switchMap(q => this.api.search(q)),   // cancels the in-flight request
  );

  // Stream → state, so the template needs no async pipe
  readonly results = toSignal(this.results$, { initialValue: [] as Row[] });
}

toSignal subscribes immediately and unsubscribes automatically when its injection context is destroyed — that is the leak-elimination story in one sentence. Three details are worth knowing precisely because they are asked:

  • Without initialValue, the signal's type is T | undefined, because the observable may not have emitted yet. Options are initialValue, or requireSync: true when you know the source is synchronous (a BehaviorSubject), which narrows the type to T and throws at runtime if the source did not emit synchronously.
  • Errors are re-thrown on read. If the source errors, reading the signal throws — so an unhandled HTTP error becomes a template exception. Handle errors in the pipe with catchError, not at the signal.
  • It is not lazy. toSignal subscribes at creation, unlike the async pipe which subscribes when the view is created. For an expensive stream behind a conditional, that difference is real.

Interview trap

"HTTP observables complete, so you don't need to unsubscribe." True and dangerously incomplete. A single-shot HttpClient call completes after one emission, so the subscription is cleaned up — but if the component is destroyed while the request is in flight and you do not unsubscribe, the handler still runs on a dead component. If that handler navigates, shows a toast, or writes to a service, you get behaviour the user did not ask for: a "saved" toast appearing on a different page. The stronger version of the answer is that unsubscribing is about cancelling work, not only about leaks — an aborted HTTP request frees a connection and can save the server real load on a slow page the user keeps leaving. And the leak risk is genuinely there for anything that does not complete: router events, form valueChanges, websockets, interval, and any Subject you own.

Gotcha

toSignal is eager and re-throws errors on read. Two consequences people hit in the same week. First, converting an expensive stream to a signal inside a component that is behind a false conditional still subscribes and still runs the request, because toSignal subscribes when it is created, not when the value is read — the async pipe was lazy, so a naive migration can turn one request into several. Second, if the source errors, the error surfaces from the signal read, which happens during template rendering, so a failed HTTP call becomes an exception in change detection rather than a handled error state. Always terminate the pipe with catchError(() => of(fallback)), or use httpResource, which models the error as a value.

Interview Q&A

Is RxJS still relevant in Angular now that signals exist?

Yes, but its territory shrank to what it is actually good at. RxJS is no longer the way you hold and propagate state — signals do that better, with automatic dependency tracking, no subscriptions to leak, glitch-free reads, and precise change detection. What RxJS still owns is anything with a time dimension or a cancellation requirement: debouncing user input, retrying with backoff, coordinating several async operations, websockets and server-sent events, and cancelling in-flight work with switchMap. Signals have no time operators at all, which is by design. So my architecture rule is that streams live inside services and are converted at the boundary: services expose signals via toSignal, and take signals into streams via toObservable where operators are needed. Components then contain almost no RxJS, which is a large readability win because component-level RxJS is where the subscription bugs live. The one thing I would push back on in an interview is the framing "signals replace RxJS" — the Angular team has been explicit that they are complementary, and the Resource API is the proof: it uses observables underneath and presents signals outward.

Explain switchMap vs mergeMap vs concatMap vs exhaustMap with a real decision each.

switchMap cancels the previous inner observable when a new outer value arrives, so only the latest matters — that is a typeahead, or reloading a detail view when the route parameter changes. Getting this wrong with mergeMap produces the classic race where a slow early request overwrites a fast later one, which is intermittent and network-dependent and therefore never reproduces in review. concatMap queues and preserves order, which is what you want for a sequence of writes that must not overtake each other — for example an offline queue flushing edits, where applying them out of order corrupts the record. mergeMap runs everything concurrently with no ordering, which is right for genuinely independent work like uploading several files, and it takes a concurrency limit so you do not open two hundred sockets. exhaustMap ignores new outer values while one inner is running, which is the correct double-click guard on a submit button — better than disabling the button in a handler, because it is declarative and cannot be defeated by the Enter key. The one-line summary I would give: latest wins, all in order, all at once, first wins.

What are the failure modes of toSignal?

Three, and they all come from the same root: toSignal subscribes eagerly and presents a stream as a value. First, the initial value. Without initialValue the type is T | undefined, which either forces null handling everywhere or tempts people into requireSync: true — fine for a BehaviorSubject, a runtime throw for anything asynchronous. Second, errors. If the source errors, the error is re-thrown when the signal is read, and reads happen during template rendering, so a failed HTTP call becomes an exception inside change detection rather than an error state you can render; the fix is catchError in the pipe. Third, eagerness. The async pipe subscribed when the view was created, so a stream behind a conditional cost nothing until it was shown; toSignal subscribes when the field initialiser runs, so a naive migration can turn a lazily-issued request into one that always fires, sometimes several times. None of these are reasons to avoid it — it eliminates a whole class of subscription bugs — but they are exactly the details that separate someone who has used it from someone who read the release notes.

How do you prevent memory leaks from subscriptions, and how do you find existing ones?

The best prevention is not subscribing: bind streams through the async pipe or convert them with toSignal, both of which tear down automatically. When a genuine side effect requires a subscription, takeUntilDestroyed() is the modern answer — it reads the DestroyRef from the injection context, so it is one operator with no boilerplate, and it takes an explicit DestroyRef when you are outside that context. The legacy patterns you will meet are a destroy$ Subject with takeUntil, which works but is four lines of ritual per component and breaks silently if takeUntil is not last in the pipe, and a manual Subscription collection unsubscribed in ngOnDestroy. To find existing leaks I use Chrome heap snapshots: exercise the suspect route repeatedly, snapshot, and look for a growing count of component instances that should have been collected, then follow the retainer path — it almost always terminates in a Subject in a long-lived service. The high-risk sources are the ones that never complete: router events, form valueChanges and statusChanges, interval, websockets, and any Subject exposed by a root-scoped service. HTTP calls are the low-risk case, though even there an in-flight request on a destroyed component can run a handler that navigates or toasts.

What is a hot vs cold observable and why does it matter in Angular?

A cold observable creates its producer per subscriber, so each subscription gets its own independent execution; HttpClient calls are cold, which is why subscribing twice sends two requests. A hot observable has a producer that exists independently and multicasts to whoever is listening; Subjects, router events and DOM event streams are hot, and a late subscriber misses what already happened unless the stream replays. In Angular this matters in two very practical places. First, the async pipe used twice in a template on a cold HTTP observable issues two requests — the classic fix is shareReplay({ bufferSize: 1, refCount: true }), or better, restructure so there is one subscription feeding a signal. Second, shareReplay without refCount keeps the source alive forever and caches the last value, which is sometimes exactly what you want for a config load and is otherwise a memory leak with a friendly face. Since signals arrived, my preferred answer to "this is being subscribed twice" is not shareReplay at all — it is to convert once at the service boundary with toSignal or httpResource, because a signal is inherently multicast and has no subscription semantics to get wrong.

How would you implement a typeahead with cancellation, debouncing and error handling?

Today I would reach for httpResource first, because it gives cancellation, loading state and error-as-a-value for free, and in v22 there is a debounced() helper for the input side. But I should be able to write the RxJS version, because it is the classic whiteboard question and because you still need it when the trigger is not a simple parameter change. The pipeline is: the query signal converted with toObservable, then debounceTime(300) so we do not fire per keystroke, then distinctUntilChanged() so backspacing to a previous value does nothing, then filter(q => q.length >= 2) to avoid a full-table scan on one character, then switchMap to the request so an outstanding call is cancelled, then catchError(() => of([])) inside the switchMap so an error kills one request rather than terminating the whole outer stream — that placement is the detail interviewers probe. Then toSignal for the template. For loading state I would either track it with tap on either side of the request, or accept that this is exactly why the Resource API exists and use it instead.

Your team has 200 components using the async pipe. Is converting to signals worth it?

Not as a project on its own. The async pipe is not broken: it unsubscribes correctly, it marks OnPush views for check, and it is lazy in a way toSignal is not. A bulk conversion is a large diff with a real regression risk — particularly the eagerness difference — and no user-visible benefit. What I would do instead is convert at the boundary, once, per service: expose signals from services and let new and touched components consume them, so the async pipes disappear as a side effect of normal work. I would prioritise the cases where signals genuinely fix something: templates with several async pipes on the same stream, *ngIf="x$ | async as x" pyramids nested three deep, and anything where a combineLatest is really a derived value and should be a computed. And I would prioritise it ahead of a zoneless migration, because converting state to signals removes most of what would otherwise break under zoneless. The argument I would make to leadership is about defect classes, not elegance: subscription leaks and race conditions stop being possible in the converted code, and those are the bugs that reach production.

What does takeUntilDestroyed do that takeUntil(this.destroy$) did not?

Functionally the same completion behaviour, with three practical improvements. It removes the boilerplate — no Subject field, no ngOnDestroy, no risk of forgetting to call complete(). It works outside components: in a service, in a functional guard, in an interceptor, anywhere with an injection context, and with an explicit DestroyRef anywhere else — the destroy$ pattern was tied to a lifecycle hook and therefore to classes with lifecycles. And it removes a subtle failure mode: takeUntil must be the last operator in the pipe, because operators after it can resubscribe to the source and outlive the completion signal, which is an easy mistake that produces a leak that looks like it is handled. There is one thing to be careful about: takeUntilDestroyed without an explicit DestroyRef must be called in an injection context, so putting it in a method rather than a field initialiser throws NG0203. The pattern that scales is to capture const destroyRef = inject(DestroyRef) at field level and pass it explicitly wherever the pipe is built later.

When would you still expose an Observable from a service rather than a signal?

When the thing genuinely is a stream of events rather than a value. A websocket feed of trade ticks, notifications pushed from the server, a message bus between features, a progress stream from a long upload, or an audit log of user actions — for all of these, every emission matters and collapsing them into "the latest value" loses information. Signals cannot express "this happened three times", only "the current state is X", and a consumer that misses an intermediate value of a signal has not necessarily missed anything, whereas a consumer that misses a notification has. The second case is when the consumer needs operators: if callers routinely want to debounce, buffer, retry or combine your output with timing, handing them an observable is kinder than handing them a signal they have to convert back. What I would not do is expose both for the same concept, because then there are two sources of truth and someone will subscribe to one and read the other. If a service naturally holds both — a stream of events and a derived current state — I expose the stream as an observable and the state as a signal, and I make the naming say which is which.

What RxJS anti-patterns do you look for in code review?

Nested subscriptions are the first — a subscribe inside a subscribe means someone reached for callbacks instead of a flattening operator, and it defeats cancellation entirely. Manual state juggling around a subscription is the second: loading = true before, loading = false in both the next and error handlers, which is three places to get wrong and is what finalize or the Resource API exists for. Third, mergeMap in a typeahead, which is the race described earlier. Fourth, shareReplay() with no configuration, which by default never unsubscribes from the source and caches forever — I want to see refCount: true and a deliberate bufferSize, or a reason. Fifth, a Subject exposed publicly from a service, letting any consumer emit into it; the service should expose asObservable() and a method. Sixth, takeUntil that is not the last operator. And finally, business logic buried in a chain of ten operators with no intermediate names — the fix is not fewer operators, it is extracting named pipeable functions so a reader can follow it, which is also the only way it becomes testable.

07HTTP, resources and data loading

Data loading is where the signal migration finally paid off for application developers, because it is the one place where "state" and "events over time" genuinely collide: a fetch is an event, but what the UI needs is state — the value, whether it is loading, and whether it failed. For a decade every Angular team hand-rolled that triple, badly, in every component. The Resource API — resource(), rxResource() and httpResource(), experimental in v19/v20 and stable in v22 — is the framework's answer.

  //   ticket.value()      Ticket | undefined

  readonly comments = rxResource({

  });

Important boundary: resources are for reads. A resource re-runs when its parameters change, which is exactly wrong for a mutation — you do not want a POST re-firing because a signal moved. Interview trap

Using a resource for a mutation. It looks tempting — you already have loading and error state — but a resource's whole contract is "this value is a function of these parameters, re-derive it when they change". A POST is not a function of its parameters; it is an event with a side effect. Wire a save button to a resource and you get a request fired on every keystroke that touches a parameter, duplicate charges on a payment form, and no way to say "not yet". The correct shape is an explicit method that returns a promise, plus a separate small signal for the in-flight state if you need it, and then either resource.reload() or an optimistic resource.set() to reflect the result. In v22 Signal Forms has a submission API that models exactly this for the form case, which is the strongest hint about the intended split.

Gotcha

Upload progress silently disappears on v22. Because fetch is now the default backend and the Fetch API cannot report upload progress, an app that shows a percentage bar during file upload will find the progress events stop arriving after the upgrade — with no error, just a bar that jumps from 0 to 100. The fix is to opt back into the XHR backend with withXhr() and use reportUploadProgress. Related: reportProgress is deprecated in favour of the two explicit flags, so a blanket reportProgress: true now means "download progress" only. This is the kind of detail that separates "I read the release notes" from "I did the upgrade".

Interview Q&A

What is the Resource API and what problem does it actually solve?

It solves the fact that every Angular team has independently written the same buggy async state machine hundreds of times. A read is not just a value — it is a value plus a loading flag plus an error plus a notion of "we have old data while new data is arriving", and it has to re-run when its inputs change and cancel what is in flight when they do. Hand-rolled versions get some of that right and one or two things wrong, usually the race condition and the reload-versus-initial-load distinction. resource() takes a params function and a loader and gives you signals for value, error, isLoading and a five-state status, plus reload() and a local set() for optimistic updates; rxResource() is the same with an observable loader, and httpResource() is the same specialised to HttpClient GETs with Transfer State integration for SSR. They went stable in v22, along with chain() for dependent resources and an id option that lets a server-rendered fetch be reused on the client. The important limit is that it is a read API — cancellation on parameter change is precisely the wrong semantics for a write.

How would you handle a request that depends on the result of another request?

In v22 the intended tool is chain(), which composes resources so the second one's parameters can read the first one's value and the whole thing stays a single reactive graph — no nesting, no manual "only fetch if the first resolved" guard, and cancellation propagates correctly. Before that, the pattern was a second httpResource whose params function returns undefined until the first has a value, which is a supported idiom: a resource with undefined params is idle and does not fetch. The RxJS answer, which is still right when the dependency involves more than parameter passing, is switchMap from the first to the second, or forkJoin when several independent calls must all complete. The design question I would raise in an interview is whether the dependency should exist at all: two sequential round trips is two times the latency, and very often the right fix is a backend endpoint that returns both, or a batch/GraphQL-style query. Waterfall requests are one of the highest-impact and least-glamorous performance problems in real applications, and noticing that in the design rather than optimising the client afterwards is the lead-level move.

Design error handling for HTTP across a whole application.

Three layers, each with a clear job. At the interceptor layer I handle everything that is cross-cutting and identical everywhere: attaching auth, refreshing an expired token once and replaying the request, mapping transport failures to a typed application error, retrying idempotent GETs with backoff and jitter, and reporting to the error tracker with a correlation id. Interceptors must not show UI — a toast from an interceptor is how you get four identical toasts for one failed page. At the feature layer I decide what a failure means for this screen: a resource's error() rendered as an inline error with a retry button for a data panel, a field-level error for a form submission, a full-page error state for a route resolve failure. At the global layer I install an ErrorHandler for anything unhandled, which should log and show a generic recovery affordance rather than a stack trace. Two details I would insist on. Retries only on idempotent verbs unless the endpoint accepts an idempotency key, because retrying a POST is how you charge someone twice. And error messages that are honest about the category: distinguishing "you are offline", "we could not reach the server", "you are not allowed to do that" and "something went wrong on our side" changes what the user does next, and lumping them into "Error" is a real product failure.

Interceptors: functional or class-based, and what order do they run in?

Functional, registered through withInterceptors([...]). They are plain functions using inject(), so they are trivially unit-testable, tree-shakeable, and free of the class-plus-multi-token ceremony; the class form with HTTP_INTERCEPTORS still works and is what you will find in older codebases. Ordering is the part people get wrong: interceptors run in registration order on the way out and in reverse order on the way back, like middleware. So an interceptor registered first sees the request earliest and the response latest. That matters concretely: a logging interceptor registered before the auth interceptor will log requests without the Authorization header, and a caching interceptor placed after a transform interceptor caches transformed rather than raw responses. The array form of withInterceptors is a real improvement here because the order is visible in one place rather than implied by provider registration across several files. One caution for teams migrating: if you have both functional and class interceptors, the class ones registered via withInterceptorsFromDi() run as a group relative to the functional ones, so mixing them makes the ordering harder to reason about — I would convert them all.

How do you cache HTTP responses in Angular?

I would ask what problem we are solving first, because there are four different caches and people conflate them. HTTP caching in the browser, driven by Cache-Control and ETag headers, is free and correct and should be exhausted before writing any code — a lot of client caching exists only because nobody set headers. An interceptor-level cache is right for deduplicating identical in-flight GETs and for short-lived memoisation of reference data; it is a Map keyed by URL plus relevant headers, returning the same observable to concurrent callers, with an explicit TTL and an invalidation path — and it must never cache authenticated responses across users. An application-level cache is a service holding signals, which is really "state that happens to have come from the server" and is the right place for things like the current user or a list you mutate optimistically; the eviction policy is then a domain decision. And under SSR there is Transfer State, which is not really a cache but avoids re-fetching on the client what the server already fetched — httpResource does this automatically with an id. The thing I would flag in v22 is that transfer caching now excludes credential-bearing requests by default, which is a security fix: previously an authenticated response could be serialised into the HTML and served from a shared CDN cache to the wrong person.

A user reports "sometimes the wrong data shows". How do you diagnose it?

My first hypothesis is a request race: two requests in flight for the same view, and the slower one landing last. That happens with mergeMap where switchMap was needed, with a manual subscribe that has no cancellation, or with a hand-rolled loader that does not check whether its parameters are still current. It is network-dependent, which is why it appears "sometimes" and never in review — throttling the network in DevTools usually reproduces it in a minute. The second hypothesis is caching, and the frightening version is a shared cache keyed without the user or tenant, which under SSR or a CDN means one user seeing another's data; I check that any cache key includes identity and that authenticated responses are marked private. The third is state that outlives its scope: a root-scoped service holding page state, so navigating away and back shows the previous page's data until the new fetch lands. The fourth is optimistic updates that were not rolled back on failure. The structural fix for the first is httpResource or switchMap, for the third is route-scoped providers, and for the fourth is treating rollback as a required part of any optimistic write rather than an afterthought.

How do you implement optimistic updates well?

The shape is: apply the change locally, send the request, and reconcile. With the Resource API that is resource.set(next), which moves the status to local so the UI can show the pending state if it wants, then either reload() on success to converge with the server's version, or restore the captured previous value on failure. Three things make the difference between an optimistic update that feels magic and one that produces support tickets. First, keep the pre-update value explicitly so rollback is exact — reconstructing it from a diff is where bugs live. Second, decide what happens to concurrent edits: if a second edit is applied while the first is in flight, rolling back the first must not discard the second, which usually means a small queue or a version number rather than a naive restore. Third, tell the user when it failed, and do it where the change was made rather than in a corner toast they will miss — silently reverting a value the user typed is worse than never having applied it. I would also be selective about where to use it at all: it is right for high-frequency, low-stakes edits like toggling a flag or reordering a list, and wrong for anything with money or legal consequence, where the honest spinner is the better product decision.

What changed in HttpClient in v22 and what does it break?

The backend switched from XMLHttpRequest to fetch by default, which is why withFetch() is now deprecated — it does nothing you do not already have. That is mostly good: fetch works in non-browser runtimes, supports streaming responses, and integrates with AbortController cleanly. The breakage is upload progress, because the Fetch API cannot report it; reportProgress is deprecated and split into reportDownloadProgress and reportUploadProgress, and the upload flag requires opting back into the XHR backend with withXhr(). So any app with a file-upload progress bar has real work to do, and the symptom is silent — the bar just stops moving until it completes. Alongside that, v22 tightened transfer caching to exclude credentialed requests by default, and added SSRF protections and stricter binding sanitisation. The interview-worthy summary is that this release moved HttpClient onto the platform primitive and paid for it with one specific capability regression that has a documented opt-out.

Would you use a data-fetching library like TanStack Query with Angular?

It was a very defensible choice before v22 and it is a narrower one now. What those libraries gave you that Angular did not was a request cache with keys, deduplication of concurrent identical requests, stale-while-revalidate semantics, background refetch on window focus or reconnect, pagination and infinite-scroll helpers, and mutation orchestration with rollback. The Resource API now covers the core loop — reactive params, cancellation, loading and error state, reload, optimistic local set, and SSR transfer — but it deliberately does not include a global cache keyed across components, window-focus refetching, or a mutation layer. So the decision comes down to whether the application's shape needs those. A dashboard with many components requesting overlapping data, where deduplication and a shared cache are the point, still benefits. A CRUD app with route-scoped data does not, and adding a second reactivity model plus a dependency you must keep aligned with Angular's release cadence is a real cost. My default now would be to start with httpResource, build the two or three cache behaviours we actually need on top of a small service, and reach for a library only when we find ourselves reimplementing its third feature.

How do you test code that uses httpResource?

provideHttpClientTesting() with HttpTestingController still works and is where I would start: configure the TestBed, create the component, then expect the request, flush a response, and assert on the rendered output. The one thing that differs from testing an observable-based service is that a resource's fetch is triggered reactively rather than by a call you make, so the test has to let change detection run before the request exists — with TestBed that is a fixture.detectChanges() or an await fixture.whenStable() before expectOne, and forgetting it produces a confusing "expected one request, found none". Beyond the happy path I make sure to cover three cases explicitly, because they are the ones the Resource API exists to get right and therefore the ones a regression would silently break: the loading state renders a skeleton, an error response renders the error branch and reload() recovers, and changing the parameter cancels the previous request rather than applying a stale response — which you can assert by flushing the responses out of order. For anything above the unit level I would not mock the HTTP layer at all and would use an interception proxy or a component test against a stubbed server, because the interesting failures are in the interceptor chain rather than in the component.

08Routing, lazy loading, defer

The router is the piece of Angular that most directly shapes architecture, because it is simultaneously the URL contract, the code-splitting boundary, the lifetime boundary for DI, the data-loading trigger, and the SSR rendering-mode boundary. Every one of those is an interview topic, and a lead is expected to see them as one decision rather than five.

Mental model: a route configuration is a tree, and the router's job on every navigation is to compute the new tree state, run the guards and resolvers that gate it, load whatever code is missing, and then swap component subtrees at each router-outlet where the tree changed. Everything else — params, data, lazy loading, injector scopes — hangs off that.

A modern route file

export const routes: Routes = [
  { path: '', pathMatch: 'full', redirectTo: 'tickets' },

  {
    path: 'tickets',
    // Lazy-load a whole feature's routes, not a module.
    loadChildren: () => import('./tickets/routes').then(m => m.TICKET_ROUTES),

    providers: [TicketFilters],

    // SSR render mode per route (v20+)
    data: { title: 'Tickets' },

  },

  { path: '**', loadComponent: () => import('./not-found').then(m => m.NotFound) },

  providers: [
    provideRouter(
      routes,
      withComponentInputBinding({ queryParams: true }),  // v22 options
      withViewTransitions(),                             // v17: native View Transitions API

withComponentInputBinding() is the quality-of-life feature people forget: route params, query params, and resolved data are bound directly to matching component inputs, so id = input.required<string>() just receives the :id segment. No ActivatedRoute, no subscription, no paramMap pipe — and because it is a signal input, a navigation from /tickets/1 to /tickets/2 that reuses the component updates it reactively, which used to be the number-one router bug.

Interview trap

"We lazy-load everything, so our initial bundle is small." Lazy loading only helps if the chunk boundaries match usage, and it is very easy to lazy-load nothing at all by accident. The two classic mistakes: a lazy feature that imports a shared barrel file (@app/shared) which re-exports everything, so the "lazy" chunk drags in the whole shared library and the bundler often hoists it into the main chunk anyway; and a service that is providedIn: 'root' but only used by a lazy feature, whose imports therefore land in the eager graph. The way to answer well is to say you verify rather than assume: run the build with --stats-json and inspect the chunk graph, and check that the lazy chunk actually appears as a separate network request when you navigate. canMatch is worth naming too — it is the only guard that prevents the chunk from being downloaded, because canActivate runs after the code has already been fetched.

Gotcha

The component is reused across a param change and nothing reloads. Navigating from /tickets/1 to /tickets/2 matches the same route, so by default Angular reuses the component instance: ngOnInit does not run again, and any data you loaded there is stale. The historical fixes were subscribing to route.paramMap, or setting onSameUrlNavigation and a RouteReuseStrategy. The modern fix is to make the param a signal input via withComponentInputBinding() and derive everything from it — an httpResource keyed on the input re-fetches automatically and cancels the previous request. A v22 wrinkle in the same area: paramsInheritanceStrategy now defaults to 'always', so child routes inherit parent params and data. That fixes far more bugs than it causes, but it has no migration, so a child that previously read undefined from data now reads the parent's value.

Try it — make the chunk graph real

1. Build with ng build --stats-json and open the stats in a bundle visualiser. Find one lazy route and confirm it is a separate chunk. Then add an import of a shared barrel into it and rebuild.

2. Take a long page and wrap the below-the-fold half in @defer (on viewport). Throttle the network to Slow 3G and compare first paint.

3. Navigate between two ids of the same route with data loaded in ngOnInit, then convert to a signal input plus httpResource.

Expected outcome

(1) The barrel import usually pulls a surprising amount into the lazy chunk, and sometimes moves shared code into the main bundle because two lazy chunks now both need it — which is the concrete argument for deep imports over barrels. (2) On Slow 3G the deferred version paints the above-the-fold content noticeably sooner, and the Network panel shows the deferred chunk requested only when you scroll. (3) The ngOnInit version shows ticket 1's data on ticket 2's URL until you force a reload; the signal-input version updates immediately and cancels the superseded request.

Interview Q&A

Walk me through what happens on a navigation.

A navigation starts from a link, an imperative router.navigate, or a browser history event. The router parses the URL into a tree of segments, then applies redirects and matches segments against the route config depth-first, running canMatch guards as it goes — that is the only stage that can reject a route before its lazy chunk is downloaded. Once a candidate route tree is built, it lazily loads any missing configuration or components, then runs canDeactivate on what is leaving and canActivate and canActivateChild on what is entering; any guard returning false or a UrlTree cancels or redirects. Then resolvers run, and the navigation blocks until they all settle. Finally the router activates the new route tree, creating and destroying component subtrees at each outlet where the tree differs, updating the browser URL, and emitting router events throughout. Two consequences worth naming: route-level providers are created and destroyed with the route subtree, which is what makes route scope a real lifetime; and if the same route matches with different params, the component is reused rather than recreated, which is the source of the classic stale-data bug.

When do you use canMatch versus canActivate?

canActivate answers "may this user open this page", and by the time it runs the route has been matched and its lazy chunk downloaded. canMatch answers "does this route exist for this user at all", and it runs during matching, before any code is fetched — which gives it two capabilities canActivate does not have. First, it can genuinely prevent shipping code: a feature behind a flag or an admin-only bundle should use canMatch, otherwise every user downloads the admin chunk and is merely told no. Second, because a failed match makes the router continue looking, you can register two routes on the same path with different canMatch guards and serve different components — a clean way to do role-specific pages or an A/B test without branching inside a component. I use canActivate for straightforward authentication, where a redirect to the login page is the desired behaviour, and canMatch for authorisation that should make the route invisible and for anything that should not be downloaded. One v22 detail: canMatch now takes a mandatory currentSnapshot parameter, so guards written earlier need a signature update — the migration handles it.

Resolvers or component-level loading? Defend the trade-off.

My default is component-level loading with a skeleton, and I treat resolvers as an exception that needs justification. The reason is perceived performance: a resolver blocks the navigation, so the user clicks and the UI sits there doing nothing until the request finishes, with no feedback unless you also wire up router events to a progress bar. Component-level loading navigates instantly, paints the layout, and fills it in — which is both faster to first paint and much easier to make feel good, and with httpResource it is now less code than a resolver. The exceptions where a resolver is right: data needed to render correct SSR output, such as a page title, meta tags or an Open Graph image, because a skeleton is what the crawler would otherwise index; a check that depends on the record itself and should redirect rather than render, such as "this order was cancelled, go to the archive view"; and cases where a partial render would be actively misleading rather than merely empty. When I do use a resolver I make sure it has a timeout and an error path, because a resolver that never settles is a navigation that never happens, and the user's only recourse is to reload the page.

How do you decide lazy-loading boundaries in a large app?

I start from user journeys rather than folder structure. The unit that should be a chunk is a set of routes a user visits together and that a different user might never visit at all — the admin area, the reporting section, the onboarding flow, the settings pages. Splitting more finely than that produces many small requests and a waterfall on navigation; splitting less finely means shipping code nobody uses. Then I look at the heavy leaves independently of routing: a rich-text editor, a charting library, a PDF or spreadsheet exporter, a map. Those should be split by @defer or, in v22, by injectAsync at the service level, because they are often larger than the route that hosts them and are used by a minority of visitors. The third thing I check is shared code: a barrel file re-exported across features defeats splitting, and a service in root that only a lazy feature uses will be pulled eagerly. Finally I verify rather than assume — a stats build and a bundle visualiser after every significant change, and a CI budget that fails the build when the initial bundle grows past a threshold, because bundle size regresses one innocent import at a time.

What are deferrable views and how do they differ from route-level lazy loading?

@defer code-splits inside a template rather than at a route boundary, so a single page can ship its critical content immediately and load the rest on a trigger. The block declares a set of triggers — on viewport, on interaction, on hover, on idle, on timer, or when with an expression — plus optional @placeholder, @loading and @error blocks, and a separate prefetch trigger so you can download early and render late. The compiler creates a real chunk boundary, and the dependencies must be used only inside the deferred block, otherwise there is nothing to split and the compiler tells you. The difference from route-level lazy loading is granularity and intent: routes split by destination, defer splits by importance within a destination. The two compose — a lazy route that internally defers its heavy widgets is normal. The timing options matter more than people expect: @placeholder (minimum 300ms) and @loading (after 100ms; minimum 500ms) exist to prevent the flash of a spinner that appears and disappears in 60ms, which reads as a glitch. And under SSR, deferred blocks are what incremental hydration keys off, which is the subject of the next unit.

How do you handle route state: query params, path params, or a service?

The URL should hold anything a user would reasonably expect to survive a refresh, a back button, or being sent to a colleague. Path params identify the resource — the ticket id, the tenant. Query params hold the view state over it: filters, sort, page number, an open tab, a search term. Getting this right is a product decision disguised as a technical one, because "I filtered a list, opened a record, hit back, and lost my filters" is one of the most common complaints about internal tools, and it is entirely caused by keeping that state in a service. What does not belong in the URL is transient UI state — whether a tooltip is open, scroll position, a half-typed value — and anything sensitive, since URLs end up in logs, referrer headers and browser history. For sharing state between sibling routes I use a route-scoped provider, so the lifetime matches the feature and it resets when the user leaves. In v22 two router changes help here: withComponentInputBinding({ queryParams: true }) binds query params to signal inputs so there is no subscription, and paramsInheritanceStrategy defaulting to 'always' means a child route can read the parent's params without walking up the snapshot chain.

How would you implement breadcrumbs, page titles and analytics for routing?

Titles first, because Angular has a built-in answer people miss: a title on the route, or a TitleStrategy subclass when the title depends on resolved data or needs a suffix. It sets document.title correctly and, importantly, announces the page change to screen readers, which a hand-rolled version usually forgets. Breadcrumbs come from walking the activated route tree and reading a convention from data — a static label, or a resolver output for dynamic segments like an entity name; I build that once as a signal derived from router events rather than letting each page assemble its own. Analytics hangs off the same events: I subscribe to NavigationEnd once, in a root service, and emit a page view with the normalised route path rather than the raw URL, so /tickets/1234 and /tickets/5678 aggregate as /tickets/:id instead of producing thousands of distinct pages. Two details I insist on. Focus management: a single-page navigation does not move focus, so keyboard and screen-reader users stay wherever they were — moving focus to the main heading on NavigationEnd is the fix. And error events: subscribing to NavigationError and NavigationCancel catches lazy-chunk load failures after a deploy, which otherwise look to the user like a dead link.

What is RouteReuseStrategy and when have you needed it?

It is the extension point that decides whether the router destroys a component subtree or detaches and stores it for later reattachment. The default reuses a component when the same route matches with different params, and destroys it otherwise. The case where you need a custom strategy is a tabbed workspace: a user has three ticket detail tabs open, switches between them, and expects scroll position, unsaved input and expanded panels to be exactly as they left them. Re-rendering from cached data is not enough because it loses DOM state. A custom strategy lets you keep those subtrees alive and reattach them. I would reach for it reluctantly, though, because it is genuinely hard to get right: you own the cache eviction, and holding component subtrees alive means holding their subscriptions, timers and memory alive too, so a naive implementation is a leak that grows with usage. Before writing one I would ask whether the requirement can be met by persisting the state instead of the DOM — a signal service keyed by tab id restores scroll and form values well enough for most products, and it survives a page refresh, which a detached view does not.

How do you handle a lazy chunk that 404s after a deployment?

This is one of the most common production incidents in any SPA and interviewers like it because it is operational rather than syntactic. The cause: the user loaded the app before a deploy, the deploy replaced hashed chunk files, and now a lazy import requests a filename that no longer exists. The user sees a navigation that silently fails, and the error appears as a chunk-load failure in the tracker. There are three layers of fix. Serve-side: keep the previous build's chunks available for a grace period rather than deleting them on deploy — with immutable, hashed filenames this is cheap and it eliminates most of the problem. Client-side: subscribe to NavigationError, detect a chunk-load failure, and reload the page at the target URL, which fetches the new manifest and completes the navigation with one visible flash instead of a dead link. Product-side: detect a new deployment — a version endpoint polled occasionally, or a service worker update event — and prompt the user to refresh at a moment of their choosing. I would do all three, in that order of priority, and I would make sure the reload-on-chunk-error path has a loop guard, because reloading into the same error is a much worse failure than the original.

How do you make single-page navigation accessible?

The core problem is that a client-side navigation changes the page without the browser's usual page-load behaviour, so three things that normally happen for free do not. Focus stays where it was, which for a keyboard or screen-reader user means they are still inside the old page's navigation menu with no idea anything changed; the fix is to move focus to the new view's main heading or to a skip target on NavigationEnd, and to make that element programmatically focusable. The document title does not change unless you change it, so screen readers announce nothing; Angular's TitleStrategy or a route title handles that, and it also fixes the browser history and tab labels. And scroll position is not restored, which affects everyone; withInMemoryScrolling({ scrollPositionRestoration: 'enabled', anchorScrolling: 'enabled' }) is the one-line fix that most apps are missing. Beyond those, I would add a visually-hidden live region announcing route changes for the cases where moving focus is too disruptive, make sure loading skeletons are announced as busy rather than as garbage text, and be careful with withViewTransitions(), which is a genuine improvement visually but must respect prefers-reduced-motion.

Signal Forms

import { form, required, minLength, email, min, validate, validateHttp, submit, Field } from '@angular/forms/signals';

@Component({
  imports: [Field],
  template: `
    <form (submit)="save($event)">
      <input [field]="f.name" />
      @if (f.name().touched() && f.name().errors().length) {
        <p class="err">{{ f.name().errors()[0].message }}</p>
      }

      <input [field]="f.email" type="email" />
      <input [field]="f.age" type="number" />

      <button [disabled]="f().invalid() || f().submitting()">Save</button>
    </form>
  `,
})
export class ProfileForm {
  // 1. The model is an ordinary signal holding an ordinary object.
  private readonly model = signal({ name: '', email: '', age: 0 });

  // 2. The form is a FieldTree derived from it, with a schema describing the rules.
  readonly f = form(this.model, (path) => {
    required(path.name, { message: 'Name is required' });
    minLength(path.name, 2);
    email(path.email);
    min(path.age, 18, { message: 'Must be 18 or over' });

    // Cross-field rules see the whole model.
    validate(path.email, ({ value, valueOf }) =>
      value().endsWith('@example.com') && valueOf(path.age) < 21
        ? { kind: 'domainAge', message: 'Example accounts must be 21+' }
        : null);

    // Async validation with debouncing built in (v22).
    validateHttp(path.email, {
      request: ({ value }) => `/api/email-available?e=${value()}`,
      debounce: 400,
      errors: (taken) => taken ? { kind: 'taken', message: 'Already registered' } : null,
    });
  });

  save(e: Event) {
    return submit(this.f, async (form) => {
      const res = await this.api.save(form().value());
      return res.ok ? undefined : res.fieldErrors;   // server errors map back onto fields
    });
  }
}

The design idea is worth stating clearly, because it is what interviewers want to hear: Signal Forms invert the relationship between the model and the form. In reactive forms, the FormGroup is the model, so you build a parallel structure that mirrors your domain type and then map between them — which is why every reactive-forms codebase has patchValue calls and mapping functions and a getRawValue() cast. In Signal Forms the model is your own typed object in a plain signal, and the form is a derived view over it carrying validation and UI state. Nothing to map, and the schema is type-checked against the model's shape.

Interview trap

"Disabled controls are excluded from form.value." This is the classic reactive-forms gotcha and it is asked constantly. form.value returns only enabled controls, so disabling a field before submit silently drops it from the payload — and the symptom is a backend rejecting a request for a missing field that is clearly visible on screen. getRawValue() returns everything, and is what you almost always want on submit. The related trap is that a control disabled in the template with [disabled] on a reactive control produces the "it looks like you're using the disabled attribute with a reactive form directive" warning, because the form model and the DOM now disagree about who owns that state. Signal Forms fixes the underlying design: disabled() with a when condition is declarative, and the value remains part of your model because your model is a plain object you own.

Gotcha

Async validators run on every keystroke unless you tell them not to. A "is this email taken" validator on a reactive control fires a request per character by default: type a twenty-character address and you have made twenty requests, and because async validators do not cancel, the responses can land out of order and leave the control in the wrong state. The reactive-forms fixes are updateOn: 'blur' on the control, or debouncing inside the validator with a switch-mapped stream — both of which people write from memory and get subtly wrong. Signal Forms builds it in: validateHttp takes a debounce option, cancellation is handled, and v22 added reloadValidation() for re-running async validators after something external changes, plus debounce(form.field, 'blur') for the value side.

Interview Q&A

Compare template-driven, reactive and Signal Forms. Which would you pick today?

Template-driven forms put the source of truth in the template with ngModel and validation directives; they are quick for a two-field form and become unmanageable beyond that, because there is no object to test and cross-field logic has nowhere to live. Reactive forms put an explicit FormGroup tree in the class, which is testable, composable and typed since v14 — and which duplicates your domain model, so you spend real effort on patchValue, mapping and getRawValue() casts, and you consume state through valueChanges subscriptions. Signal Forms, stable in v22, keeps the model as an ordinary signal of your own typed object and derives a field tree over it with a schema function that declares the rules; consumption is by reading signals, so there are no subscriptions, and the schema is type-checked against the model. For new work in v22 I would pick Signal Forms — it removes the duplication that caused most reactive-forms defects, and it is stable rather than preview. For existing reactive forms I would not migrate wholesale; v22 shipped interop through compatForm and SignalFormControl precisely so the two can coexist, and I would convert forms when they are being changed anyway. Template-driven I would leave alone and not write new.

How do you implement cross-field validation, and where does it go wrong?

In reactive forms it goes on the group rather than the control, because only the group can see both fields, and that is where the problems start: the error lands on the group, so displaying it next to the confirm-password input means reaching up to the parent and checking a specific error key; group.get('password') returns AbstractControl | null with no type information, so a renamed field fails at runtime; and the error state does not clear on the field the user is fixing unless you wire that up. In Signal Forms the rule is declared on the field it should appear on, and the validator receives the whole model through a valueOf accessor, so the error is attached where the user is looking and the model access is typed. Either way, the design questions are the same and are what I would actually discuss: when should the rule run — usually on blur or submit rather than per keystroke, because telling someone their passwords do not match while they are still typing the second one is hostile; where should the message appear — on the field the user can act on; and does the rule need to be re-evaluated when the other field changes, which is the bug people ship, since editing the first password field must revalidate the confirmation.

How do you handle a dynamic form whose fields come from a server-driven schema?

This is the case that separates the form systems. With reactive forms you build the FormGroup imperatively from the schema, adding and removing controls with addControl and removeControl as the schema changes, and you accept that the form is typed as a loose record — the type safety that v14 added does not survive a structure you only learn at runtime. With Signal Forms the model can be a record signal and the schema function can branch on the server description, and v22 added validateStandardSchema() which accepts a standard-schema validator, so a Zod or Valibot schema fetched or generated from the backend can drive validation directly rather than being translated by hand. That last part is the real win: the most common defect in server-driven forms is client and server validation drifting apart, and sharing one schema definition removes the drift by construction. The rendering side is a recursive component that switches on a field type and delegates to registered controls, and the thing I would insist on architecturally is a registry keyed by type rather than a growing switch statement, so teams can contribute field types without editing a central file. And regardless of the mechanism, the server must still validate everything, because a client-driven schema is a convenience and never a control.

How do you build a reusable custom form control?

The classic mechanism is ControlValueAccessor: implement writeValue, registerOnChange, registerOnTouched and setDisabledState, and register the component under NG_VALUE_ACCESSOR with multi: true and useExisting plus forwardRef. It works with both template-driven and reactive forms, and v22 made CVA components work with Signal Forms too, so an existing library of controls keeps its value. The parts people get wrong are the ones that only show up in real use: registerOnTouched must actually be called on blur or the control never becomes touched and error display never triggers; setDisabledState must be implemented or the control ignores disable(); and validation should be exposed through NG_VALIDATORS rather than swallowed inside the component, otherwise the parent form's validity does not reflect it. For new work in a v22 codebase, FormValueControl and the signal-forms field binding are the lighter path — the control exposes a value model and the framework wires it — and the interop means you do not have to choose one for the whole design system at once. Whichever mechanism, the accessibility contract is the part I care about most in review: a custom control needs a real label association, a focusable element, keyboard operation matching the native equivalent, and aria-invalid and aria-describedby pointing at the error text.

A form with 60 fields is slow to type in. What is wrong?

Most likely one of four things, and I would profile rather than guess. First, validation running on every keystroke across the whole tree — every value change revalidates the group, and if any validator is expensive or asynchronous that is sixty validators per character; updateOn: 'blur' on the noisy controls, or debounced async validation, usually fixes it outright. Second, change detection: in a zone-based app without OnPush, every keystroke checks the entire component tree, and a large form is a lot of bindings — this is the case where OnPush and signals produce a step change rather than an incremental one. Third, template expressions: a form template with function calls in bindings, especially inside a repeated section, re-evaluates them on every check; error-message helpers are the usual culprit. Fourth, valueChanges subscriptions doing real work per keystroke — recomputing a summary, rebuilding a derived list, or writing to storage. Beyond fixing those, the structural answer for genuinely huge forms is to stop rendering them all at once: split into steps or sections, and use @defer or a simple conditional so only the visible section exists in the DOM. Sixty fields on one screen is usually also a product problem worth raising.

How do you show validation errors well?

The rules I would write into a design system. Do not show an error before the user has had a chance: validate on blur or on submit, not on the first keystroke, so a required field does not turn red the moment it is focused — Angular's touched and dirty flags exist for exactly this and the common bug is checking invalid alone. Once a field is in error, switch that field to validating on change so the error clears as soon as it is fixed, which is the behaviour that feels responsive. Put the message adjacent to the field, associated with aria-describedby, and set aria-invalid, so screen readers get it; a red border alone fails both accessibility and colour-blind users. On submit, mark everything touched, focus the first invalid field, and if the form is long, show a summary at the top listing the errors as links — that is a WCAG-friendly pattern and it is what users actually need on a twenty-field form. Messages should say what to do rather than what is wrong: "Enter a date in the future" beats "Invalid date". And server-side errors must map back onto fields rather than appearing as a generic banner, which is exactly what the Signal Forms submit API supports by letting the submit handler return field errors.

Should the team migrate its reactive forms to Signal Forms?

Not as a project. Reactive forms are not deprecated, they work, and a bulk migration of a large form surface is a high-risk diff with no user-visible benefit — forms are exactly the code where subtle behavioural regressions turn into lost customer data. What I would do is set the direction and let it happen at the edges: new forms use Signal Forms, and an existing form converts when it is already being substantially changed. v22 shipped interop for exactly this — compatForm and SignalFormControl let the two systems coexist in one screen, and ControlValueAccessor components work with both, so the design system does not have to fork. The forms I would prioritise converting are the ones where the reactive model is causing real defects: forms with heavy patchValue and mapping code, forms with a tangle of valueChanges subscriptions, and forms where the disabled-control-drops-from-value trap has already bitten. And I would set the expectation that this is a two-year drift, not a quarter's work, which is a much easier commitment to keep than a migration sprint that gets abandoned at forty percent.

How do you test forms?

Reactive and signal forms are both testable without the DOM, and that is where I put most of the coverage: build the form or model, set values, and assert on validity and errors. Those tests are fast, they express the business rules directly, and they do not break when the markup changes. But they are not sufficient, because the defects users hit are in the wiring — a control not bound, an error not displayed, a disabled field dropping out of the payload, a submit handler that does not mark fields touched. So I add a smaller set of component tests that drive the real DOM: type into inputs, blur, submit, and assert on what is rendered. For those I strongly prefer querying by accessible role and label rather than by CSS class, because that catches missing labels as a side effect and because it does not break when someone renames a class. Async validation gets explicit tests for the debounce and for out-of-order responses, since that is where the real bugs are. And for a design system's custom controls I would test the ControlValueAccessor contract directly — writeValue reflects, changes propagate, blur marks touched, disabled state applies — because every one of those has a silent failure mode.

What is the deal with typed reactive forms, and what did v14 not fix?

Before v14, form.value was any and every control was untyped, so a renamed field failed silently at runtime. v14 made the form classes generic, so fb.group({ name: fb.control('') }) infers a typed group and form.value has a real shape. What it did not fix is the awkward parts. Controls are nullable by default because reset() sets them to null, so every value is T | null unless you use NonNullableFormBuilder or pass nonNullable: true, and mixing the two in one codebase is a constant small friction. form.value is a deep Partial, because disabled controls are excluded, so the type honestly reflects the trap but does not remove it — getRawValue() gives the complete type and is what you want on submit. get('path.to.field') is still stringly-typed and returns AbstractControl | null, so any nested access loses the types you just gained. And dynamic forms cannot be typed at all, because the structure is not known statically. Those residual problems are a large part of why Signal Forms exists: when the model is your own object in a signal, the typing question mostly disappears.

How would you approach a design system's form components across many teams?

I would separate three layers and version them independently. The bottom layer is behaviour and accessibility: unstyled directives providing keyboard interaction, ARIA wiring and focus management — this is what @angular/aria now provides in the box as of v22, and building on it rather than reimplementing it is the single biggest quality win available. The middle layer is the styled controls, composing those directives with hostDirectives and exposing design tokens as CSS custom properties so themes do not require forks. The top layer is form-level composition: a field wrapper that owns label, hint, error placement and the aria-describedby plumbing, so no product team writes that association by hand and gets it wrong. Across teams, the things I would standardise hard are the accessibility contract, the error-display timing, and the field wrapper; the things I would deliberately leave open are layout and copy. On the forms-system question I would support both reactive and signal forms through ControlValueAccessor plus the v22 interop, because a design system that forces every consuming team to migrate on the same day will simply not be adopted. And I would ship it with a schematic that scaffolds a conformant form, because that is how conventions actually spread.

The mental model: SSR produces HTML the browser can paint before any JavaScript runs; hydration is the process of attaching the already-running framework to that existing DOM instead of throwing it away and re-rendering. Every SSR bug you will ever debug comes from the gap between what the server rendered and what the client expects to find.

Hydration, and why it got incremental

Angular's first hydration (v16) was non-destructive full hydration: the client walks the server-rendered DOM, matches it against what it would have rendered, reuses the nodes, and attaches listeners and bindings. Compared to the pre-v16 behaviour — which destroyed the server DOM and re-rendered from scratch, producing a visible flicker and a Largest Contentful Paint that fired twice — it was a large improvement. But it still had to hydrate the whole page before anything was interactive, which meant downloading and executing every component's code even for content below the fold.

Incremental hydration (stable v20, default in v22) fixes that by reusing @defer. A deferred block's server-rendered HTML is sent as normal, but its JavaScript is not loaded until the block's hydrate trigger fires — so a page can be fully painted and mostly interactive while the comments section, the chart and the footer remain inert HTML until you scroll to them.

@defer (hydrate on viewport) {
  <app-comments [ticketId]="id()" />
}

@defer (hydrate on interaction) {
  <app-share-menu />
}

@defer (hydrate never) {
  <app-legal-footer />   <!-- static: rendered on the server, JS never shipped -->
}

hydrate never is the one to remember: it is how you ship genuinely static regions as HTML only, with zero client-side cost. Note the distinction from a plain @defer trigger — on viewport means "do not render until visible", while hydrate on viewport means "the server already rendered it; do not make it interactive until visible".

Interview trap

Enabling SSR "for performance" without saying which metric. SSR improves First Contentful Paint and Largest Contentful Paint, because the browser has HTML to paint before the JavaScript arrives. It does not improve Time to Interactive on its own — historically it made it worse, because the browser now downloads HTML and the same JavaScript, then hydrates. It also adds server cost and an operational surface you did not have. So the honest framing is: SSR is a first-paint and crawlability decision, not a general speed-up, and for an authenticated internal dashboard where nobody indexes anything and the first paint is behind a login, it is usually the wrong trade. Incremental hydration is what finally improves the interactivity side, by not shipping and executing code for content nobody has scrolled to. Being able to say which metric you are buying, and what you are paying, is the whole answer.

Gotcha

ngSkipHydration is a bandage, and it is contagious. When a hydration mismatch appears — usually from a third-party library that manipulates the DOM directly — the quick fix is to add ngSkipHydration to that component's host, which tells Angular to destroy and re-render that subtree instead of reusing it. It works, and it silently gives up SSR's benefit for everything inside, including a large subtree if you put it too high. Two rules: put it on the smallest possible wrapper around the offending element, and treat every instance as a tracked debt item with a note on why. The alternative fix is almost always better — initialise the library inside afterNextRender so it only touches the DOM on the client, at which point the server-rendered markup and the client's expectation still agree.

Interview Q&A

When should an application use SSR, and when should it not?

SSR earns its cost when the first paint matters to someone who is not already logged in, or when a crawler needs to see content. So: marketing sites, e-commerce catalogues, documentation, news, anything with organic search traffic or social previews, and any consumer app where the first-visit experience on a mobile network is a business metric. It does not earn its cost on an authenticated internal tool where every user is a repeat visitor behind a login, nobody indexes anything, and the app shell is cached after the first load — there you are paying server compute and a whole operational surface for a metric nobody looks at. The nuance I would add is that it is no longer an all-or-nothing decision: since v20 the render mode is per route, so the marketing pages can be prerendered at build time, the search page rendered per request, and the account area rendered client-side only, which is also the correct security posture for private data. The other thing I would insist on is that SSR means running a Node server, which means deployment, scaling, monitoring and a new class of incident — if the organisation is not ready for that, prerendering gives you most of the SEO benefit with none of the runtime.

Explain hydration, and what changed with incremental hydration.

Hydration is the client-side framework attaching to server-rendered DOM rather than re-creating it. Before v16, Angular destroyed the server output and re-rendered from scratch, which caused a visible flicker and made the largest-contentful-paint metric fire twice; v16's non-destructive hydration walks the existing DOM, matches it against what it would have produced, and reuses the nodes while attaching bindings and listeners. That fixed the flicker but not the cost: the whole page still had to be hydrated, meaning every component's JavaScript downloaded and executed before anything was interactive. Incremental hydration, stable in v20 and the default in v22, uses @defer blocks as hydration boundaries: the server renders everything, but a block's code is fetched and hydrated only when its trigger fires — on viewport, on interaction, or never. The practical effect is a page that paints completely and becomes interactive where the user is looking, while the comments, the chart and the footer stay inert HTML until needed. hydrate never is the extreme and very useful case: a region that is rendered on the server and ships no client JavaScript at all. Alongside it, event replay queues clicks that happen before hydration so nothing is lost.

You get NG0500 hydration mismatch in production. How do you debug it?

A mismatch means the DOM the client expected does not match the DOM the server sent, so I look for the three ways that happens. First, non-deterministic rendering: anything using Math.random, Date.now, a locale or timezone that differs between server and browser, or a feature flag evaluated differently on each side — these produce a value that legitimately differs, and the fix is to compute once and transfer, or to render that part only after hydration. Second, invalid HTML: a block element inside a paragraph, a div inside a table row, unclosed tags. The browser's parser silently repairs those, so the DOM tree the client finds is not the tree the server serialised, and this is by a distance the most common cause — validating the rendered HTML finds it in minutes. Third, something else mutating the DOM before hydration: a third-party analytics or chat script, a browser extension, or a library initialised too early. To locate it I reproduce with the dev build, since Angular's dev-mode message names the component and the node; if it only reproduces in production I bisect by adding ngSkipHydration to subtrees until it stops, then remove them one at a time. The fix I aim for is deterministic rendering or deferring the DOM work to afterNextRender; ngSkipHydration is the fallback and should be scoped as tightly as possible.

How do you avoid fetching the same data twice under SSR?

Transfer State is the mechanism: the server serialises the responses it fetched into the HTML payload, and the client reads them from there instead of issuing the request again. Historically you wired that by hand with TransferState and a key per request, and the failure mode was forgetting, which produced a page that fetched everything twice and flashed a loading state after a perfectly good server render. provideClientHydration() made HTTP transfer caching automatic for GET requests, and in v22 httpResource integrates with it directly — a resource given an id is cached across the boundary, so the hydrated component shows the data immediately with no loading state and no second request. Two cautions. First, whatever you transfer is embedded in the HTML in plain text, so it must not contain anything the recipient should not see; v22 changed the default to exclude credential-bearing requests from the transfer cache precisely because an authenticated response ending up in a shared or CDN-cached document is a real data-leak vector. Second, transferred data is a snapshot from the moment of the server render, which may be seconds or, behind a CDN, minutes old — for anything the user could have just changed, the right pattern is to show the transferred value immediately and revalidate in the background.

How do you handle browser-only code in an SSR app?

The rule I enforce is that nothing touches a browser global during construction or in a template expression. Concretely: inject the DOCUMENT token instead of referencing document, so the server can provide its own implementation; put anything that measures or manipulates DOM inside afterNextRender, which by contract only runs in the browser; and for third-party libraries that assume a browser, initialise them there too, or lazy-import them at that point so their module-level code never executes on the server. isPlatformBrowser still exists and is fine for a branch, but I prefer afterNextRender because it also gets the timing right — it runs after the DOM exists, so you are not fighting the render cycle as well. Storage is its own case: localStorage and cookies behave differently, and a component that reads a preference from storage will render one thing on the server and another on the client, which is a hydration mismatch waiting to happen; the answer is either to read it from a cookie the server can also see, or to render a neutral default and apply the preference after hydration. In review, a bare window. or document. outside afterNextRender is an automatic comment, because it will work perfectly until someone enables SSR and then crash the server.

What is event replay and why does it matter?

There is a window between the moment the server-rendered HTML paints and the moment hydration finishes, during which the page looks completely interactive and is not. A user taps a button in that window and nothing happens — the listener does not exist yet. On a fast connection this is imperceptible; on a mid-range phone on a poor network it can be a second or more, and the user's experience is that your app ignored them, so they tap again, which is how you get duplicate submissions once hydration lands. Event replay, added in v18 and enabled through withEventReplay(), installs a tiny global listener in the server-rendered output that records events during that window and replays them once the relevant component is hydrated. It is a small amount of code for a disproportionate improvement in perceived reliability, and it matters much more with incremental hydration, because now a region can stay unhydrated indefinitely by design — without replay, hydrate on interaction would swallow the very interaction that triggered it, which would be absurd. That is why it is the default posture in modern Angular SSR setups rather than an optimisation you add later.

How do you make SSR performant and safe operationally?

Three areas. Performance: cache aggressively at the edge, because the cheapest server render is the one you do not do — anonymous pages should be cacheable by a CDN with a sensible TTL, and only genuinely personalised routes should hit the origin per request. Prerender everything that does not depend on the request, since build-time rendering is free at runtime. Keep the render fast by avoiding blocking resolvers and by giving every server-side fetch a hard timeout, because a slow upstream turns into held Node processes and a queue that never drains. Safety: never render private data on a route that can be cached, mark authenticated responses private and keep them out of the transfer cache, be extremely careful with anything request-scoped stored outside the request's injector, and set maxResponseBodySize so a runaway render cannot exhaust memory. Operations: SSR means you now run a Node service, so it needs the same treatment as any other — health checks, memory limits, autoscaling on CPU, structured logs correlated with the client's request id, and an alarm on render latency and error rate. And I would insist on a fallback: if the render service is unhealthy, serve the client-side shell rather than an error page, so a degraded first paint is the worst outcome rather than an outage.

SSR, SSG or CSR — how do you decide per route?

I ask two questions per route: does the content depend on the request, and does it need to be visible without JavaScript? If it does not depend on the request, prerender it — that is the landing pages, the docs, the blog, the pricing page, and it is strictly better than SSR because there is no runtime cost and no failure mode. If it depends on the request and needs to be indexable or fast on first paint — a search results page, a product page with live stock, a localised catalogue — render it on the server. If it depends on the request and is private, render it on the client: an account area or a dashboard has no SEO value, and rendering personal data on a server that might sit behind a shared cache is a risk with no upside. Since v20 this is expressed directly in a server routes file, so the decision is visible in one place rather than being a global build flag, and getPrerenderParams lets a parameterised route be prerendered for a known set of values with a server fallback for the rest. The trap to avoid is treating it as a one-time decision — traffic patterns change, and a route that was cheap to render per request at launch can become the reason your Node fleet triples.

What does SSR do to Core Web Vitals, honestly?

It reliably improves First Contentful Paint and usually Largest Contentful Paint, because the browser receives paintable HTML instead of an empty shell and a script tag — on a slow connection that difference is seconds. Cumulative Layout Shift can go either way: SSR helps when the server renders the real content at the right size, and hurts when hydration swaps content or when a mismatch causes a subtree to be destroyed and re-rendered, which is a layout shift with extra steps. Interaction to Next Paint is the honest weak point: classic SSR does not improve it and can make it worse, because the same JavaScript still has to download and execute, and during hydration the main thread is busy — that is precisely the gap event replay papers over and incremental hydration actually closes, by not executing code for regions the user has not reached. So the accurate summary is that SSR buys paint metrics, incremental hydration buys interactivity metrics, and event replay buys perceived reliability in the gap between them. I would also say that the largest Web Vitals wins in most Angular apps are not SSR at all — they are image handling with NgOptimizedImage, font loading strategy, and bundle size, and I would measure before assuming SSR is the lever.

How would you introduce SSR to an existing large client-side app?

Incrementally, and starting with the routes that benefit most. First I would run ng add @angular/ssr and set every route to client render mode, which changes nothing functionally but gets the server build, the deployment and the monitoring in place — that is the risky part and it is better done while the blast radius is zero. Then I would switch the public marketing and content routes to prerender, which is the highest benefit for the least risk since there is no runtime rendering to go wrong. Then, one route at a time, promote the ones with genuine first-paint or SEO value to server rendering, fixing what breaks as it appears: browser globals at construction, non-deterministic rendering, invalid markup, and third-party scripts. I would expect that list to be short but tedious, and I would use it as the argument for a lint rule banning bare window and document access. Private routes stay client-rendered permanently. Throughout I would keep a synthetic check that fetches key URLs with JavaScript disabled and asserts on the content, because that is the only test that catches an SSR regression before users do — and SSR regressions are silent, since the app still works perfectly in the browser.

Interview trap

Optimising change detection when the problem is the network. A very common failure of judgment: the app "feels slow", so the team adds OnPush everywhere and detaches views, and the number does not move — because the real cost was a 3MB bundle on a mobile connection, an unindexed API call taking 800ms, three sequential waterfall requests, or an unoptimised hero image. The answer that scores is a diagnosis order rather than a fix list: measure in the field first, split the timeline into network, parse and execute, first render, and per-interaction cost, and only then pick the tool. Runtime change detection is genuinely the bottleneck in a specific shape of app — large tables, real-time data, heavy forms — and it is nowhere near the top of the list for a normal CRUD application on a slow connection.

Gotcha

Development-mode measurements lie. A dev build runs change detection twice per pass for the NG0100 check, ships unminified code with source maps, does no tree shaking, and serves through Vite's dev pipeline. Profiling it can easily show two to five times the real cost and can point at completely different hot spots. Always profile a production build, served the way production serves it, with the network throttled to something a real user has. The corollary applies to bundle size too: the number that matters is the transferred, compressed size of what is fetched on first load, not the raw output directory — and it is worth checking that your server actually serves Brotli, because that assumption is wrong more often than anyone expects.

Try it — measure before and after, twice

1. Build with --stats-json, open the visualiser, and find the three largest contributors to the initial chunk. Pick one and remove it with @defer or injectAsync. Rebuild and compare transferred size.

2. Open the Coverage panel on a production build of your app's landing route and read the unused-bytes percentage.

3. Record an Angular DevTools profile while typing in your largest form, then add OnPush to the component and record again.

Expected outcome

(1) In most real apps the top three are a charting library, a date or i18n library, and an editor or grid — and one of them is usually loaded on a route that does not use it, because a barrel import pulled it in. (2) Sixty to eighty percent unused on first load is normal and is the concrete number to take to a planning discussion; it converts "we should lazy-load more" from an opinion into a measurement. (3) On a zone-based app the profile before shows the entire component tree being checked on every keystroke, and after shows only the form subtree — often a ten-times reduction in checked views. If the numbers barely move, you have just learned that change detection was not your problem, which is equally valuable.

Interview Q&A

An app feels slow. Walk me through your diagnosis.

First I would find out what "slow" means and to whom — first load on a phone, a specific interaction, a large table, or navigation between routes — because those have almost disjoint causes. Then I would look at field data if there is any, because a laptop on office wifi is not the population. For a first-load complaint I go to the network waterfall and the Coverage panel: transferred bytes, how many round trips before first paint, whether the largest image is optimised and prioritised, and how much shipped JavaScript never runs. For an interaction complaint I record an Angular DevTools profile, which attributes time to components and tells me whether the problem is many cheap checks or one expensive one; many cheap checks means a zone app without OnPush or a high-frequency event source, one expensive one means a specific component's bindings or a large list without virtualisation. For navigation I check whether a resolver is blocking and whether the lazy chunk is being fetched serially after the navigation starts. Only after that do I choose a fix, and I re-measure the same way afterwards. The habit I would emphasise is writing the number down before and after, because "it feels faster" is how teams ship changes that did nothing.

How do you reduce initial bundle size in a real application?

I start with a stats build and a visualiser rather than a checklist, because the wins are always concentrated and always slightly surprising. The usual order of magnitude: route-level lazy loading first, and then verifying it actually happened, because barrel files and root-provided services routinely pull lazy code back into the eager graph. Then heavy leaves — a charting library, a rich text editor, a PDF or spreadsheet exporter, a map — moved behind @defer or, in v22, injectAsync with an idle prefetch, since those are often larger than the feature that hosts them. Then dependency hygiene: per-function imports instead of whole libraries, platform APIs instead of date libraries where possible, and only the locale data you actually ship. Then the framework itself: dropping zone.js is around 30KB gzipped and is free if the app is already signal-based, and removing @angular/animations is roughly 60KB and is required before v23 anyway. Finally I would put budgets in angular.json wired to fail CI, because bundle size regresses one innocent import at a time and no amount of one-off cleanup survives without a ratchet.

What is the single highest-impact change detection fix you have made?

The pattern that has paid off most reliably is removing high-frequency event sources from the zone. In a zone-based app, a drag interaction, a scroll listener, or a canvas overlay fires a change detection pass over the entire component tree per event — I have seen hundreds of full-tree checks per second during a drag, on an app where every individual check was perfectly fast. Moving the handler into runOutsideAngular and re-entering only at the moments state actually changes, typically on drag end, turned a janky interaction into a smooth one without touching any component. The second-most-impactful is track on a large list, because getting it wrong destroys and rebuilds every row on every update, which is invisible in code review and obvious in a profile. The framing I would give in an interview is that both of those are the same insight: the expensive thing is usually the number of times work happens, not the cost of the work. And both become non-issues in a signal-based zoneless app, which is the strongest practical argument for that migration — it deletes the category rather than tuning it.

How do you handle rendering a table with 50,000 rows?

You do not render 50,000 rows. Every row is DOM nodes and component instances and bindings, and no change detection strategy makes 50,000 of anything free. Virtual scrolling is the answer — the CDK's cdk-virtual-scroll-viewport renders only the visible window plus a buffer, so the DOM stays at a few dozen rows regardless of the dataset. With variable row heights you need an autosize strategy or a measured item-size function, and that is where most implementations get janky. Alongside it: paginate or window on the server so you are not transferring 50,000 records either, since a 20MB JSON parse blocks the main thread before any rendering happens; keep the row component small and OnPush with no function calls in bindings, because whatever the row costs is multiplied by the window size and by every scroll frame; and track by a stable id so scrolling reuses nodes instead of rebuilding them. If the product genuinely requires seeing all the data at once — a spreadsheet-like grid — that is the point where I would evaluate a specialised grid component with a canvas or windowed renderer, because reimplementing one well is a multi-month project. And I would push back on the requirement itself: nobody reads 50,000 rows, they search, filter and aggregate, and doing that server-side is usually both faster and better product design.

How do you find and fix a memory leak?

Reproduce it as growth first: navigate into and out of the suspect route ten times with the heap profiler recording, and see whether retained size grows monotonically. Then take a heap snapshot, filter by the component class name, and check the instance count — if ten instances of a component you visited ten times are still alive, they are retained. Then follow the retainer path, which almost always terminates in one of four things: a subscription to a long-lived Subject or router event stream that was never unsubscribed, an event listener added to window or document without removal, a timer or interval that was never cleared, or a reference held in a root-scoped service's cache or array that nothing ever prunes. The fixes are the obvious counterparts, and the systemic fix is to make the leaky patterns unavailable: takeUntilDestroyed or the async pipe instead of manual subscribe, DestroyRef.onDestroy for timers and listeners, and a review rule that any collection in a root service needs a stated eviction policy. In a signal-based codebase the first category mostly disappears, since there is no subscription to leak — which is worth saying, because "we removed a class of bug" is a stronger answer than "we fixed a bug".

What do Angular DevTools give you that Chrome DevTools do not?

Attribution in framework terms. Chrome's performance panel tells you a function took 40ms; Angular DevTools tells you which component was checked, how many times, and why — that mapping is the difference between a five-minute diagnosis and an afternoon of reading flame charts. It also gives you a live component tree with current input values and state, which makes "what is this component actually receiving" a question you look at rather than log; an injector tree that shows the resolution path for any dependency, which turns most NG0201 investigations into a glance; and since v20 a signal graph showing producers and consumers, so "why did this view refresh" becomes visible. Angular 22 pushes this further by registering the dependency and signal graphs as Chrome DevTools extension tools in dev mode, which also makes them accessible to AI debugging assistants. What I still use Chrome for is everything below the framework — long tasks, layout and paint, network, memory, and any third-party script's cost — and the two together are how I actually work: Angular DevTools to find which component, Chrome to find out why that component is expensive.

How do you prevent performance regressions rather than fixing them?

Three ratchets, all in CI. Bundle budgets in angular.json that fail the build, not just warn — with a warning threshold slightly below the error so there is a signal before the wall. Lighthouse or a synthetic performance check on the key routes for every pull request, tracking a small number of metrics rather than an overall score, because the score moves for reasons unrelated to your change. And real-user monitoring in production for the field values of the Core Web Vitals, segmented by device class and connection, because that is the only measurement that reflects your actual users and it is routinely three times worse than what the team sees. On top of the automation, two cultural things matter more than they sound. First, a named owner for the number, because a metric everyone is responsible for is a metric nobody looks at. Second, treating a budget failure as a normal engineering decision rather than a blocker to be bypassed — sometimes the right answer genuinely is to raise the budget, and the value of the ratchet is that it forces the conversation rather than letting the regression land silently.

What is the performance cost of Angular itself, and how do you talk about it?

Honestly, and with the current numbers rather than the 2019 ones. The framework runtime for a modern standalone, zoneless app is much smaller than Angular's reputation suggests — dropping zone.js removed about 30KB gzipped and its startup work, standalone components made the compiler output more tree-shakeable, and removing the animations package saves another 60KB or so. It is still larger than a minimal React or Svelte baseline, and pretending otherwise damages your credibility; what I would say is that the framework floor is a fixed cost measured in tens of kilobytes, while application code, dependencies and images are measured in hundreds, so the framework is rarely the deciding factor past the smallest apps. Where Angular genuinely costs you is startup work on low-end devices — DI resolution, component instantiation and the initial render — which is why incremental hydration and deferring below-the-fold content matter more here than in a lighter framework. And the counterweight I would put on the table is what the size buys: a router, forms, HTTP, i18n, accessibility primitives and testing that would otherwise be dependencies with their own sizes and their own upgrade schedules. Comparing a framework baseline to an unbundled competitor is not a fair comparison, and saying so is part of a good answer.

How do you approach a "the page takes 8 seconds to load" incident?

I would treat it as an incident rather than a performance project: establish scope, find the change, mitigate, then fix properly. Scope means checking whether it is all users or a segment — a region, a device class, one tenant — and whether it started at a deploy, which the RUM data should answer in minutes. If it correlates with a deploy, the fastest mitigation is a rollback, and I would not spend twenty minutes on diagnosis before doing that. If it does not, I split the eight seconds into its parts using a real trace: DNS and TLS, time to first byte, transfer, parse and execute, first paint, and then the API calls the app makes before it becomes usable. In practice the answer is usually one of a small set — a new dependency added to the initial bundle, an API endpoint that lost an index and is now taking seconds, a third-party script added by marketing through a tag manager, a CDN misconfiguration serving uncompressed assets, or an SSR render blocked on a slow upstream with no timeout. The lesson I would draw out afterwards is prevention: a bundle budget in CI, a timeout on every server-side fetch, and third-party scripts loaded with an explicit performance budget of their own rather than injected by whoever has access to the tag manager.

Is OnPush everywhere always the right default?

As of v22 the question mostly answers itself, since OnPush is the default for new components and the old strategy is deprecated and renamed Eager. But the reasoning still matters, because you have to decide what to do with the annotations the migration stamped onto an existing codebase. Historically the objection to OnPush everywhere was that it required a discipline the whole team had to share: state must be immutable, mutating an input object without changing its reference silently fails to update the child, and a component that gets its data from a source Angular cannot see needs an explicit markForCheck. In practice a partially-adopted OnPush codebase was worse than either extreme, because the failure was silent and intermittent. Signals remove the objection: a view that reads a changed signal refreshes regardless of strategy, so the main failure mode is gone, and that is exactly why the team felt able to flip the default. So my answer is yes, with the caveat that it should arrive together with signals rather than before them — turning on OnPush across a codebase that still mutates objects and assigns plain fields from subscriptions is how you generate a week of "the UI is stale" bug reports.

Interview trap

Testing implementation instead of behaviour. The tell is a test that asserts on a CSS class, a private method, or the internal shape of a component's state — those break on every refactor and pass while the feature is broken, which is the worst of both worlds. The discipline that fixes it: query the DOM the way a user or assistive technology would, by accessible role and label, not by .btn-primary; assert on rendered output and emitted events rather than on internal fields; and when mocking, mock at the boundary of the system under test rather than mocking the class you are testing. The Angular-specific version of the trap is over-mocking with TestBed: a test that provides fakes for every dependency verifies that your fakes work together, not that your code does. Component test harnesses (from the CDK) are the framework's answer for shared components — they give you a stable, behaviour-level API so tests do not couple to markup.

Gotcha

Assigning to a signal input in a test silently does nothing. component.ticket = someTicket compiles if the type lines up loosely, and then the signal still holds its previous value, so the assertion fails with a message that makes no sense. The correct call is fixture.componentRef.setInput('ticket', someTicket), which goes through the input machinery and marks the view for check. The sibling gotcha in zoneless tests: a test that used to pass because fixture.detectChanges() happened to flush a promise now needs await fixture.whenStable(), because there is no zone tracking the microtask. Both of these produce failures that look like the framework is broken, and both are one-line fixes once you know.

(2) The assignment version fails with the input's default value still in place — the clearest possible demonstration that a signal input is not a property. (3) The cancellation test is the interesting one: flush the responses out of order and assert the view shows the second response, which is the behaviour a hand-rolled loader would get wrong and the Resource API gets right.

Interview Q&A

What changed in Angular testing recently, and how would you migrate a large suite?

Karma was deprecated and Vitest became the default test runner in Angular 21, stable and production-ready, with jsdom by default and an optional Playwright provider for real browsers. Angular 22 added polish around it: a migrate-karma-to-vitest schematic, an improved refactor-jasmine-vitest codemod with a --fake-async flag, --isolate and --quiet flags on the test builder, and a zone.js Vitest patch so fakeAsync, flush and waitForAsync still work for suites that depend on them. For a large suite I would migrate in that order: run the runner migration first so the suite executes under Vitest with the zone patch, and change nothing about the tests. That alone gets most of the speed benefit and is a reviewable, revertible change. Then run the Jasmine-to-Vitest codemod on the spies and matchers, which is mechanical, and fix the residue by hand. Then, over time, replace fakeAsync-based tests with await fixture.whenStable() as those files are touched, since that is the zoneless-compatible idiom. I would not attempt all three in one pull request on a suite of any size, because when a thousand tests break at once nobody can tell which change caused it.

How do you test a component that uses signals?

Mostly by reading them, which is the point — a signal has no subscription and no async ceremony, so a service's state can be asserted synchronously right after the method that changed it. For a component, the two Angular-specific mechanics are setting inputs and flushing effects. Signal inputs must be set through fixture.componentRef.setInput(name, value); assigning to the property does not go through the input machinery and leaves the signal untouched, which produces a very confusing failure. Effects run as part of change detection rather than immediately, so a test that asserts on an effect's side effect needs TestBed.flushEffects() or an await fixture.whenStable() first. Beyond that I would keep most logic out of the component entirely: a store or service holding the signals is testable with no TestBed at all beyond TestBed.inject, which is faster and expresses the rules more directly, and the component test is then only about wiring — the input renders, the click calls the method, the error branch appears. In a zoneless test I lean on whenStable rather than fakeAsync, because there is no zone to advance.

What is your testing pyramid for a large Angular app, and why?

A broad base of plain unit tests over services, stores, validators and pure functions — fast, deterministic, and where the business rules actually live. A substantial middle layer of component tests through TestBed, covering the wiring that unit tests cannot see: inputs rendering, events emitting, conditional branches, form controls actually bound, error states displayed. Then a deliberately small set of end-to-end tests over the critical journeys — sign in, the primary task, checkout or submit — run against a real build. The ratio matters less than the reasoning: each level up is slower, flakier and more expensive to diagnose, so a test belongs at the lowest level that can express it. The two failure modes I watch for are an inverted pyramid, where a team has hundreds of Playwright tests and a two-hour pipeline that everyone reruns until it goes green, and a hollow middle, where there is good unit coverage and good E2E coverage and nothing testing the wiring — which is exactly where Angular defects live, because the framework is doing the wiring. I would also add a small number of contract tests against the backend, because a mocked HTTP layer will happily let you ship against an endpoint that changed shape last week.

How much should you mock in an Angular test?

As little as the test's boundary allows. If the unit under test is a service that calls an API, mock the HTTP layer with provideHttpClientTesting and let the interceptors, the serialisation and the error mapping run for real — those are exactly where the bugs are, and mocking the service instead of the transport tests nothing. If the unit is a component, provide real collaborators where they are cheap and pure, and fake only the ones that reach out of the process. The anti-pattern is a test that provides a stub for every constructor dependency: it passes when the code is broken because the stubs agree with the code's assumptions rather than with reality, and it has to be rewritten every time a dependency changes. Angular's DI makes over-mocking unusually easy, which is why I treat a long providers array in a test as a smell — usually it means the component knows about too many things and the fix is in the production code, not the test. For shared components I would push teams toward CDK component harnesses, which give a behaviour-level API so a consuming test can drive a date picker without knowing its markup and without stubbing it out entirely.

How do you test routing, guards and interceptors?

Functional guards and interceptors are the easy case now that they are plain functions: call them inside TestBed.runInInjectionContext with fake dependencies provided, and assert on the returned value or the modified request. That is a fast unit test and covers most of the logic. What it does not cover is composition — the order interceptors run in, whether the guard actually gates the route, whether a redirect lands where you think — so I add integration tests using RouterTestingHarness, which navigates for real against a route config with stubbed components and lets you assert on the resulting URL and rendered component. That level catches the ordering mistakes, which are the ones that reach production: a logging interceptor registered before auth logs unauthenticated requests, a canMatch guard that lets the wrong variant through. For resolvers I test the function directly and then assert through the harness that navigation actually blocks and then completes. What I would not do is test the router itself — asserting that navigate changes the URL is testing Angular, not your app.

Your E2E suite is flaky. What do you do?

I treat flakiness as a defect with an owner, not as weather, because the moment a team learns to rerun the pipeline, the suite has stopped providing information and is purely a tax. Practically: quarantine the flaky tests immediately so the pipeline is trustworthy again, then fix them with a deadline rather than leaving them quarantined forever. The causes are a short list. Timing assumptions — fixed sleeps instead of waiting for a condition — which modern Playwright's auto-waiting mostly removes if you stop writing explicit waits. Shared state between tests, so order matters and parallelism breaks them; the fix is that every test creates its own data and cleans up, or runs against a seeded, isolated tenant. Real network dependencies, where a third-party sandbox or a slow staging API introduces randomness; stub at the network boundary for everything that is not the thing under test. Animations and transitions, which are a classic source of "the element was detached" errors and should be disabled in the test environment. And selectors coupled to markup, which break intermittently as components re-render; role and label based queries or test ids are far more stable. Underneath all of it, a smaller E2E suite is a more reliable one — most flaky tests are testing something that belonged one level down.

How do you set and enforce a coverage target?

Carefully, because coverage is a good diagnostic and a bad target — the moment a percentage is mandated, people write tests that execute code without asserting anything, and you have paid for maintenance with no defect reduction. What I would do instead is use coverage differentially: require that new and changed lines are covered, which is enforceable per pull request and is a reasonable ask, rather than dragging a legacy codebase to an arbitrary global number. I would also look at what is uncovered rather than at the number — uncovered error paths and edge branches are worth far more than another test on a getter, and a coverage report read as a map is genuinely useful. Where I would set a hard floor is on the code that would hurt most if it broke: payment, permissions, data migration, anything with legal or financial consequence. And I would pair coverage with a mutation-testing spot check occasionally, because that is the honest measure of whether the assertions mean anything — a suite with ninety percent coverage and a low mutation score is a suite that runs the code without checking it. In an interview I would be explicit that I have seen teams hit ninety percent coverage and ship the same number of defects, and that the conversation should be about which risks are covered, not which percentage is reached.

How do you test accessibility?

In three layers, because automation catches perhaps a third of real issues. Automated: an axe-core check in component and E2E tests, which reliably catches missing labels, insufficient contrast, invalid ARIA, duplicate ids and missing landmarks. That is cheap and should gate the pipeline. Structural: write tests that query by accessible role and name rather than by CSS selector, which turns every component test into an implicit accessibility assertion — if the test cannot find the button by its accessible name, neither can a screen reader, and this single habit change catches more than a dedicated audit does. Manual: keyboard-only walkthroughs of the critical journeys, and a screen reader pass on anything custom, because focus order, focus trapping in dialogs, live-region announcements and the coherence of the reading experience are not automatable. Angular helps more than it used to — @angular/aria went stable in v22 with unstyled, accessible primitives for the patterns people historically got wrong, like comboboxes, dialogs, grids and tree views, and building on those rather than on a div with a click handler is the highest-leverage decision available. I would also make sure route changes move focus and announce the new page, since that is the single most common single-page-app accessibility failure and it never shows up in a component test.

What makes an Angular test suite slow, and how do you fix it?

Historically, launching a real browser per run and rebuilding the world on every change — which is precisely what moving to Vitest fixes, and why suites typically get several times faster with no test changes at all. After that, the dominant cost is TestBed compilation: every configureTestingModule plus createComponent compiles components, and a suite where every test is a component test pays that thousands of times. The fix is structural rather than clever — move logic out of components into services so most tests need no TestBed, and share module configuration in beforeEach rather than reconfiguring per test. Then look for tests that do real waiting: fixed sleeps, real timers, or unnecessary whenStable loops; fake timers and condition-based waiting remove them. Vitest's parallel workers help a lot but expose shared state, so tests that pass serially and fail in parallel are a signal that something global is being mutated — which is worth fixing regardless. And in v22 the --isolate flag lets you trade isolation for speed deliberately rather than accidentally. The target I would set is that the unit and component suite runs in under a couple of minutes locally, because past that people stop running it before pushing and the feedback loop is gone.

Would you use component harnesses, and why?

Yes, for anything shared across teams, and I would treat shipping a harness as part of the definition of done for a design-system component. A harness is a class exposing a component's behaviour as an API — await menu.open(), await select.chooseOption('Berlin') — so a consuming test never touches the component's markup. The value is asymmetric: without them, every team that uses your date picker writes selectors against its internal DOM, and the next time you refactor it you break a hundred test files you do not own, which in practice means you stop refactoring it. With them, the markup is free to change as long as the behaviour holds. They also work in both unit tests and Playwright, so the same interaction vocabulary is used at both levels, and they encourage behaviour-level assertions rather than markup assertions. The cost is real — a harness is code to write and maintain — so I would not write one for a component used in one place. The rule I would set is: if a component is published in a shared library, it ships with a harness; if it lives in one feature folder, it does not need one.

Interview trap

Treating a route guard as a security control. Guards decide what the UI offers; they do not decide what the user can do. Anyone can open DevTools, call the API directly, or simply request the URL with a token — the client is fully under the user's control. The same applies to hiding buttons by permission, to filtering a list client-side, and to any "admin only" lazy chunk, which is downloadable by anyone who knows its filename. The correct framing is that the client's job is to avoid showing people things that will fail, and the server's job is to make them fail. Where the client does add real security value is in reducing information disclosure — a canMatch guard that prevents an admin bundle from being downloaded at all does not stop a determined attacker, but it does keep your internal admin UI's structure out of casual reach.

Gotcha

Micro-frontends usually cost more than they solve. Module Federation and Angular Elements make it technically possible to compose independently deployed Angular applications, and there are real cases for it — separate business units, wildly different release cadences, an incremental migration away from a legacy stack. But the costs are systematically underestimated: two or more copies of Angular unless you get shared-dependency versioning exactly right, and getting it wrong produces bizarre DI failures because the same class exists twice; cross-app routing, auth and design-token propagation that all have to be invented; a debugging story that spans repositories; and a coupled upgrade path anyway, because a shared Angular version means everyone upgrades together. In my experience most teams reaching for micro-frontends actually want independent deployability of features, which a well-bounded monorepo with lazy routes and enforced module boundaries delivers with a fraction of the complexity.

Try it — make the architecture enforceable

1. Add ESLint no-restricted-imports (or Nx module-boundary tags) so a ui library cannot import from data-access, and two feature libraries cannot import each other. Run it against the existing codebase.

2. Replace one hand-rolled component — a combobox, a tabs widget or a dialog — with the @angular/aria primitive plus your own styling, and test it with keyboard only.

3. Add a bundle budget and an axe check to CI, and see whether the pipeline is currently green.

Expected outcome

(1) The first run almost always produces a page of violations, and reading them is the fastest architecture review available — the cross-feature imports in particular map exactly onto the parts of the codebase that are hardest to change. Fix them incrementally with a warning level first, then error. (2) The hand-rolled widget will be missing something: type-ahead, Home and End keys, focus return on close, aria-activedescendant, or escape handling. Finding out which is a good demonstration of why headless primitives are worth adopting. (3) If the pipeline goes red, you have just converted invisible debt into a tracked, prioritisable list — which is the actual point of both ratchets.

Interview Q&A

How would you structure a large Angular codebase for twenty engineers?

A monorepo with library boundaries, and lint rules that enforce them. Concretely: thin applications that contain almost nothing but routes and configuration, and libraries organised by domain first and by type second — for each feature area, a feature library holding smart components and routes, a data-access library holding the services and resources, a ui library of presentational components, and a domain library of types and pure business rules that imports nothing from Angular. The rules matter more than the layout: features may not import other features, UI may not inject data services, and domain has no framework dependency. Those three constraints are what keep the dependency graph acyclic and keep testing cheap, and they must be enforced by tooling rather than documented, because otherwise they last about two quarters. Lazy-loading boundaries follow feature libraries, which gives code splitting for free from the same decomposition. I would also insist on ownership metadata — a CODEOWNERS file mapping libraries to teams — because at twenty engineers the real bottleneck is review latency and unclear ownership, not code organisation.

Do you need a state management library?

Usually not, and I would want to hear which problem it is being brought in to solve before agreeing. The four candidate problems are shared access across components, complex state transitions, debuggability, and consistency across many teams. Signals in a service solve the first completely: private writable signals, public readonly or computed projections, and methods as the only mutation path give you an encapsulated store with no dependency and no boilerplate. If the state machine is genuinely complex — many transitions with rules about which are legal — then an explicit store buys real safety, and NgRx SignalStore is the natural choice because it is signals underneath so the migration is mechanical and the mental model does not change. Full Redux-style NgRx earns its boilerplate when you need an event log, time-travel debugging, or coordinated effects across features, which is real in some domains and imaginary in most. The failure I have seen most is a CRUD application with a hundred action-reducer-effect triples where the actual requirement was "two components need the same list", and the second failure is the opposite — a signal service that has grown to forty fields with no transition rules, which is a store with the safety removed. I would also point out that a lot of what teams call state is really cached server data, and httpResource handles that better than any store.

How do you build and govern a design system in Angular?

Three layers, versioned and released like a product. The bottom is behaviour and accessibility — headless directives providing keyboard interaction, focus management and ARIA wiring, which since v22 is largely @angular/aria in the box rather than something to build. The middle is styled components composing those via hostDirectives, with design tokens exposed as CSS custom properties so a theme is configuration rather than a fork. The top is patterns: a field wrapper owning label, hint and error placement, a page layout, a data table shell. Governance matters as much as code. I would ship every component with a harness so consumer tests do not couple to markup, and treat a breaking change to a harness as a breaking change to the library. I would publish with semver and provide migration schematics for our own breaking changes, so consuming teams run a command rather than read a changelog. I would keep a visual regression suite, because CSS regressions are invisible in unit tests. And I would resist the pull toward a component for every design, since a library of two hundred components nobody can find is worse than fifty well-documented ones — the real deliverable is documentation and examples, and adoption is a support problem long before it is an engineering one.

Micro-frontends with Angular: when, and how?

Rarely, and only for an organisational problem rather than a technical one. The legitimate cases are separate business units with genuinely independent roadmaps, a gradual migration where a legacy application must be replaced page by page, or a platform hosting third-party-authored extensions. If the goal is just "teams want to deploy independently", a monorepo with enforced boundaries and lazy routes gives most of that with far less machinery. When it is justified, the two mechanisms are Module Federation, which shares a runtime and lets remotes be loaded as modules, and Angular Elements or web components, which gives stronger isolation at the cost of a clumsier interface. The problems to plan for explicitly are shared dependency versioning — two copies of Angular loaded at once produces DI failures that look like the framework is broken, because the same class genuinely exists twice; cross-app concerns like routing, authentication, design tokens and error reporting, all of which have to be designed rather than inherited; and the uncomfortable truth that a shared Angular version means everyone upgrades together, which removes much of the independence that motivated the split. I would insist on a written contract for the host-remote interface and a shell that degrades gracefully when a remote fails to load, because a remote that 404s after a deploy should not take down the page.

Where does XSS come from in an Angular app?

Almost always from deliberately turning off the protection. Angular escapes interpolation and property bindings contextually by default, and it sanitises [innerHTML], so the framework's baseline is genuinely good. The vulnerabilities come from DomSanitizer.bypassSecurityTrustHtml and its siblings, which exist for legitimate reasons — embedding a sanitised rich-text document, an SVG, a trusted iframe URL — and which are used casually to make a warning go away. Any bypass on user-controlled content is a vulnerability. The second source is bypassing Angular entirely: ElementRef.nativeElement.innerHTML =, a third-party library that injects markup, or a directive writing to the DOM directly. The third is URLs — binding user input to an href or src without validating the scheme allows javascript: URLs, and Angular's sanitiser catches the common cases but not every construction. The controls I would put in place: a lint rule flagging every bypassSecurityTrust call so each one requires a review comment explaining why, sanitising rich text server-side with a well-maintained library rather than trusting the client, a strict Content Security Policy with nonces so an injected script cannot execute even if markup slips through, and Trusted Types where the browser support allows. Angular 22 also tightened binding sanitisation and added SSRF protections on the server side, which is worth knowing if the app renders on a server.

How do you handle authentication and authorisation in a large Angular app?

Authentication I would push to a standards-based flow rather than build: OpenID Connect with authorisation code plus PKCE against an identity provider, using a certified library rather than hand-rolling token handling. Token storage is the decision that matters most: localStorage is convenient and means any XSS is a full account takeover, so httpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookies with a backend-for-frontend are the stronger posture wherever the architecture allows, and where it does not, in-memory tokens with a silent refresh are better than persistent storage. The client-side plumbing is then a functional interceptor that attaches the token, refreshes once on a 401 and replays the request, and de-duplicates concurrent refreshes so ten parallel requests do not trigger ten refreshes. Authorisation on the client is presentation only: guards and permission-based rendering exist so users are not shown things that will fail, and canMatch is worth using for role-specific areas because it avoids downloading the chunk at all. The enforcement is entirely server-side, and I would say that plainly, because the most common architectural mistake I see is a team that has genuinely convinced itself the guard is the control. I would also make sure permissions come from the token or a single endpoint rather than being inferred in several places, because permission logic duplicated across components drifts and produces UI that disagrees with itself.

Built-in i18n or a runtime translation library?

It comes down to one question: does a user switch language during a session? Angular's built-in @angular/localize extracts messages at build time and produces a separate bundle per locale, which is the fastest possible runtime — no lookups, no dictionaries shipped, dead translations eliminated — and it gives you locale-specific URLs, which is what you want for SEO. The cost is that switching locale means loading a different build, so it is a page reload and a different URL. A runtime library like Transloco loads JSON dictionaries and resolves at runtime, so switching is instant and adding a locale needs no rebuild, at the cost of a lookup per binding and shipping the dictionaries. So: a marketing or content site with a handful of locales and search traffic per language should use the built-in approach; an internal application where a user toggles between two languages, or a product where locales are added by operations without a deploy, should use a runtime library. Whichever is chosen, the parts people underestimate are the same — pluralisation and gender rules, which need ICU message format rather than string concatenation; date, number and currency formatting, which should come from Intl and the locale rather than being hand-rolled; right-to-left layout, which is a CSS and design problem not a translation one; and the translator workflow, which is a real operational pipeline and is where these projects actually stall.

How do you keep a large Angular estate upgradeable?

By making upgrades routine rather than projects. Concretely: never fall more than one major behind, because ng update hops one major at a time and being two behind more than doubles the work; adopt at the first minor rather than on release day so the ecosystem catches up; and schedule the upgrade as normal work in the sprint after that minor lands, with a named owner. The yearly cadence starting after v22 makes this much easier to plan — one major each June with 24 months of support means being one behind is now a supported steady state. Beyond cadence, three things determine how painful each upgrade is. Dependency discipline: every third-party Angular library is a potential blocker, so I would keep the count deliberately low, prefer libraries with a track record of same-week major support, and treat an unmaintained dependency as a risk item with a replacement plan rather than a thing we live with. Deprecation tracking: the deprecations page is a planning input, and work like removing @angular/animations before v23 belongs in a roadmap, not in the upgrade pull request. And test coverage at the wiring level, because that is what an upgrade breaks — a suite that only tests pure functions will pass happily through a broken migration.

How would you introduce a significant change — signals, zoneless, a design system — to a skeptical team?

I would start by not pitching the technology. The first move is to find the pain the change addresses in their terms — a class of bug in the tracker, a recurring review argument, a slow pipeline, an incident — and confirm it is real, because if it is not, the skepticism is correct and I should drop it. Then I would do a bounded, reversible pilot on one feature, done by me or with the most skeptical person on the team, and bring back specifics: lines of code, defects avoided, the measurement before and after. Specifics move people; principles do not. Then I would make the new way the easy way — a schematic that scaffolds it, a lint rule for new code only, a documented example in the codebase rather than in a wiki — and explicitly say that existing code is not being rewritten, because the fear behind most resistance is a forced migration with no time allocated. And I would name the costs honestly, including the ones that argue against me: signals mean a new mental model and a period of mixed idioms, zoneless breaks third-party integrations, a design system slows the first three features it touches. A lead who only lists benefits gets discounted; a lead who names the costs and still recommends it gets trusted. Finally I would give it a review date rather than declaring victory, so the team knows the decision is reversible.

What are the AI-related additions to Angular, and do they matter?

There are three, and I would be careful to separate the ones that affect production code from the ones that affect the development loop. The Angular MCP server, introduced in v21 and expanded since, exposes tools that let an AI assistant query Angular documentation, look up best practices, run migrations such as the zoneless one, and generate code using current idioms — that is a development-time productivity feature, and its real value is that it stops assistants generating 2019-era Angular, which is a genuine problem given how much has changed. Angular Skills in v22, installed via a skills command, are the same idea packaged as portable guidance for coding agents. Those two are useful and low-risk. The third is different: experimental WebMCP support, where your application can declare typed tools that AI agents running in the browser can call, and where Signal Forms can automatically register themselves as tools with a schema derived from their fields. That is genuinely novel — it makes your app callable by an agent rather than just usable by a person — and it is explicitly experimental, so I would put it in a prototype and not in a production checkout flow. The thing I would raise in an architecture discussion is the security model: exposing application capabilities to an in-browser agent is an authorisation surface, and it needs the same scrutiny as a public API rather than being treated as a UI feature.

14The big Q&A

Everything that does not belong to a single building block: the ecosystem decisions, the internals questions, the operational reality, and the scenario questions that are really about judgment. Twenty-two of them, grouped.

Architecture and ecosystem decisions

Angular or React for a new product — how do you actually decide?

I would decide on four axes and be explicit that none of them is "which is better". Team and hiring market: if the organisation is React-heavy or hiring in a React-heavy market, the coordination cost of Angular is real and often decisive. Lifespan and team count: Angular's opinionated, versioned, all-in-one nature pays off over years and across many contributors, because the architecture questions are pre-answered and upgrades are one coordinated event with codemods rather than twelve independent ones. Product shape: heavy forms, data grids, complex permissions, strict accessibility and internationalisation requirements are where Angular's included batteries genuinely save months; a content site or a small interactive tool is not. And constraints: a strict bundle budget or an embedded target favours something lighter. What I would avoid is the 2019 argument — since standalone components, signals and zoneless landed, the day-to-day authoring experience converged a lot, and reciting old objections signals you have not looked recently. I would also say that either choice is recoverable and the bigger risk is inconsistency: two frameworks in one product costs more than picking the second-best one.

You inherit an Angular 12 app with no tests, 300 components and one original author left. What do you do in the first 90 days?

First two weeks: understand and stabilise, change nothing structural. Get it building reproducibly, get a staging environment, add error tracking and basic real-user monitoring if they are missing, and read the incident history — the fastest way to learn where the bodies are is to see what has broken. I would also write down the domain model with the remaining author while they are still there, because that knowledge is the asset that disappears. Weeks three to six: get onto a supported version, purely mechanically, one major per pull request with no refactoring, because being on an unsupported version is a security and hiring liability and because the schematics do most of the work. In parallel, add characterisation tests around the highest-risk flows — not for coverage, but so the upgrade and any later refactor have a safety net. Weeks seven to twelve: pick the two or three things that hurt most in daily work — usually build time, a leaky abstraction everyone works around, or a deployment process — and fix those, while establishing the conventions for new code: standalone, signals, control flow, lint rules, budgets. What I would not do is start a rewrite or a modernisation sprint. The goal at 90 days is that the team can ship safely and the codebase is on a supported version, not that it is beautiful.

How do you evaluate whether to add a third-party Angular library?

I would treat it as taking on a maintenance obligation, not as saving time. The questions in order: does it support the current Angular major, and how fast did it support the previous two — a library that lags by six months will eventually block our upgrade, which is the single most common way an Angular estate gets stuck. Who maintains it, and what happens if they stop; bus factor of one is a risk I want named. How large is it, and can it be tree-shaken or lazily loaded. Does it hold state or reach into the DOM in ways that will break under SSR or zoneless. How replaceable is it — a library behind our own thin wrapper can be swapped in a week, one whose types leak through the whole codebase cannot. And what does it actually save; a lot of dependencies replace two hundred lines we could own and understand. For anything that becomes structural — a component library, a state library, a charting engine — I would want a written decision record and a rough exit plan, because the expensive dependencies are the ones nobody chose deliberately.

When would you use Angular Elements or custom elements?

When Angular components need to run somewhere Angular does not own the page. The concrete cases are embedding a widget into a CMS-rendered or server-rendered page owned by another team, shipping a component to consumers using different frameworks, and incrementally replacing parts of a legacy application without a big-bang rewrite. createCustomElement wraps an Angular component as a standard custom element with attributes mapped to inputs and DOM events to outputs, so the host page needs to know nothing about Angular. The costs are real and worth naming: you ship the Angular runtime with the element, so several independent elements on one page can mean several copies unless they share a bundle; inputs arrive as strings from attributes unless you set properties, which surprises people; content projection and slots behave differently from what Angular developers expect; and styles need Shadow DOM to be safe, which then makes theming from the host page a deliberate design rather than a default. I would use it for genuine cross-boundary embedding and not as an internal architecture, because inside an Angular app, components are strictly better than custom elements in every way.

Framework internals

What does the Angular compiler actually do?

Angular is an ahead-of-time compiled framework, and understanding that explains most of its behaviour. The compiler reads your decorators and templates at build time and generates code: a component becomes a class with a static definition holding a template function, which is split into a create pass that builds DOM once and an update pass that evaluates bindings and writes only what changed. Templates are parsed into an AST, type-checked against your class — that is what strictTemplates configures — and lowered into instruction calls rather than being interpreted at runtime. This is why the template can only reference what the compiler can see, why a missing imports entry produces an unmatched element rather than a runtime lookup failure, and why control flow was moved into the compiler where it could be optimised. The Ivy renderer, which became the default in v9, made these definitions locality-independent — each component compiles on its own without needing to know about a module — and that is the technical foundation that standalone components, better tree shaking and readable build output were all built on. The current work in this area is prototyping the Go-based TypeScript compiler for a five-to-ten-times speedup, which is the next thing to watch.

Why is a signal a function call rather than a property?

Because reading has to be observable. The entire model depends on the framework knowing, at the moment of a read, which consumer is currently executing so it can record the dependency edge — and a plain property read cannot do that without a proxy. Angular deliberately avoided proxies: they add per-access overhead, they behave surprisingly around identity and built-in types, and they make the reactive boundary invisible in the source, so you cannot tell by looking whether an expression subscribes to something. Making the read an explicit call makes the reactive graph legible — count() in a template or a computed is visibly a tracked read, and the same expression outside a reactive context is visibly just a read. It also keeps the mechanism simple enough to reason about and to debug, which matters when you are looking at a signal graph in DevTools trying to work out why a view refreshed. The cost is ergonomic: it is one more pair of parentheses, and forgetting them in a template gives you a truthy function object rather than an error, which is the single most common signals mistake. That trade — a small, memorable footgun in exchange for a transparent and cheap mechanism — is a reasonable one to defend in an interview.

What is the difference between the element injector and the environment injector, and when does it matter?

Element injectors are created by providers on components and directives and form a tree mirroring the view hierarchy; environment injectors are created at bootstrap, at lazy-loaded boundaries, and by route-level providers, and form a separate tree. A resolution walks the element chain from the requesting node upward, and only when that is exhausted continues into the environment chain to the root and finally the platform. It matters in three practical situations. First, a component provider gives its whole view subtree a private instance, which is how a form group or a grid selection model is scoped correctly, and no environment provider can express that lifetime. Second, route-level providers live in the environment tree and are created and destroyed with the route, which is the correct lifetime for feature state and is why feature state in a root service goes stale. Third, when debugging a wrong instance or a missing provider — knowing which tree the token is in tells you where to look, and Angular DevTools' injector view shows the actual path. The subtlety that catches people is viewProviders, which restricts visibility to the component's own view and hides the instance from projected content, and directives such as host: true or skipSelf that deliberately truncate the walk.

How does Angular's tree shaking work, and what defeats it?

Tree shaking depends on static analysability: the bundler must be able to prove a symbol is never reachable. Angular's design supports it deliberately — providedIn: 'root' means a service is only included if something injects it, standalone components declare their dependencies locally so unused ones drop out, and Ivy's per-component compilation means there is no module-level registry forcing everything to be retained. What defeats it, in rough order of how often I see it: barrel files, where importing one symbol from an index that re-exports fifty pulls in the whole graph and, worse, can merge what should be separate lazy chunks; side effects at module scope, since a module that does work on import cannot be removed and a missing or wrong sideEffects field in a dependency's package makes the bundler conservative; dynamic access patterns like a registry keyed by string, where the bundler cannot know which entries are reachable; and eagerly providing something in root that is only used by a lazy feature, which pulls its whole dependency graph into the initial bundle. The practical answer is that you do not reason about this, you measure it — a stats build and a visualiser, checked when the bundle moves, because tree shaking failures are invisible in source review and obvious in a chunk graph.

Security and correctness

A code review has bypassSecurityTrustHtml in it. What do you say?

My first question is where the content comes from, because that single answer decides everything. If it is a constant in the codebase or something generated by our own build, the bypass is defensible and I would want a comment saying so. If it is user-generated, or comes from an API that carries user-generated content, or comes from a CMS that editors can write HTML into, then it is a vulnerability regardless of how trusted the source feels — CMS content is user-generated content with a nicer name, and "our editors would not do that" is not a security control. The alternatives I would push for, in order: do not render HTML at all, and use a structured format the component renders itself; sanitise server-side with a well-maintained allowlist library and keep the client's sanitiser on; or if the bypass is genuinely necessary, isolate it — a sandboxed iframe with a restrictive policy for genuinely untrusted rich content. Beyond the individual review, I would want a lint rule that makes every bypassSecurityTrust call require an explicit suppression comment, so each one is a deliberate, greppable decision rather than something that spreads by copy-paste, and a strict Content Security Policy so that even a mistake here does not become script execution.

Your app stores the JWT in localStorage. Is that a problem?

It is a risk amplifier rather than a vulnerability on its own. Nothing but your own JavaScript can read localStorage, so the exposure is conditional on an XSS — but if you do get an XSS, storing the token there turns a scripting bug into full account takeover with a token the attacker can exfiltrate and use from anywhere, potentially for its whole lifetime. The stronger posture is an httpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookie, ideally with a backend-for-frontend that holds the real token and issues a session cookie, because then a script cannot read it at all; the trade is that you now have to think about CSRF, which is what the XSRF-TOKEN convention and SameSite are for, and cross-origin API calls get more complicated. Where cookies genuinely do not fit, in-memory tokens with a silent refresh are the middle ground — an XSS can still steal the current token, but it does not persist across tabs or reloads. Whatever the storage, the mitigations that matter are the same: short access-token lifetimes, refresh-token rotation with reuse detection, a strict CSP, and never putting anything sensitive beyond the token itself in client storage. In an interview I would be careful not to answer with a flat "localStorage is insecure" — the nuanced version, with the threat model spelled out, is a much better answer.

How do you prevent one tenant's data reaching another in a multi-tenant Angular app?

On the client, the honest answer is that you cannot prevent it — you can only avoid causing it. The enforcement is entirely server-side: every query scoped by tenant, derived from the authenticated principal rather than from anything the client sends, and tested. What the client can do is avoid creating the conditions. The specific Angular hazards are caching and SSR. Any client-side cache — an interceptor cache, a service holding a list, a service worker — must have the tenant in the key, or must be cleared on tenant switch; a cache keyed only by URL is a cross-tenant leak the moment a user has access to two tenants. Under SSR the risk is sharper: root-provided services are per-request, but anything held in module scope, in a static field, or in the platform injector is shared across every request the process serves, so caching request data there means one user's render can carry another's data. That is also why v22 excluding credentialed requests from the transfer cache by default matters — an authenticated response embedded in HTML that then sits in a shared CDN cache is exactly this bug. Operationally I would test it deliberately: an automated check that logs in as two tenants in sequence and asserts no data crosses, plus load tests against the SSR service, because these failures are concurrency-dependent and will not appear in a single-request test.

Performance, debugging and operations

Users on mid-range Android report the app is unusable. Your laptop is fine. What now?

Believe the users and change how I measure. My laptop has ten times the CPU, a fast network and a warm cache, so a local profile tells me almost nothing about that population. I would get field data segmented by device class and connection — real-user monitoring for the Web Vitals, not a Lighthouse score — and then reproduce with CPU throttling at four to six times and a slow network profile, which usually makes the problem obvious immediately. The causes cluster: JavaScript parse and execute time, which is roughly linear in bundle size and brutal on a weak CPU, so this is where lazy loading, defer and dropping zone.js pay off most; long tasks blocking the main thread, often from a large list rendering or a big JSON parse; unoptimised images, which dominate both bytes and decode time; and layout thrashing from measuring the DOM in a loop. The fixes are the ones from the performance unit, but the framing I would give is that low-end mobile is not a special case to optimise for at the end — it is the honest test of the app, and a team that profiles on it routinely does not ship this problem in the first place. I would also check whether the affected users are on a particular route or feature, because a global complaint often has a specific cause.

How do you monitor an Angular app in production?

Four streams, each answering a different question. Errors: a global ErrorHandler reporting to a tracker with source maps uploaded so stack traces are readable, release tagging so a spike maps to a deploy, and — importantly — filtering the noise from browser extensions and bot traffic, because a tracker nobody trusts gets ignored. Real-user performance: the Core Web Vitals from the field, segmented by route, device class and connection, because that is the only number that reflects users rather than the team. Business and interaction telemetry: page views keyed by the normalised route rather than the raw URL so parameterised routes aggregate, plus the handful of funnel events that tell you whether the product works. And correlation: a request id generated on the client and sent as a header, so a client-side error can be traced to the server-side logs of the request that caused it — which is the single highest-value piece of plumbing and the one most often missing. Two Angular-specific additions: capture the app version and the chunk manifest version in error reports, because chunk-load failures after a deploy are one of the most common SPA incidents and are otherwise indistinguishable from network noise; and if SSR is in play, monitor the render service as a service, with latency, error rate and memory, not as part of the frontend.

Something broke in production and you cannot reproduce it. How do you proceed?

I would try to turn "cannot reproduce" into "have not yet found the variable". The variables in a browser app are a short list: browser and version, device and CPU class, network conditions, locale and timezone, screen size, extensions, and the user's actual data and permissions. So the first move is to get those from the error report and look for a pattern — a bug that is only on Safari, only for one tenant, only for users with more than a hundred records, or only in a timezone west of UTC is a very different investigation from a random one. Timezone and locale in particular account for a startling share of "unreproducible" bugs. Then I would look at the data: production data has shapes that test data does not, and a null field, an empty array or a two-thousand-character string is often the whole story. If that fails, I would add instrumentation rather than guess — targeted logging or a feature-flagged diagnostic path shipped to the affected segment, which is faster than a week of speculation. Session replay is worth having for exactly this, with the caveat that it needs careful redaction. And I would resist the pull to ship a speculative fix, because a fix that does not reproduce also cannot be verified, and now you have two unknowns.

How do you deploy an Angular app safely?

Immutable, hashed asset filenames served with long cache lifetimes, and an index.html that is never cached — that combination is the foundation, and getting it wrong produces a user running a mix of old and new files, which fails in ways that make no sense. Keep the previous build's chunks available for a grace period rather than deleting them, because a user who loaded the app before the deploy will request old chunk filenames when they navigate; that single practice removes most chunk-load incidents. Handle the rest on the client: catch navigation errors caused by a failed chunk load and reload at the target URL, with a loop guard. Detect new versions and offer the user a refresh rather than forcing one mid-task. Beyond that, the usual delivery hygiene applies and matters more than people expect for frontends: deploy behind a flag or a canary so a bad release reaches one percent first, keep a rollback that takes a minute rather than a rebuild, and treat the configuration as part of the artefact so staging and production run the same bundle with different runtime config rather than different builds. And if there is a service worker, understand its update lifecycle before shipping it, because a misconfigured service worker can pin users to an old version far more durably than any cache header.

Legacy and migration reality

What legacy Angular patterns should you recognise, and which are actually harmful?

The ones you will meet constantly and that are merely dated: NgModules with declarations and a SharedModule; constructor injection; *ngIf and *ngFor; @Input and @Output decorators; ngOnChanges; the async pipe. All of those still work, all have migrations, and none is a bug — I would treat them as debt and convert on contact rather than in a campaign. The ones that are actively harmful and worth fixing on sight: manual subscribe without teardown, which leaks and produces work on destroyed components; *ngFor without trackBy, which destroys and rebuilds every row; function calls in template bindings; bypassSecurityTrust on anything user-controlled; direct DOM manipulation through nativeElement, which breaks SSR and encapsulation; and a god SharedModule that everything imports, which defeats lazy loading and tree shaking simultaneously. Then there is one category that is on a clock: @angular/animations was deprecated in 20.2 and is scheduled for removal in v23, so that migration is not optional debt, it is dated work. I find that distinction — dated versus harmful versus deadlined — is the useful thing to bring to a code review, because treating all legacy the same is how modernisation efforts lose credibility.

How do you migrate from NgModules to standalone in a large app?

Use the schematic, in the order it is designed for, and do not hand-write it. ng generate @angular/core:standalone runs in three modes: convert declarations to standalone, remove the now-unnecessary NgModules, and switch bootstrapping to bootstrapApplication. I would run them as three separate pull requests, in that order, each green in CI, because when something breaks you want to know which of the three did it. The friction points are predictable. A SharedModule that everything imports has to be decomposed into direct imports, and that is where the real work is — it is also the highest-value part, because it is what was defeating tree shaking. Anything relying on module-level provider scoping needs a new home, usually route-level providers. forRoot and forChild patterns in your own libraries become provideX() functions. And third-party libraries that only ship modules still work — a standalone component can import an NgModule — so they are not a blocker, which is worth saying because teams often think they are. Afterwards I would run cleanup-unused-imports, since the conversion tends to leave over-broad import arrays, and add a lint rule so no new NgModule appears. The whole thing is usually days rather than weeks, and the surprise is how much dead code it surfaces.

What do you do about an app still on AngularJS?

Start by naming the position honestly: AngularJS reached end of life on 31 December 2021, so it receives no security patches, and the options are commercial extended support, a migration, or an accepted risk that should be written down and signed off by someone senior rather than left implicit. Then decide based on the app's future. If it is in maintenance and will be retired, extended support plus a hard freeze is a legitimate answer and far cheaper than a rewrite nobody will finish. If it is strategic, I would migrate incrementally rather than rewrite: the routing-level strangler pattern is usually better than ngUpgrade in 2026 — put a reverse proxy in front, move one route at a time to a new Angular application, and let the two coexist behind one domain with shared authentication cookies. That gives you working software throughout and a stopping point at every step, which a big-bang rewrite does not. ngUpgrade hybrid bootstrapping still exists and is appropriate when the two must interleave on the same page, but it means running both frameworks and both change detection systems together, which is a performance and debugging cost you carry for the whole migration. Whichever route, the part I would fight for is not doing it alongside a feature freeze — a migration with no user-visible progress for a year is how these projects get cancelled at sixty percent.

How do you handle a third-party library that blocks your upgrade?

First, verify it is genuinely blocking rather than just warning — peer dependency warnings are often noise, and an overrides entry plus a smoke test tells you in an hour whether it actually works on the new major. If it truly breaks, the options in ascending order of cost: check whether a fork or a community-maintained successor exists, since popular abandoned libraries usually have one; patch it locally with a patch-package-style approach, which is fine for a small fix and terrible as a long-term position; contribute the fix upstream, which is worth trying and worth not depending on; wrap and replace, if it was already behind our own abstraction; or remove it, which is often more feasible than it looks because a surprising number of dependencies are doing something we could own in a few hundred lines. What I would not do is stay on the old Angular major waiting, because that turns a bounded problem into an unbounded one — falling two majors behind roughly doubles the eventual work and starts to affect security and hiring. The systemic fix is upstream of this: treat every Angular-coupled dependency as an upgrade risk at adoption time, keep the count small, prefer libraries with a history of same-week major support, and keep anything structural behind our own interface so replacing it is a week rather than a quarter.

Judgment and scenarios

Two teams disagree: one wants NgRx everywhere, one wants signals in services. You decide.

I would start by making the disagreement concrete, because "NgRx versus signals" is usually a proxy for a real dispute about consistency versus autonomy. So: which specific problems have we had — untraceable state changes, duplicated logic, components reaching into each other, onboarding friction — and which of the two approaches would have prevented each one? That usually reveals that both sides are right about different parts of the codebase. My likely decision is a written default rather than a mandate: signals in services as the standard, with a defined shape — private writable signals, public readonly projections, methods as the only mutation path — and NgRx SignalStore permitted where a feature has genuinely complex transitions, with the bar stated explicitly so it is a decision rather than a preference. Consistency has real value at scale, but so does not paying Redux ceremony for a CRUD screen. I would also make the decision reversible and dated: revisit in two quarters with evidence. And I would care more about the interfaces between features than about what happens inside them — as long as features talk to each other through defined contracts, letting one use a heavier store internally is a much smaller problem than the argument itself becoming a standing conflict.

A senior engineer keeps writing components with 400-line templates and 15 inputs. How do you handle it?

Privately first, and with curiosity rather than a rule. There is usually a reason — the domain is genuinely coupled, they have been burned by premature abstraction, or the component grew feature by feature and nobody had a moment to split it. I would ask what would have to be true for them to split it, and often the honest answer is "nothing, I just kept going", which makes the conversation easy. What I would bring is the cost in their terms rather than the principle: a component with fifteen inputs cannot be tested without constructing fifteen things, cannot be reused, produces merge conflicts because everyone edits the same file, and is the reason a change that should be an hour takes a day. Then I would make the alternative concrete by pairing on splitting one of them — usually into a smart container plus two or three presentational components with a shared type — because a demonstration lands where a guideline does not. Systemically I would prefer a lint rule with a generous threshold over a review argument repeated weekly, and a documented house pattern with a schematic so the good structure is the default when a new feature starts. And I would keep my own perspective honest: if the codebase has fifty of these, the problem is the absence of a pattern, not one engineer.

Product wants a feature in two weeks that needs a framework upgrade first. What do you say?

I would separate the two and price them separately, because bundling them is how both go badly. First I would check whether the upgrade is genuinely required or merely desirable — quite often the blocker is a library version rather than the framework, and there is a smaller path. If it truly is required, I would present the options with honest numbers: ship the feature on the current version with a known workaround, which costs some rework later and I would say how much; do the upgrade first, which delays the feature by a stated amount and reduces risk; or do a narrow, temporary version of the feature now and the proper one after. What I would not do is quietly absorb the upgrade into the feature estimate, because when it overruns — and upgrades overrun — the conversation becomes about my credibility instead of about the trade-off. I would also make the risk of deferring explicit rather than moralising about technical debt: an unsupported version has security implications and each deferred major roughly doubles the eventual cost, and that is a business fact product can weigh. In my experience product partners make good decisions when given real options and bad ones when given either a flat no or an unqualified yes.

How do you set frontend engineering standards without becoming the bottleneck?

By encoding standards in tools rather than in myself. Anything that can be a lint rule, a schematic, a CI check or a template should be — module boundary rules, bundle budgets, accessibility checks, an ESLint configuration shipped as a package, a generator that scaffolds a conformant feature. A standard that lives in a wiki and is enforced in review is a standard that depends on one person reading every pull request, which is the definition of a bottleneck and which also fails the moment that person is on holiday. For the things that genuinely need judgment, I would use lightweight architecture decision records so the reasoning is written down once and can be referenced, disagreed with, and revisited — that scales far better than answering the same question in four channels. I would also deliberately distribute the authorship: the person who runs into a problem writes the rule, which makes the standards owned rather than imposed and is how you build the next set of people who can do this. And I would keep the standard set small and revisit it, because a hundred rules nobody remembers is functionally the same as none. The measure I would hold myself to is whether the team makes good decisions when I am not in the room.
