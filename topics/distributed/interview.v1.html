Distributed systems & messaging

interview · reference stack Kafka 4.3.1 / RabbitMQ 4.3.5 · released 2026-06-25

Distributed systems interviews at lead and principal level test one thing above all: whether you know which guarantee you are actually buying, and what it costs. Nearly every question in a real loop reduces to "the call timed out and you do not know whether it ran" — duplicate delivery, reordering, split brain, a partial write, a consumer group that will not rebalance. Expect the hour to move from screener definitions (at-least-once versus at-most-once, queue versus topic) through mechanism (how Kafka assigns partitions, how Raft elects a leader, how an outbox actually works) to judgment (would you approve this design, and how do you run the migration without downtime). 01Consistency models, CAP and the guarantees you get

This is where every distributed-systems loop starts, because it is the cheapest way to tell a candidate who has read a blog post from one who has debugged a stale read at 3am. At screener level it is definitions; at senior level it is "which model does your datastore actually give you, and what does that let a user observe"; at principal level it is the trade conversation with a product owner who wants both. The failure mode interviewers listen for is treating consistency as a single boolean instead of a lattice of models, each with a price in latency and availability.

screenerWhat does the CAP theorem actually say, and why is "pick two" misleading?

Answer

CAP says that when a network partition occurs, a replicated system must choose between remaining available (serving requests that may return stale or conflicting data) and remaining consistent (refusing requests on the minority side so that no client ever observes a violation of linearizability). "Pick two of three" is the misleading part: partitions are not something you choose, they are something the network does to you, so the real statement is during a partition, choose C or A. The rest of the time — which is almost all of the time — you are not making a CAP choice at all, you are making a latency choice, which is why PACELC is the more useful framing. Note also that the C in CAP is specifically linearizability, not the C in ACID: a system can be perfectly ACID and still fail CAP's C across regions.

Example

Three-node cluster, network splits 2 | 1

CP  (etcd, ZooKeeper, Kafka KRaft controller, RabbitMQ quorum queue):
    majority side (2) keeps serving; minority side (1) rejects writes.
    Guarantee kept. Availability lost for anyone who can only reach the 1.

AP  (Cassandra with CL=ONE, DynamoDB eventually-consistent reads, DNS):
    both sides keep serving; the two sides diverge; you now own a
    conflict-resolution problem (last-write-wins, vector clocks, CRDTs).

Follow-ups

  • Is a single-node database CA? — Trivially, and uselessly: there is no partition to survive because there is nothing to partition. The label only means something for replicated systems.
  • Where does a system that "chooses CP" actually lose availability? — On the minority side of the partition and during the leader-election window after it, typically one election timeout (etcd default 1000ms, Raft implementations commonly 150-300ms randomised).
  • Can one system be both? — Per-operation, yes. DynamoDB lets you ask for an eventually consistent read (AP-ish, half the cost) or a strongly consistent read (CP-ish) on the same table; Cassandra tunes it per query with consistency levels.

Common wrong answer

"We're an AP system, so we chose availability and partition tolerance over consistency." It sounds fluent and it is nearly meaningless: it implies partition tolerance was a design choice, and it says nothing about what a client can actually observe. What an interviewer wants is the observable statement — "a read after a write on a different replica can return the old value for up to N seconds, and here is what we do about it."

midRank the consistency models you care about in practice, strongest to weakest.

Answer

The useful ladder is: strict serializability (linearizable multi-object transactions — Spanner, CockroachDB, FoundationDB), linearizability (single-object real-time order — etcd, ZooKeeper writes, a Raft register), sequential consistency (one global order, but not necessarily matching wall clock), causal consistency (operations that are causally related are seen in order; concurrent ones may be seen in any order), then the session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, writes-follow-reads), and finally eventual consistency (replicas converge if writes stop, and nothing else is promised). Orthogonal to that ladder is the transaction isolation ladder — serializable, snapshot isolation, repeatable read, read committed — which is about concurrency between transactions, not about replication. The reason to memorise the ladder is that the right answer to "what consistency do we need" is almost always a specific rung, not "strong", and the rungs below linearizability are dramatically cheaper across regions.

Example

ModelClient can observeTypical cost
Strict serializabilityNothing anomalous, everCross-region consensus per commit; Spanner pays a TrueTime commit-wait
LinearizableSingle key behaves like one registerOne majority round trip per op
CausalNo effect before its causeMetadata (vector clocks / dependency tracking), local reads
Read-your-writesOwn writes always visibleSticky routing or a per-session token
EventualAnything, until convergenceCheapest; you own conflict resolution

Follow-ups

  • Is snapshot isolation stronger or weaker than serializable? — Weaker: it permits write skew, where two transactions each read a consistent snapshot and write disjoint rows that together break an invariant (the classic on-call rota with two doctors both going off duty).
  • Is causal consistency enough for a bank balance? — No. Causal will not stop two concurrent withdrawals from both reading the same balance; you need linearizability or a compare-and-set on a single authoritative record.
  • What is the strongest model available without coordination? — Causal consistency: it is provably the strongest model that stays available under partition (the CAC / "consistency without coordination" result).

Common wrong answer

Conflating linearizability with serializability, usually as "strong consistency means ACID". They answer different questions: linearizability is about real-time ordering of operations on one object; serializability is about an equivalent serial order of multi-object transactions with no real-time requirement at all. A serializable database can serve you a transaction that appears to run in the past. Say "strict serializability" when you mean both.

midWhat is PACELC, and why do practitioners prefer it to CAP?

Answer

PACELC says: if there is a Partition, trade Availability against Consistency; Else — meaning in normal operation, which is 99.9% of the time — trade Latency against Consistency. It is the better framing because the day-to-day design decision is not about partitions at all, it is about how many round trips a read costs. A system is described as, for example, PC/EC (Spanner: consistent under partition, and still pays latency for consistency normally) or PA/EL (Cassandra with low consistency levels, DynamoDB eventual reads: available under partition, and fast the rest of the time). In an interview it lets you say something concrete: "our reads are served from a local replica in 2ms; making them linearizable would mean a cross-AZ quorum and roughly 8-15ms, and cross-region 70ms+, so we bought read-your-writes with a session token instead."

Example

PC/EC  Spanner, CockroachDB, etcd, ZooKeeper
PA/EL  Cassandra (CL=ONE), DynamoDB (eventually consistent reads), Riak
PA/EC  MongoDB with majority read concern and a driver that fails over
PC/EL  rare in practice - consistent under partition, fast (stale) when healthy

Follow-ups

  • Where does a read replica of PostgreSQL sit? — PA/EL for the replica path: streaming replication is asynchronous by default, so a replica read can be arbitrarily stale (watch pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp); synchronous_commit = remote_apply moves it toward EC at the cost of write latency.
  • Does adding replicas improve consistency? — No. It improves durability and read throughput, and it usually makes consistency harder and quorums slower, because a write must now reach more nodes.

Common wrong answer

"We use a consensus store, so consistency is solved." Consensus solves agreement on a log; it does not solve what your application does with a cached copy, a read replica, a CDN, or a client that retries. Most real inconsistency bugs live in the layers around the consistent store, not in it.

seniorDefine linearizability precisely. How would you test whether a system provides it?

Answer

Linearizability means every operation appears to take effect atomically at some instant between its invocation and its response, and that instant order is consistent with real time: if operation A completes before operation B starts, every observer must order A before B. It is a single-object, real-time property, and it composes — a system of linearizable objects is linearizable — which is why it is the model people build locks and leases on. You test it by recording a history of concurrent client operations with invocation and response timestamps and searching for a valid sequential order; that search is NP-complete in general, which is why tools such as Jepsen's Knossos and the faster Elle checker are used, and why you inject partitions, clock skew and process pauses during the run. The important practical consequence is that a system is only linearizable under the failure conditions you actually tested.

Example

Client A:  |--- write(x=1) ---|
Client B:                        |--- read(x) -> 0 ---|

B started strictly after A returned, and read the old value.
That single history is enough to prove NOT linearizable.
No amount of "it converges eventually" repairs it.

Follow-ups

  • Is a Kafka partition linearizable? — The log append is: with acks=all and min.insync.replicas > 1, an acknowledged offset is durably ordered. Consumer reads are not, because a follower fetch or a stale consumer can lag behind the high watermark.
  • Why can a leader lease break linearizability? — Because a leader that is paused by GC or descheduled can wake up believing it still holds the lease after a new leader was elected. The fix is fencing tokens: a monotonically increasing epoch that the downstream resource checks and rejects if stale.
  • What does "read index" mean in Raft? — A way to serve a linearizable read without appending an entry: the leader confirms it is still leader via a heartbeat round, then reads locally once its state machine has applied the committed index.

Common wrong answer

"Linearizable means all reads go through the leader." Going through the leader is neither sufficient (a deposed leader still thinks it is the leader) nor necessary (Raft read-index and lease reads, or a quorum read, are both linearizable without funnelling every read through one node). Say the leader must confirm it still holds a majority before answering.

seniorExplain quorum reads and writes. If R + W > N, is your data safe?

Answer

With N replicas, writing to W and reading from R with R + W > N guarantees that the read set and the write set overlap by at least one replica, so a read sees at least one copy of the newest acknowledged write. That is an intersection guarantee, not a consistency guarantee: you still need a way to decide which of the returned values is newest, which is why Dynamo-style stores carry version vectors or timestamps and why last-write-wins silently drops concurrent updates. The classic gaps are: a write that reached fewer than W replicas but not zero (a "sloppy" partial write leaves the value visible to some readers), hinted handoff putting a replica outside the intended set, a node restored from backup, and clock skew choosing the wrong winner under last-write-wins. So R + W > N buys you overlap, and everything after overlap is conflict resolution.

Example

N=3, W=2, R=2   ->  R+W=4 > 3, sets overlap

write x=1 to [n1,n2]      ack
n2 dies, is replaced empty
read from [n2,n3]         -> both return the OLD value, no overlap in
                             practice because n2 lost its data
Fix: durable membership + anti-entropy (Merkle repair), and never
     count a replaced node as if it held the write.

Follow-ups

  • What is W=N good for? — Nothing in production: a single slow or down replica blocks every write. Use W = majority, and read at majority when you need it.
  • How does DynamoDB do it? — Writes go to three AZs; an eventually consistent read hits one replica, a strongly consistent read is routed so it reflects all prior successful writes and costs twice the read units.
  • What breaks last-write-wins? — Concurrent writes with skewed clocks: the update with the later timestamp wins even if it was logically first, and the other update is gone with no trace. Prefer CRDTs or explicit sibling resolution when concurrency is real.

Common wrong answer

"R + W > N gives you strong consistency." It gives you a non-empty intersection between the read and write quorums, which is a much weaker claim. Dynamo-style quorums are famously not linearizable — reads are not repaired atomically, so two consecutive reads can go forward and then backward in time unless the system does read-repair-before-return.

seniorA user edits their profile and the next page load shows the old value. Fix it without making the system strongly consistent.

Answer

You need read-your-writes for that one session, not linearizability for the whole system, and there are four standard ways to buy it. Sticky routing pins the session to the replica that took the write (cheap, breaks on failover). A version token — the write returns a log sequence number, commit timestamp or ETag that the client sends back, and the read path waits for or routes to a replica that has applied at least that version — is the robust one and is exactly what Kafka's read_committed, MongoDB's causal-consistency afterClusterTime and Postgres's pg_wal_lsn_diff based routing all do. Reading from the primary for a short window after a write (typically 2-5 seconds) is the pragmatic middle ground. And optimistically rendering the value the client just submitted covers the UI case without touching the backend at all. Name the trade in the interview: token-based read-your-writes costs a little latency on affected reads only, whereas making all reads linearizable costs every read forever.

Example

// write returns the position the read path must have reached
var lsn = await _primary.UpdateProfileAsync(userId, dto);
Response.Cookies.Append("rc", lsn.ToString(), Secure);   // session token

// read path: pick a replica that has caught up, else fall back to primary
var want = ParseLsn(Request.Cookies["rc"]);
var replica = _pool.FirstOrDefault(r => r.AppliedLsn >= want) ?? _primary;
return await replica.GetProfileAsync(userId);

Follow-ups

  • Why is sticky routing fragile? — Any failover, deploy, autoscale event or client on a different device breaks the stickiness, and the bug reappears exactly when the system is already unhealthy.
  • What if the token comes from an untrusted client? — Treat it as a hint, clamp it (reject values far in the future), and never let it drive authorisation. Its only power should be "wait or route to primary".
  • How long should the read-from-primary window be? — Slightly longer than your p99.9 replication lag, and it must be monitored — the window is a lie the moment lag exceeds it.

Common wrong answer

"Add a cache-busting query string" or "just increase replication frequency". Both treat a consistency-model problem as a caching or tuning problem, and both fail under load, which is when it matters. The interviewer is checking whether you know that read-your-writes is a named session guarantee with standard implementations.

seniorWhat is causal consistency, and when is it exactly the right target?

Answer

Causal consistency guarantees that if operation B could have been influenced by operation A — same session, or B read a value A wrote — then every replica applies A before B; operations with no causal relationship may be applied in any order and different replicas may disagree about them. It is the right target for anything where the visible anomaly is "the reply appeared before the comment" or "the friend-removal happened after the post they were not supposed to see": social feeds, comment threads, collaborative documents, notification streams. It is attractive because it is the strongest model that can be provided while remaining available under partition, and because it is implementable with metadata rather than coordination — dependency stamps, version vectors, or a per-session token that the read path waits on. Its limit is equally sharp: it says nothing about concurrent conflicting updates, so anything with a shared numeric invariant (stock, balance, seat count) still needs a single point of order.

Example

Alice: post P                     (no dependency)
Bob:   read P, reply R            (R depends on P)

Causal:      no replica may show R without P.
Eventual:    a replica can show "Re: ..." with the post missing.
Not covered: Alice and Bob both set the title concurrently -> still a conflict.

Follow-ups

  • How do you carry causality cheaply? — Per-session vector or a single Lamport-style dependency timestamp per client; full vector clocks over all clients do not scale, so real systems bound the metadata (per-datacenter vectors, dependency compression).
  • What do CRDTs add? — They make concurrent updates mergeable by construction (grow-only sets, counters, RGA/Yjs sequences), so you get convergence without a coordinator. They cannot express arbitrary invariants such as "never negative".
  • Is Kafka causal? — Within a partition, yes by construction. Across partitions there is no ordering at all, which is why "put causally related events on the same key" is such a common design instruction.

Common wrong answer

"Causal consistency means events arrive in the order they were sent." Sending order is not causality — two clients sending independently have no causal relation, and their order is genuinely undefined. Candidates who say this then propose a global timestamp sort, which reintroduces clock skew as a correctness bug.

seniorWhat are the four session guarantees, and how are they implemented?

Answer

They come from the Bayou work and they are the vocabulary interviewers expect from a senior candidate: read-your-writes (a session sees its own writes), monotonic reads (a session never sees time go backwards), monotonic writes (a session's writes are applied in the order it issued them), and writes-follow-reads (a write issued after reading a value is ordered after that value). Together they give a single user a coherent experience without giving the whole system a global order, which is why they are the cheap and correct answer to most "stale data" bug reports. All four are implemented with the same two primitives: a session-scoped version token that the read path waits for or routes on, and per-session ordering of writes (a single in-flight write, or a sequence number the server enforces). MongoDB packages them as causally consistent sessions; Cosmos DB exposes "Session" as a first-class consistency level; DynamoDB and Cassandra leave it to you.

Example

monotonic reads violated (no session token):
  t0 read from replica A (lag 0s)  -> balance 100
  t1 read from replica B (lag 30s) -> balance  40   <-- time went backwards

fix: carry max-version-seen in the session; a replica behind it is skipped.

Follow-ups

  • Which one do users complain about loudest? — Read-your-writes; but monotonic reads produces the scarier support ticket, because the value visibly regresses and users assume data loss.
  • How do you get monotonic writes with a queue in the path? — Same partition key per session (or per entity) plus max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=1, or an idempotent producer, so a retry cannot reorder two writes from the same session.

Common wrong answer

Treating these as "nice-to-have UX polish". They are precisely-defined guarantees with cheap implementations, and calling them UX suggests you would fix them with a spinner. The strong answer names the guarantee that was violated, then the token that restores it.

principalWhen is it right to choose availability over consistency, and how do you sell that to a product owner?

Answer

Choose availability when the cost of a stale or duplicated answer is lower than the cost of no answer, and when the anomaly is detectable and repairable after the fact. Reads of catalogues, feeds, recommendations, search, dashboards, telemetry and most notification paths qualify; anything that mints money, allocates a scarce unit, or grants access does not. The conversation with a product owner works when you stop using the words consistent and available and describe the two observable outcomes instead: "during a regional network fault, option A shows a price that is up to 30 seconds old to about 2% of sessions; option B shows an error page to 100% of sessions in that region for the same period." Then you name the repair: reconciliation job, compensating action, customer-facing correction, and the SLO you will hold. At principal level the extra move is scoping — availability for the read path and the browse funnel, consistency for the single write that allocates the unit — rather than one setting for the whole system.

Example

Ticketing:
  browse / seat map      AP  (stale by seconds is fine, refresh on click)
  hold a seat            CP  (single-partition CAS, fencing token, TTL)
  payment capture        CP  + idempotency key, at-least-once with dedup
  confirmation email     AP  (at-least-once, dedup on message id)
The system is not "AP" or "CP". Four different answers in one flow.

Follow-ups

  • What if the product owner wants both? — Offer the third option that usually satisfies them: stay available but degrade honestly — serve the stale value with a visible "as of 14:32" and disable the actions that would be unsafe.
  • How do you know overselling actually happened? — You instrument it: an invariant checker that runs continuously and alerts on violation, plus a reconciliation report. A trade you cannot measure is a trade you cannot defend.
  • What is the failure mode of overselling by design? — It is fine at 0.1% and a brand crisis at 5%; the deciding factor is whether the compensating path (refund, upgrade, apology) is cheap and automatic.

Common wrong answer

"Business-critical data must always be strongly consistent." It sounds responsible and it is how systems end up with a global serialisable database in the read path of a home page. The senior answer partitions the problem by operation, and reserves coordination for the operations that truly allocate something.

principalHow do you verify that a distributed system provides the guarantee it claims?

Answer

You do not verify it by reading the vendor page; you verify it by generating concurrent histories under injected faults and checking them against a model. The practical toolkit is: a workload generator with known invariants, a fault injector (partitions, asymmetric partitions, clock skew, process pause via SIGSTOP, disk-full, slow disk, node kill mid-write), and a checker — Jepsen's Knossos for linearizability, Elle for transactional anomalies, or a hand-rolled invariant checker for your own domain. Complement it with deterministic simulation testing, which is how FoundationDB and TigerBeetle are built and which is increasingly the expected answer at principal level: run the whole system on a simulated clock and network so a failing schedule is reproducible from a seed. In production the equivalent is continuous verification: an always-on invariant job, plus consumer-lag and divergence alarms. The senior insight to state is that guarantees are only as good as the fault set you exercised, so the interesting question is always "which fault did you not inject?"

Example

Fault menu that actually finds bugs, roughly by yield:
  1. process pause (SIGSTOP 30s)      - breaks lease/heartbeat assumptions
  2. asymmetric partition (A->B only) - breaks "I can see it so it can see me"
  3. clock jump forward/backward      - breaks LWW and TTL logic
  4. slow disk (fsync 2s)             - turns "healthy" nodes into zombies
  5. kill -9 mid-write                - finds torn writes / missing fsync
  6. rolling restart during rebalance - finds the real consumer-group bugs

Follow-ups

  • Why is a process pause more interesting than a node kill? — A killed node stops lying; a paused node wakes up and acts on stale beliefs, which is how split brain and duplicate leaders actually occur.
  • How do you make this affordable? — Run the fault suite in CI against a small cluster nightly, in an ephemeral namespace, with a seeded generator so failures reproduce; save the failing seed as a regression test.
  • What is game day for? — Verifying the humans and the runbook, not the code: an untested failover procedure is an outage with a delay fuse.

Common wrong answer

"We load-tested it and it held up." Load tests exercise the happy path at scale; consistency bugs live in the interaction of concurrency and failure. A system can pass a week of load testing and lose data in the first thirty-second partition.

seniorWhat are the eight fallacies of distributed computing, and which one bites hardest in 2026?

Answer

The list (Deutsch and Gosling): the network is reliable; latency is zero; bandwidth is infinite; the network is secure; topology does not change; there is one administrator; transport cost is zero; the network is homogeneous. They are worth naming because each maps to a concrete design control — retries with budgets, explicit timeouts, backpressure and payload limits, mTLS and authz, service discovery and connection re-resolution, versioned contracts, cost-aware data placement, and protocol negotiation. The one that bites hardest now is "topology does not change": in an autoscaled, spot-instanced, rolling-deployed environment the set of peers changes continuously, and the bugs that follow are stale DNS in long-lived connection pools, load balancers holding connections to terminated pods, and consumer groups rebalancing every deploy. A close second is "latency is zero", which in 2026 shows up as chatty service-to-service calls and per-item LLM or vector-store calls inside a loop that was designed for local memory access.

Example

Symptom: 5xx spike for exactly 300 seconds after every deploy.
Cause:   HTTP client caches DNS for the JVM/runtime default TTL and keeps
         pooled connections to pods that no longer exist.
Fix:     bounded connection lifetime (e.g. 60-120s), DNS TTL respected,
         retry on connection-reset for idempotent calls only.

Follow-ups

  • Which fallacy does a service mesh remove? — Mostly "the network is secure" (mTLS by default) and part of "topology does not change" (endpoint discovery). It does not remove latency, and it adds a hop plus a new failure domain.
  • How does "one administrator" show up? — Cross-team change collisions: someone rotates a certificate, changes a quota, or drains a zone while you are mid-migration. The control is change calendars and, more usefully, systems that degrade rather than break under someone else's change.

Common wrong answer

Reciting all eight and stopping. The list is trivia unless each fallacy is attached to a control you have actually implemented. Interviewers use it as an opening for "give me a time the network was not reliable and what you changed afterwards."

02Messaging fundamentals and delivery semantics

Every loop that mentions "microservices" or "event-driven" spends time here, and it is the unit with the widest depth range: the same topic supplies the screener question ("queue versus topic") and the principal question ("how do you stop the broker becoming a distributed monolith"). Interviewers test it because the vocabulary is where miscommunication starts — two engineers saying "exactly-once" and meaning different things is a real production risk. At lead level you are expected to move fluently between the guarantee, the broker setting that provides it, and the code that has to cope when it does not.

screenerQueue versus topic: what is the difference and when do you use each?

Answer

A queue is point-to-point: each message is delivered to exactly one of the competing consumers, and consuming removes it, so adding consumers adds throughput for the same work. A topic is publish-subscribe: each message is delivered to every interested subscriber, so adding a subscriber adds a new independent view of the same stream. The design question behind it is ownership: a queue models "this work must be done once by someone", a topic models "this fact happened and anyone may care". Modern brokers blur the line — Kafka is a partitioned log where a topic gives pub/sub across consumer groups and queue-like competition within a group, and since Kafka 4.2 (2026-02-17) share groups add true per-message queue semantics on the same topic — but the ownership question is still the one that decides your design.

Example

Queue  (SQS, RabbitMQ classic/quorum queue, Kafka share group)
  order.process   -> one of N workers charges the card exactly once

Topic  (Kafka topic + consumer groups, SNS, RabbitMQ fanout exchange)
  order.placed    -> billing, warehouse, analytics, email each get a copy
                     and each keeps its own position

Follow-ups

  • Which one for a command, which for an event? — Commands ("charge this card") go to a queue owned by the handler; events ("card was charged") go to a topic owned by the publisher. Getting this backwards is the root of most event-driven coupling problems.
  • How do you get pub/sub from a queue broker? — Fan out at the broker: an exchange with bindings in RabbitMQ, SNS-to-many-SQS in AWS. Never fan out in the publisher by calling N queues — a partial failure leaves subscribers inconsistent.
  • Can a topic subscriber compete with itself? — Yes: that is precisely a Kafka consumer group, where partitions are divided among group members.

Common wrong answer

"Kafka is a topic, RabbitMQ is a queue." Both do both. RabbitMQ has had fanout, topic and headers exchanges since forever plus streams since 3.9; Kafka has had queue-like consumer groups from the start and real share-group queues since 4.2. Answer with the semantics you need, then name the broker feature that provides it.

screenerDefine at-most-once, at-least-once and exactly-once. Which do you build for?

Answer

At-most-once means a message may be lost but never duplicated — you acknowledge before processing. At-least-once means a message is never lost but may be duplicated — you acknowledge after processing, so a crash between the work and the ack causes redelivery. Exactly-once as a wire guarantee is impossible in the general case, because the network can always lose the acknowledgement and the sender cannot distinguish "not delivered" from "delivered, ack lost"; what is achievable is effectively-once processing, which is at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent or transactionally deduplicated consumer. In practice you build at-least-once and you make the handler idempotent, because that combination is cheap, portable across brokers, and survives the failures that the exactly-once machinery itself can hit. Say that sentence in an interview and you have answered three questions at once.

Example

// at-most-once: ack first, then work. A crash loses the message.
await channel.BasicAckAsync(tag, multiple: false);
await HandleAsync(msg);

// at-least-once: work first, then ack. A crash redelivers.
await HandleAsync(msg);          // MUST be idempotent
await channel.BasicAckAsync(tag, multiple: false);

// effectively-once: at-least-once + dedup in the same transaction as the effect
using var tx = db.BeginTransaction();
if (await db.TryInsertProcessedAsync(msg.Id, tx))   // unique index on msg.Id
    await ApplyEffectAsync(msg, tx);
await tx.CommitAsync();
await channel.BasicAckAsync(tag, multiple: false);

Follow-ups

  • When is at-most-once acceptable? — High-volume telemetry, metrics, cache invalidation hints, click streams for approximate analytics: anything where the cost of loss is smaller than the cost of the dedup machinery.
  • What does Kafka's exactly-once actually cover? — Read-process-write within Kafka: the transactional producer plus read_committed consumers make consume-transform-produce atomic. It does not extend to your database or an outbound HTTP call.
  • Does a broker with "exactly-once" mean you can drop idempotency? — No. The moment your handler touches an external system, that leg is at-least-once again.

Common wrong answer

"We use Kafka's exactly-once semantics, so duplicates are impossible." This is the single most common rejection trigger in this unit. Kafka EOS is scoped to Kafka-to-Kafka; if the consumer writes to Postgres, sends an email, or calls Stripe, exactly-once ends at the broker boundary and the handler must still be idempotent.

screenerWhy put a broker between two services instead of calling HTTP directly?

Answer

Four reasons, in the order they actually justify the cost: temporal decoupling (the consumer can be down or deploying and no request is lost), load levelling (a burst is absorbed by the queue instead of by the downstream's thread pool), fan-out (one publish serves N independent consumers without the publisher knowing them), and failure isolation (a slow consumer does not turn into a caller-side timeout cascade). What you pay for it is real: eventual consistency, at-least-once duplicates, harder end-to-end tracing, an extra operational component, and a debugging model where "where is my message" replaces a stack trace. The lead-level answer names the cost as clearly as the benefit and adds the decision rule: use a synchronous call when the caller needs the result to continue and the operation is fast; use a broker when the caller only needs the work to eventually happen.

Example

SituationChooseWhy
Read a user's profile to render a pageHTTP / gRPCCaller needs the answer now; latency budget is the constraint
Send the welcome email after signupQueueSignup must not fail because SMTP is down
Notify five teams that an order shippedTopicPublisher must not know or wait for subscribers
Charge a card during checkoutHTTP + idempotency keyUser is waiting; needs a synchronous decision
Reindex a catalogue of 10M itemsQueueLoad levelling; restartable; parallelisable

Follow-ups

  • Does a broker improve availability? — It improves the publisher's availability by decoupling it from the consumer, and it becomes a new shared dependency whose own availability now caps everything. Net effect depends on whether the broker is more reliable than the consumer, which it usually is.
  • What breaks when you convert a synchronous call to a queue? — Error reporting: the caller no longer learns about failure. You need a status resource, a callback event, or a saga; "fire and forget" without one is how silent data loss ships.
  • Is request/reply over a broker a good idea? — Occasionally (RabbitMQ reply-to with a direct-reply-to pseudo-queue), but you inherit queue latency and lose HTTP's tooling. Prefer HTTP unless you need the queue's buffering.

Common wrong answer

"Queues make the system asynchronous, which makes it faster." A broker adds latency to the individual operation; what it improves is throughput under burst and the caller's tail latency. Saying "faster" without that distinction signals you have not measured either.

midPush versus pull consumers: what changes?

Answer

In a push model the broker sends messages to the consumer as they arrive (RabbitMQ basic.consume, SNS to HTTP, webhooks), which gives the lowest latency but makes flow control the broker's problem — hence prefetch/QoS limits, and hence the classic failure where an unbounded prefetch lets one consumer buffer thousands of messages and starve its peers. In a pull model the consumer asks for a batch when it is ready (Kafka poll(), SQS ReceiveMessage), which gives natural backpressure, easy batching and replay, at the cost of either polling latency or long-poll complexity. Most modern systems are pull with long polling, because backpressure that emerges from the protocol is far more robust than backpressure you have to configure. The senior point is that push systems need an explicit credit mechanism (AMQP prefetch, gRPC flow control, reactive streams demand) or they will fail under load in a way that looks like a consumer bug.

Example

// RabbitMQ push: prefetch IS your backpressure. Unbounded = outage.
await channel.BasicQosAsync(prefetchSize: 0, prefetchCount: 20, global: false);

// Kafka pull: the poll loop is the backpressure, but the session timeout
// punishes you for a slow batch -> tune max.poll.records, not the timeout.
// max.poll.interval.ms default 300000 (5 min) since 0.10.1

Follow-ups

  • What prefetch value should you use? — Start with roughly (processing concurrency) x (round-trip time / processing time), commonly 10-50 for fast handlers and 1-5 for slow ones. Prefetch 1 with a 5ms RTT and a 2ms handler will halve your throughput.
  • How does SQS long polling work? — WaitTimeSeconds up to 20 keeps the receive call open until a message arrives, which removes both empty-receive cost and polling latency. Short polling also samples only a subset of servers, so it can return empty on a non-empty queue.
  • What is the pull model's hidden cost? — Consumer liveness detection: the broker only knows you are alive when you poll, so a handler that blocks for longer than max.poll.interval.ms gets kicked out of the group and its work is redone.

Common wrong answer

"Push is faster, pull is more scalable." Both statements are shallow. The real distinction is who owns flow control. A candidate who says "push, with prefetch set so a consumer holds at most one batch of in-flight work" has answered; one who says "push is faster" has not thought about the 3am failure.

midWhat is a dead-letter queue and what is your policy for it?

Answer

A dead-letter queue holds messages that could not be processed after the configured attempts, so the main queue keeps flowing instead of head-of-line blocking on one poison message. The policy matters more than the mechanism: a DLQ with no alert and no owner is a data-loss bucket with extra steps. My rule is — alert on non-zero depth with a named owner, keep the original message plus the failure reason, the attempt count and the trace id in headers, retain for longer than your incident response time (14 days is a common floor), and provide a one-command replay that goes back to the tail of the source queue and is itself idempotent. The subtle part is deciding what belongs there: transient failures (downstream 503, timeout) should be retried with backoff and never reach the DLQ, while permanent failures (schema violation, unknown message type, business rule rejection) should go there immediately rather than burning 25 retries.

Example

RabbitMQ (quorum queue):
  x-dead-letter-exchange: dlx
  x-delivery-limit: 5            <-- quorum queues count redeliveries
  4.3 adds delayed retries so a retry does not need a separate delay queue

SQS: RedrivePolicy { deadLetterTargetArn, maxReceiveCount: 5 }
     plus a redrive-allow-policy so only the source queue can redrive back

Kafka: no native DLQ. You publish to .dlt yourself
     (Spring Kafka DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer, Kafka Connect
      errors.tolerance=all + errors.deadletterqueue.topic.name,
      Kafka Streams DLQ in exception handlers since 4.2, KIP-1034)

Follow-ups

  • Why is Kafka different? — Because the log is not a work queue: you cannot remove one record, so "dead-lettering" is publishing elsewhere and advancing the offset. That choice is the application's, which is why every framework rolls its own.
  • How do you replay safely? — Replay in small batches, with the handler idempotent, and after confirming the root cause is fixed; tag replayed messages so you can tell a replay storm from real traffic in your dashboards.
  • What if the DLQ has 2 million messages? — Treat it as a data-repair project, not a queue: dump to object storage, classify by failure reason, fix and re-emit the classes that are recoverable, and write off the rest explicitly with a decision record.

Common wrong answer

"Retry three times then dead-letter" as a universal policy, with no distinction between transient and permanent errors. It sends every downstream blip to the DLQ and, worse, retries schema errors three times each. Classify the exception first; retry only what retrying can fix.

midHow does scaling work with competing consumers, and where is the ceiling?

Answer

With a plain queue (RabbitMQ, SQS) you scale by adding consumers: the broker hands each message to one of them, so throughput grows roughly linearly until the broker, the downstream database, or a shared lock becomes the bottleneck — there is no per-consumer partition ceiling, but there is also no ordering. With a partitioned log (Kafka classic consumer groups) the unit of parallelism is the partition, so the ceiling is hard: 12 partitions means at most 12 useful consumers in a group, and the thirteenth sits idle. That is the single most common Kafka gotcha in interviews, and the follow-up is always "so how do you scale beyond it": add partitions (which changes key-to-partition mapping and therefore ordering), or — since Kafka 4.2 — use a share group, where consumers can exceed the partition count because delivery is tracked per record rather than per partition offset. Whichever you choose, the real ceiling is usually the downstream: 200 consumers all writing to one Postgres table will queue on locks long before the broker notices.

Example

Kafka classic consumer group, 12 partitions:
  6 consumers  -> 2 partitions each
  12 consumers -> 1 each  (max useful parallelism)
  20 consumers -> 12 working, 8 idle standbys

Kafka share group (KIP-932, production-ready in 4.2, 2026-02-17):
  12 partitions, 50 consumers -> all 50 can consume; per-record
  acknowledgement (ACCEPT / RELEASE / REJECT), delivery counts,
  no ordering guarantee, and no per-partition offset commit.

Follow-ups

  • Why not just create 1000 partitions up front? — Partitions cost open file handles, memory, replication traffic and metadata; they lengthen leader-election and rebalance times, and they fragment batching so producer throughput drops. Size for peak plus headroom (a common rule is target throughput / per-partition throughput, rounded up, times 2), not for infinity.
  • Can you decrease partition count? — Not in Kafka. You create a new topic and migrate, which is why over-partitioning is a one-way door people regret less than under-partitioning.
  • What if one key is 40% of the traffic? — That is a hot partition and no amount of consumers fixes it. Sub-key the hot tenant (tenant#shard), or handle it on a dedicated topic with its own consumers.

Common wrong answer

"Just add more consumers to the group." In Kafka this is the answer that ends the topic: consumers beyond the partition count do nothing, and the candidate has revealed they have never watched a consumer group table. Name the partition ceiling before you are asked.

seniorThe broker acknowledged the publish. What exactly has been promised?

Answer

Much less than people assume, and the answer is entirely configuration-dependent. In Kafka, acks=0 promises nothing (fire and forget), acks=1 promises the leader has it in its page cache — not on disk, and a leader crash before replication loses it — and acks=all promises every in-sync replica has it, which is only meaningful if min.insync.replicas >= 2, otherwise "all" can mean "the one surviving replica". In RabbitMQ, a publisher confirm means the message reached the queue; whether it is on disk depends on the queue type and the message being persistent, and with quorum queues the confirm arrives after a Raft majority has it. Kafka does not fsync per message by default — it relies on replication across brokers for durability rather than on disk flush — which is the fact interviewers most often probe. So the correct answer form is: "acknowledged means replicated to N nodes' page cache; we tolerate the loss of N-1 and we accept correlated power loss as a risk we mitigate with rack/AZ spread."

Example

Kafka durable-by-default triple (what a lead should say from memory):
  producer: acks=all, enable.idempotence=true (default since 3.0),
            retries=MAX, max.in.flight.requests.per.connection<=5
  topic:    replication.factor=3, min.insync.replicas=2
  broker:   unclean.leader.election.enable=false (default since 2.4)

RF=3 + min.insync=2 survives one broker loss and still accepts writes.
RF=3 + min.insync=3 stops accepting writes the moment one broker restarts.

Follow-ups

  • What does unclean leader election do? — Allows an out-of-sync replica to become leader, trading silent data loss for availability. Off by default since Kafka 2.4; turning it on is an explicit decision to lose records.
  • Is a RabbitMQ persistent message safe after a confirm? — With a quorum queue, yes to the extent that a Raft majority has written it. With a classic durable queue on one node, a confirm can precede the disk flush window, and a power cut loses it.
  • How do you lose data with acks=all and RF=3? — Set min.insync.replicas=1: if two brokers are down, "all" means one, and the write survives on a single node.

Common wrong answer

"acks=all means the data is written to disk on all replicas." It means the replicas have appended it, which is a write to the OS page cache; Kafka's durability story is replication, not fsync. Candidates who believe fsync happened then propose the wrong mitigation for a correlated power failure.

seniorA poison message is blocking your consumer. Walk me through the handling.

Answer

First, distinguish the two shapes: in a queue the poison message blocks only itself (other consumers keep working) unless it is redelivered in a tight loop, which burns CPU and can starve the queue; in a partitioned log it blocks the entire partition, because you cannot advance the offset past it, and every key behind it stalls. The handling is the same skeleton: bound the attempts with a delivery counter that lives with the message, use exponential backoff with jitter so a retry storm does not synchronise, classify the exception (transient versus permanent) so permanent failures short-circuit to the DLQ, and make sure retries do not hold a broker delivery open beyond the visibility or poll timeout. For Kafka specifically, the mature pattern is non-blocking retry: send the failed record to a retry topic with a delay, commit the original offset, and let a scheduled consumer of the retry topic re-attempt — that keeps the main partition flowing at the cost of losing per-key ordering for the failed record, which is a trade you must state out loud.

Example

try { await Handle(msg); }
catch (Exception ex) when (IsTransient(ex))          // 503, timeout, deadlock
{
    var n = msg.Headers.Attempt() + 1;
    if (n > MaxAttempts) { await Dlq(msg, ex); return; }
    var delay = Jitter(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(Math.Pow(2, n)));  // 2,4,8,16,32s
    await RetryTopic(delay).PublishAsync(msg.WithAttempt(n));  // non-blocking
}
catch (Exception ex) { await Dlq(msg, ex); }         // permanent: no retries

Follow-ups

  • Why not just Thread.Sleep and retry in place? — It holds the partition or the delivery for the duration, risks a max.poll.interval.ms eviction and rebalance, and multiplies latency across every message behind it. In-place retry is only for very short waits (under a second).
  • How do you get delays in Kafka, which has no per-message delay? — Tiered retry topics (retry-5s, retry-1m, retry-10m) with a consumer that pauses until the record's timestamp plus delay, or an external scheduler. RabbitMQ 4.3 added native delayed retries on quorum queues so the classic delay-queue hack is no longer needed.
  • What breaks ordering here? — Any non-blocking retry reorders that key. If ordering is a hard requirement, you must block the partition and page a human, or move the entity to a "quarantined" state that suppresses later events for that key.

Common wrong answer

"Catch the exception, log it, and commit the offset." That is silent data loss dressed as resilience — the message is gone, no alert fired, and the reconciliation gap appears weeks later. If you swallow a message you must record it somewhere durable with enough context to replay.

seniorLog versus queue: when does the distinction actually change your design?

Answer

A queue is destructive: the broker owns the delivery state, consuming removes the message, and the broker must track per-message acknowledgement — which is why queues give you per-message redelivery, priorities, selective retry and TTL cheaply, and why deep queues hurt. A log is non-destructive: records are appended and retained by policy, consumers own a cursor, so you get replay, multiple independent readers at different positions, and time travel — at the price of no per-message operations, no priorities, and per-partition ordering as the only ordering primitive. The distinction changes your design in four places: replay (a log lets you rebuild a projection from scratch; a queue does not), retention as a first-class product feature (audit, backfill, new consumer onboarding), the retry story (queues redeliver, logs need retry topics), and load shape (a log's throughput is sequential IO and holds up at TB scale, whereas a deep classic queue degrades). Kafka 4.2's share groups are interesting precisely because they add queue-shaped per-record acknowledgement on top of the log.

  • Is a deep Kafka topic a problem? — Not the way a deep queue is: retention is expected, and tiered storage (KIP-405, GA in 3.9, with follower bootstrapping from tiered offsets added in 4.3 via KIP-1023) pushes cold segments to object storage so brokers stay small.
  • When do you want both? — Very often: an event log as the system of record for facts, plus work queues fed from it for the tasks that need per-message retry and priority.

Common wrong answer

"They are the same thing, Kafka just keeps the messages." The retention difference is the visible tip; the structural difference is who owns delivery state. That is what determines whether you can replay, whether you can retry one message, and whether the broker's memory grows with your backlog.

seniorHow large should a message be, and what do you do with a 50 MB payload?

Answer

Aim for kilobytes: under about 100 KB is comfortable, 1 MB is the point where broker defaults start pushing back (Kafka's message.max.bytes default is 1 MiB, SQS caps at 256 KB, Azure Service Bus standard at 256 KB and premium at 100 MB), and anything larger distorts batching, replication and memory. For a 50 MB payload the answer is the claim-check pattern: write the blob to object storage, publish a small message containing the URI, a content hash, a size and a version, and let the consumer fetch it. That keeps the broker fast, makes retries cheap, and gives you lifecycle control over the payload independent of the topic's retention. The two things to state unprompted are the ordering of operations — write the blob first, then publish, so a consumer never sees a reference to something that does not exist — and the garbage-collection story, because orphaned blobs from failed publishes need a TTL or a sweeper.

Example

  "type": "document.ingested",
  "occurredAt": "2026-09-08T09:14:02.117Z",
  "claimCheck": {
    "uri": "s3://ingest-prod/2026/09/08/01JB0V0Z.pdf",

    "contentType": "application/pdf",
    "expiresAt": "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z"

Follow-ups

  • Why not raise message.max.bytes to 64 MB? — You must raise it consistently on broker, topic, producer (max.request.size) and consumer (fetch.max.bytes, max.partition.fetch.bytes); memory per fetch multiplies by partitions; replication and rebalance slow down; and one large record can stall a partition. It is a legitimate choice up to a few MB, and a bad one beyond.
  • What if the consumer cannot reach object storage? — Then the claim check has coupled availability to a second system; mitigate with a retry policy, the same region, and a message TTL longer than the blob's.
  • Does compression save you? — Often, for JSON: compression.type=zstd typically gets 5-10x on text and is the current default recommendation over gzip/snappy for throughput per CPU. It does not change the semantics of a genuinely large binary payload.

Common wrong answer

"Split it into chunks and reassemble on the consumer." Now you own ordering, partial-batch detection, timeouts for missing chunks, and cleanup — a distributed-systems problem invented to avoid an S3 PUT. Chunking is justified only for genuine streaming, never as a size workaround.

principalHow do you stop a message bus from turning into a distributed monolith?

Answer

The bus becomes a monolith when events leak internal structure and when consumers acquire the right to demand changes from producers. The controls are organisational as much as technical: publish domain events that describe a business fact in the producer's own vocabulary rather than CRUD row images; treat the schema as a public API with an owner, a compatibility policy enforced in CI, and a deprecation window; forbid a consumer from asking the producer to add a field for the consumer's convenience — it should join to its own data instead; and keep commands (directed, one owner, may fail) strictly separate from events (broadcast, no expectation of a listener). Structurally, put a boundary at team lines: internal topics are private, published topics are contracted, and the difference is visible in naming and access control. The failure symptom to name is the give-away: if a change to one service requires a coordinated release with three others, you no longer have services, you have a monolith with network latency between its modules.

Example

Leaky (CRUD over the bus, producer's table is the contract):
  customer.row.updated { id, col_a, col_b, col_c, updated_at }
  -> every consumer parses the producer's schema; every ALTER TABLE is a
     cross-team release.

Domain events (fact, past tense, owned vocabulary):
  customer.address.changed { customerId, newAddress, effectiveFrom, version }
  customer.deactivated     { customerId, reason, deactivatedAt, version }
  -> producer refactors freely; consumers depend on meaning, not storage.

Follow-ups

  • What about CDC, which is by definition row-shaped? — Fine as a mechanism, but terminate it at a boundary: use Debezium into a private topic, then transform to domain events in a service you own. Publishing raw CDC to other teams is exporting your schema.
  • How thin should events be? — Thin enough to avoid coupling, fat enough to avoid a callback storm. The common middle is event-carried state transfer for the fields consumers provably need, with an id for the rest — and you measure it by counting how often consumers call back to the producer.
  • How do you enforce this at 40 teams? — Schema registry with compatibility gates in CI, a naming standard, ownership metadata on every topic, and a contract-test suite the producer runs against recorded consumer expectations. Guidance without enforcement decays in one quarter.

Common wrong answer

"We use events, so the services are decoupled." Events decouple runtime availability, not schemas or semantics. A system where every service consumes every other service's row-level changes is more tightly coupled than a set of REST calls, because the coupling is now invisible and asynchronous.

03Idempotency, exactly-once and the outbox

If unit 01 is the theory, this is the unit where the theory becomes code, and it is the highest-yield preparation in the whole bank: "how do you avoid double-charging the customer" is asked in some form in almost every backend loop from mid level upward. Interviewers use it to check that you can go from a slogan ("make it idempotent") to a schema, a unique constraint and a transaction boundary. At principal level the questions turn operational — retention of dedup state, migration of an outbox under load, and what you do when the guarantee has already been violated in production.

screenerWhat makes an operation idempotent, and how do you make a handler that is not?

Answer

An operation is idempotent if applying it more than once has the same observable effect as applying it once — note "observable effect", not "same return value", because a second call may legitimately return a cached result or a 409. Some operations are naturally idempotent: setting an absolute value, deleting by id, upserting by primary key. The ones that are not are the interesting ones: increments, appends, sends, and any external side effect. You make them idempotent one of three ways — carry a client-supplied key and record it in the same transaction as the effect (the general answer), use a conditional write with a version or expected-value check so a replay fails harmlessly (compare-and-set, ETag, WHERE version = @v), or restructure the operation into an absolute assignment instead of a delta. The part candidates skip is that the dedup record and the effect must commit atomically; a dedup table written in a separate transaction just moves the race.

Example

-- NOT idempotent: a retry double-credits
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 100 WHERE id = 42;

-- Idempotent via a dedup row in the SAME transaction
BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO processed_messages (message_id, handler, processed_at)
  VALUES ('01JB0V...', 'credit-account', now())
  ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING;                     -- unique (message_id, handler)

  UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 100
  WHERE id = 42
    AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM processed_messages
                WHERE message_id = '01JB0V...' AND processed_at = now());
COMMIT;
-- simpler in code: if the INSERT affected 0 rows, skip the UPDATE and ack.

Follow-ups

  • Is HTTP PUT idempotent and POST not? — By specification, yes; by implementation, only if you wrote it that way. A PUT that appends to an audit log is not idempotent in its observable effects.
  • Is idempotent the same as commutative? — No. Idempotent means "twice equals once"; commutative means order does not matter. At-least-once delivery can reorder as well as duplicate, so you often need both, and that is what pushes people to CRDTs or version checks.
  • What if the side effect is an email? — You cannot un-send it, so dedup must happen before the send and be committed durably; the usual shape is claim the key transactionally, then send, then mark sent, accepting that a crash in the middle risks one duplicate rather than one loss.

Common wrong answer

"We check if the record already exists before inserting." Check-then-act is a race: two concurrent deliveries both check, both find nothing, both insert. The correct primitive is a database-enforced unique constraint (or a conditional write), letting the storage engine arbitrate. Interviewers ask "and what if two consumers process the same message simultaneously?" for exactly this reason.

midDesign the idempotency key for a payments API. What goes in it and how long does it live?

Answer

The key must be generated by the client, before the first attempt, and reused unchanged for every retry of that logical request — a server-generated key cannot protect against the retry of a request the server never saw a response for. Store it with the scope it belongs to (typically the API key or tenant plus the endpoint), the request fingerprint (a hash of the body), the response status and body, and a state (in-flight, completed, failed). On a repeat you have three cases: same key and same fingerprint while in flight, return 409 or make the caller wait; same key and same fingerprint after completion, return the stored response verbatim; same key but different fingerprint, return 422 because the client has reused a key for a different request, which is a client bug you must surface rather than absorb. Retention should exceed the client's maximum retry window by a wide margin — Stripe uses 24 hours, and 24-72 hours is the common industry range; longer if your clients include batch systems that retry the next day.

Example

CREATE TABLE idempotency_keys (
  scope         text        NOT NULL,          -- tenant / api key
  key           text        NOT NULL,          -- client supplied (UUIDv7/ULID)
  endpoint      text        NOT NULL,
  request_hash  bytea       NOT NULL,          -- sha256 of canonical body
  state         text        NOT NULL,          -- in_flight | completed
  response_code int,
  response_body jsonb,
  locked_until  timestamptz,                   -- lease for the in-flight case
  created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  PRIMARY KEY (scope, key)
);
CREATE INDEX ON idempotency_keys (created_at);  -- for the 72h sweeper

Follow-ups

  • Who generates the key? — The client, once per logical intent (per checkout attempt, not per HTTP call). A UUIDv7 or ULID is ideal because it is unique and time-sortable, which makes the cleanup index cheap.
  • What if the first attempt crashed mid-processing? — The locked_until lease expires and the record returns to a retryable state; without a lease, an interrupted request wedges the key forever, which is the most common bug in home-grown implementations.
  • Do you need the request hash? — Yes, and interviewers probe it. Without it, a client that reuses a key for a different amount silently gets the old response and believes the second payment succeeded.

Common wrong answer

"We hash the request body and use that as the key." Two genuinely distinct payments — same customer, same amount, same minute — hash identically, so the second is silently swallowed. Content hashing is a defence against key misuse, never a substitute for a client-supplied intent identifier.

midExplain the dual-write problem.

Answer

Dual write is any handler that must update two systems that do not share a transaction — typically "save the order to the database and publish OrderPlaced to the broker". There is no ordering of the two writes that is safe: database first then publish loses the event if the process dies in between (the order exists, nobody downstream knows); publish first then database emits an event for something that never happened; and wrapping both in a distributed transaction requires XA, which needs both resources to support it, blocks on the coordinator, and is essentially extinct in cloud stacks. It matters because the failure is silent and only visible later as drift — a warehouse that never got the pick request, an analytics count that is 0.3% low. The fix is to make one system the source of truth and derive the other from it: transactional outbox, change data capture, or event sourcing. Naming the problem by its name and immediately offering the outbox is the answer that lands.

Example

// The bug, in the shape it usually ships
await _db.SaveChangesAsync();                // (1) committed
await _bus.PublishAsync(new OrderPlaced(id));// (2) process dies here
// -> order exists, no event, no error, nobody notices for six weeks

// and the mirror image, which is worse
await _bus.PublishAsync(new OrderPlaced(id));// (1) consumers act on it
await _db.SaveChangesAsync();                // (2) throws -> phantom order

Follow-ups

  • Does a try/catch fix it? — No: the failure mode is process death, network partition or a timeout where you do not know whether the publish landed. Compensation on catch is best effort and cannot cover a crash.
  • Is XA ever right? — Rarely, and mostly in legacy on-premises estates with a shared transaction manager (MSDTC, JTA over WebSphere MQ plus Db2). It costs blocking, coordinator recovery and vendor lock-in, and no mainstream cloud broker supports it.
  • What about publishing inside the DB transaction? — That is exactly the outbox: the "publish" is an insert into a table in the same transaction, and a separate relay does the real send.

Common wrong answer

"Publish first, and if the database write fails, publish a compensating event." Now you have two events, consumers who may have acted on the first, and a compensation that can itself fail. It converts a correctness problem into a business-logic problem for every downstream team.

midWalk through the transactional outbox pattern end to end.

Answer

In the same database transaction that changes state, insert a row into an outbox table containing the message id, aggregate id (used later as the partition key), type, payload, headers and a created timestamp. A separate relay process reads unsent rows in order, publishes them to the broker, and marks them sent — either by polling with SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, or by tailing the database log with CDC, which avoids polling entirely. Delivery is at-least-once by construction, because the relay can publish and then die before marking sent, so consumers must be idempotent; ordering per aggregate is preserved if the relay processes rows per aggregate in id order and publishes with the aggregate id as the key. The operational details that separate a good answer from a great one: index on (sent_at NULL, id) or a partial index so the sweep stays cheap, delete or archive sent rows aggressively (an outbox that grows unbounded becomes the slowest table in the database), and alert on outbox age, not just depth.

Example

CREATE TABLE outbox (
  id            bigserial PRIMARY KEY,
  aggregate_id  text        NOT NULL,      -- becomes the partition key
  type          text        NOT NULL,
  payload       jsonb       NOT NULL,
  headers       jsonb       NOT NULL,      -- traceparent, schema version
  created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  sent_at       timestamptz
);
CREATE INDEX outbox_unsent ON outbox (id) WHERE sent_at IS NULL;

-- relay, safe for N concurrent workers
UPDATE outbox SET sent_at = now()
WHERE id IN (SELECT id FROM outbox WHERE sent_at IS NULL
             ORDER BY id LIMIT 200 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED)
RETURNING id, aggregate_id, type, payload, headers;

Follow-ups

  • What is the latency cost? — One poll interval. Typical settings are 50-250ms, giving sub-second end-to-end; CDC-based relays cut it to tens of milliseconds because they react to the WAL.
  • How do you keep ordering with parallel relay workers? — Partition the sweep by a hash of aggregate_id so one worker owns each aggregate, or publish keyed and accept that only per-key ordering matters (which is usually the real requirement).
  • Does the outbox work with a document store? — Yes if the store has single-document atomicity: embed the outbox array in the aggregate document (MongoDB), or use the store's change stream as the relay.
  • Who cleans up? — A scheduled delete of rows sent more than a few hours ago, or partition-by-day plus DROP PARTITION, which avoids bloat and vacuum pressure entirely.

Common wrong answer

"The outbox gives exactly-once delivery." It gives at-least-once with no loss: the relay can crash after the broker accepted the message and before the row is marked sent, and it will republish. Candidates who claim exactly-once here then design consumers with no dedup, which is the actual bug.

seniorOutbox polling, CDC, or listen-to-yourself: which do you pick?

Answer

Polling outbox is the default: no extra infrastructure, works with any database, easy to reason about and to test, and the cost is a small constant query load plus poll-interval latency. CDC (Debezium reading the Postgres WAL, MySQL binlog, SQL Server change tables) removes the polling and gives the lowest latency and zero application-side relay, at the cost of running Kafka Connect or Debezium Server, managing replication slots — an inactive slot will fill your disk and take the primary down, which is the war story to have ready — and handling schema changes and snapshots. Listen-to-yourself (publish first, then have your own consumer write the database) inverts the problem and gives you a single write path, but it makes your own state eventually consistent with your own API, which surprises product teams and complicates read-your-writes. My rule: start with the polling outbox; move to CDC when either the latency budget goes below ~100ms or the number of services doing outbox relays makes a platform-level solution cheaper; use listen-to-yourself only where the service genuinely is a stream processor.

  • Why use CDC on the outbox table instead of on the business tables? — Because the outbox row is a designed event with a stable schema, whereas the business table is your internal storage. Debezium ships an outbox event router transform for exactly this; it is the pattern to name.

Common wrong answer

"CDC is strictly better, it is just the outbox without the polling." CDC couples you to the database's physical log and its operational rules: slot management, snapshot storms on restart, schema-change handling, and a connector that must be upgraded alongside the database. Teams routinely regret adopting it before they had someone to operate it.

seniorHow does Kafka's idempotent producer actually work?

Answer

The producer obtains a producer id (PID) from the broker and attaches, to every batch, that PID, the partition, and a monotonically increasing sequence number per partition. The partition leader keeps the last five sequence numbers per PID per partition; if a retry arrives with a sequence it has already written it acknowledges it as a duplicate without appending, and if a sequence arrives out of order it rejects the batch with OutOfOrderSequenceException. That is what makes producer retries safe and why max.in.flight.requests.per.connection may be up to 5 without reordering — the broker enforces order, not the client. It has been the default since Kafka 3.0 (enable.idempotence=true, with acks=all and retries=Integer.MAX_VALUE implied), so modern clusters get it for free. Its limits are exactly what interviewers probe: it is per-producer-session and per-partition, so a producer restart gets a new PID and cannot deduplicate against the previous session, and it does nothing about a duplicate your application generates by re-reading and re-sending.

Example

# defaults on a modern client (Kafka 3.0+) - do not fight them
enable.idempotence=true
acks=all
retries=2147483647
max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=5

# the case idempotence does NOT cover:
# process restarts -> new PID -> the batch it re-sends after recovery
# is a genuine duplicate to the broker. Consumer-side dedup still required.

Follow-ups

  • What is transactional.id for then? — It gives the producer a stable identity across restarts, so the broker can fence the old instance (epoch bump) and abort its in-flight transaction. That, not idempotence, is what makes recovery deterministic.
  • Why did older guidance say max.in.flight=1? — Before idempotence (pre-0.11), a failed batch could be retried after a later batch succeeded, reordering records. With idempotence enabled, up to 5 is safe and much faster.
  • What is OutOfOrderSequenceException telling you? — Usually that the broker lost state for the PID: a segment was deleted, the topic's transactional.id.expiration.ms or producer-id expiry elapsed, or an unclean leader election occurred. It is a signal to investigate, not to catch and ignore.

Common wrong answer

"The idempotent producer means Kafka deduplicates messages." It deduplicates producer retries within one session per partition using sequence numbers. It has no idea that two different producer instances, or the same instance after a restart, sent semantically identical records.

seniorWhat do Kafka transactions cover, and what do they cost?

Answer

A Kafka transaction makes a set of writes across partitions, plus the consumer offset commits for the input, atomic — so consume-transform-produce becomes all-or-nothing and a downstream reader with isolation.level=read_committed never sees the aborted side. Mechanically, the producer registers a transactional.id, the transaction coordinator writes begin/commit markers into the log, and consumers filter aborted records using the last stable offset. That is the entire scope: it is Kafka-to-Kafka. It does not include your database, an HTTP call or an email, and it does not survive a consumer that reads with read_uncommitted. The costs are real and worth quoting: extra round trips per transaction, control records in the log, a latency floor set by transaction size (batching many records per transaction amortises it), and read_committed consumers cannot read past an open transaction, so one stuck producer stalls consumers until transaction.timeout.ms (default 60s) expires. Kafka 4.2 also finalised the stronger transaction protocol work (KIP-890) that hardens against hanging transactions.

Example

producer.initTransactions();                 // fences any previous instance
while (true) {
    var records = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(200));
    producer.beginTransaction();
    for (var r : records) producer.send(transform(r));
    producer.sendOffsetsToTransaction(offsetsOf(records), consumer.groupMetadata());
    producer.commitTransaction();            // offsets + outputs commit atomically
}
// consumer side of the DOWNSTREAM topic must set:
//   isolation.level=read_committed   (default is read_uncommitted)

Follow-ups

  • When are Kafka transactions clearly worth it? — Kafka Streams with processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2: you get atomic state-store updates plus output plus offsets with one config line, and the framework manages the transactional ids.
  • What is a hanging transaction? — A producer that died between begin and commit leaves an open transaction that blocks read_committed consumers at the last stable offset until it times out or is aborted. It is the classic "consumer lag is huge but throughput is zero" incident.
  • Does EOS help if the sink is Postgres? — No. Then you want the inbox pattern: dedup on message id in the same Postgres transaction as the effect, and commit the Kafka offset afterwards.

Common wrong answer

"Enable transactions and you get exactly-once end to end." Enabling the producer side while consumers stay on the default read_uncommitted gives you the cost with none of the benefit — aborted records are still delivered. Mention the consumer setting unprompted; interviewers watch for it.

seniorDescribe the inbox pattern and where the transaction boundary sits.

Answer

The inbox is the consumer-side mirror of the outbox: before doing the work, the handler inserts the incoming message id into a processed_messages table with a unique constraint, in the same database transaction as the business effect. If the insert conflicts, the message has already been handled and the handler acks without repeating the effect; if the transaction commits, both the effect and the record of having done it are durable together. The broker acknowledgement happens after the commit, which means a crash between the commit and the ack causes redelivery — and the inbox absorbs it, which is exactly the point. Two details separate a working implementation from a broken one: the unique key should be (message id, handler) so that a topic with several independent consumers does not have them dedup each other, and the transaction must be the same connection and transaction as the effect — an inbox in a separate store reintroduces the dual-write problem you were trying to solve.

Example

await using var tx = await conn.BeginTransactionAsync();
var inserted = await conn.ExecuteAsync(
    """
    INSERT INTO processed_messages (message_id, handler, processed_at)
    VALUES (@id, @handler, now()) ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING
    """, new { id = msg.Id, handler = nameof(ReserveStock) }, tx);

if (inserted == 1)                          // first time: do the work
    await ReserveStockAsync(msg, conn, tx);

await tx.CommitAsync();                     // effect + dedup together
await consumer.AckAsync(msg);               // crash here -> redelivered, absorbed

Follow-ups

  • What if the effect is in a different database from the inbox? — Then you are back to a dual write. Either move the inbox into the effect's store, or make the effect itself conditional (a conditional write keyed by message id) so replay is harmless.
  • Does this work for a handler that calls an external API? — Partially: claim the inbox row, call the API with its own idempotency key, then commit. The external key is what protects the leg you do not control.
  • How does this interact with ordering? — Not at all by itself: dedup does not restore order. If the effect is order-sensitive you also need a per-entity version check that rejects out-of-order application.

Common wrong answer

"We keep processed ids in Redis." Redis is a different store, so the write is not atomic with the effect and can be lost on failover with the default asynchronous replication. It is a legitimate optimisation in front of a durable inbox, never the durable record itself.

seniorHow long do you keep dedup state, and how do you bound it?

Answer

The retention floor is the maximum window in which a duplicate can still arrive, which is the broker's redelivery and retention behaviour plus your own replay policy — so it is set by an operational decision, not by taste. For an at-least-once consumer of a queue with a 4-hour message TTL, a day of dedup state is generous; for a Kafka topic with 7-day retention where you might reset an offset during an incident, you need 7 days or you must accept that a replay will double-apply. Bound it structurally rather than with a delete job: partition the table by day and drop partitions, or use a TTL-native store, or fold the dedup into the entity itself as a "last processed message id" or version column so there is no separate table to grow. State the trade explicitly — shorter retention means a replay outside the window silently double-applies, so the retention number and the replay runbook must agree, and that agreement should be written down.

Example

-- Option A: partition by day, drop old partitions (cheap, no vacuum churn)
CREATE TABLE processed_messages (
  message_id text, handler text, processed_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (handler, message_id, processed_at)
) PARTITION BY RANGE (processed_at);

-- Option B: no table at all - dedup folded into the aggregate
UPDATE orders
   SET status = 'shipped', last_event_version = @version
 WHERE id = @id AND last_event_version < @version;   -- 0 rows = duplicate/stale

Follow-ups

  • Which is better, a dedup table or a version column? — The version column when the events are per-entity and ordered, because it handles duplicates and out-of-order delivery in one predicate and costs no extra storage. The table when messages are not tied to a single aggregate.
  • What about a Bloom filter? — Fine as a fast negative check in front of the real store; never alone, because a false positive means silently dropping a message that was never processed.
  • How do you handle the replay-outside-the-window case? — Make it a deliberate operation: quiesce the consumer, use a marker so the handler knows it is in replay mode, or reset downstream state and rebuild rather than double-applying.

Common wrong answer

"We keep them forever, storage is cheap." The storage is cheap; the index is not. A billion-row dedup table with a random-UUID primary key will have terrible cache locality and will slow down every message you process. If you keep everything, at least key it time-sortably (ULID/UUIDv7) and partition it.

principalYou discover the guarantee has already been violated: 40,000 duplicate charges shipped last night. Now what?

Answer

Sequence it: stop the bleeding, quantify, notify, repair, prevent. Stop means disable the path or the consumer immediately — a paused queue is recoverable, a growing financial error is not — and take the decision in one minute rather than debating root cause. Quantify from the durable record, not from logs: query the ledger for the exact set of affected transactions with amounts and customers, because every downstream decision needs that list and it must be reproducible. Notify in parallel — finance, support and the regulator if the domain requires it — with the count, the window and the expected repair time, before customers find it themselves. Repair with an idempotent, dry-runnable reversal job that writes its own audit trail and processes in batches you can halt. Prevent with the control that would have caught it in minutes: a continuous invariant check (charges per order must equal one) with an alert, not just a unit test. At principal level the extra thing interviewers listen for is that you name the decision you would take without more data, and that the blameless postmortem targets the missing detection, not the engineer who missed the unique index.

Example

T+0   pause consumer group / disable feature flag        (1 command, no debate)
T+5   ledger query -> exact list, count, total exposure  (reproducible)
T+10  incident channel: scope, impact, ETA; page finance + support leads
T+20  root cause enough to know it will not recur on resume
T+30  reversal job: dry-run count matches, then run in batches of 500
T+60  resume consumers with the fix and the invariant alarm live
T+1d  postmortem: why detection took 9 hours, not why the bug existed

Follow-ups

  • How do you avoid a second incident during repair? — The reversal job is itself idempotent and keyed by the original transaction id, dry-runnable, rate-limited, and restartable from its own checkpoint.
  • What is the detection control you would add? — A continuous invariant: for every order, count of successful charges = 1, evaluated on a rolling window with an alert on any violation. Reconciliation that runs monthly is an audit, not detection.
  • What do you tell the team the next morning? — That the system allowed it, so the system is what changes: unique constraint at the storage layer, invariant alarm, and a game-day exercise replaying the duplicate scenario.

Common wrong answer

Starting with root-cause analysis. Interviewers running an incident-command question are timing how long it takes you to say "stop the bleeding". Diagnosing first while charges keep going out is the answer that fails the exercise, however good the eventual diagnosis is.

04Ordering, partitioning and keys

Ordering is the guarantee people assume they have and discover they do not, usually in production, usually as "the update was applied before the create". Interviewers like this unit because it has a clean screener question, a mechanical middle (how a key maps to a partition) and a genuinely hard senior end (global ordering, hot keys, order-tolerant consumers). At lead level you are expected to push back on requirements: most "we need strict ordering" requirements are really "we need per-entity ordering", and knowing the difference is worth an order of magnitude in cost.

screenerWhat ordering guarantee does a partitioned log actually give you?

Answer

Ordering is guaranteed within a partition and nowhere else. Kafka assigns each record an offset within its partition and a consumer reads that partition in offset order, so records with the same key — which by default hash to the same partition — are seen in the order they were produced. Across partitions there is no ordering at all: two records produced a millisecond apart to different partitions can be consumed minutes apart, because the consumer polls partitions independently and the partitions may be on different brokers with different lag. This is why the design instruction is always "key by the entity whose ordering you care about": customer id, account id, aggregate id. The corollary, which the follow-up usually probes, is that ordering also depends on producer settings — with idempotence off and multiple in-flight requests, a retried batch can land after a later one and reorder within the partition too.

Example

topic orders, 3 partitions, key = orderId

p0: [o1.created@10] [o1.paid@11] [o1.shipped@14]   <- ordered, same key
p1: [o2.created@10] [o2.paid@12]
p2: [o3.created@10]

Consumer sees o1 events in order. It may see o3.created AFTER o1.shipped.
There is no "global" order and asking for one costs a single partition.

Follow-ups

  • Does a null key break ordering? — Yes for that entity: null-keyed records are distributed across partitions (sticky partitioning since Kafka 2.4 batches them per partition rather than round-robining every record), so two events for the same entity can land on different partitions.
  • Do RabbitMQ queues preserve order? — A single queue with a single consumer, yes. Add a second consumer and requeues, and order is gone; a message that is nacked and requeued goes back to the head or the tail depending on configuration, which reorders relative to messages already delivered.
  • What about a consumer with a thread pool? — Fetching in order and then dispatching to N threads discards the guarantee entirely. If you parallelise, shard the work by key inside the consumer and keep one thread per key-shard.

Common wrong answer

"Kafka guarantees ordering." Unqualified, this is wrong, and the interviewer's next sentence is "across partitions too?". Always say "per partition, and therefore per key given the default partitioner" — it is four extra words that change the grade.

midHow do you choose a partition key?

Answer

Pick the identifier of the thing whose ordering and consistency you need, then check it against three properties: cardinality (must be much larger than the partition count, ideally by 100x, or partitions sit empty), distribution (roughly uniform, so no partition gets a disproportionate share), and stability (it must not change over the entity's lifetime, or the entity's events split across partitions). For most business systems the answer is the aggregate id — order id, account id, device id — and the tie-breaker between candidates is which one groups the events that must be processed together. Where the natural key is skewed, compose it: tenantId#bucket where bucket is a hash of a sub-entity gives ordering within a bucket and spreads a large tenant across partitions, at the cost of losing tenant-wide ordering — a trade you must state. Avoid keys that encode time (all today's traffic lands on one partition) and avoid the user's country or region unless you genuinely want the skew that produces.

Example

// Kafka default partitioner: murmur2(keyBytes) % numPartitions
// Same key -> same partition, deterministically, for a fixed partition count.

var key = order.Id;                       // good: high cardinality, stable
var key = order.Status;                   // bad: 5 values, massive skew
var key = DateTime.UtcNow.ToString("d");  // bad: one hot partition per day
var key = $"{tenantId}#{orderId.GetHashCode() & 0xF}";  // skew relief, 16 buckets
                                          //   -> ordering per bucket, not per tenant

Follow-ups

  • What if the key is a UUIDv4? — Distribution is perfect and stability is fine; the risk is that it may not group the events you need together — a per-event UUID gives no ordering at all, so key by the aggregate's id, not the event's.
  • Do you ever want no key? — Yes: pure telemetry and logs where order is irrelevant and even distribution matters more. Sticky partitioning makes null-key throughput excellent.
  • How do you validate a key choice before shipping? — Replay a day of production ids through the partitioner offline and plot the histogram. It takes twenty minutes and prevents the hot-partition incident.

Common wrong answer

"Use the message id so it spreads evenly." Even spread is only one of the three properties; a unique-per-message key destroys the very grouping partitions exist to provide, so the consumer can process a delete before the create for the same entity.

midWhat happens to ordering when you add partitions to a live topic?

Answer

The partitioner is hash(key) % partitionCount, so changing the count remaps most keys: an entity whose events were going to partition 3 starts going to partition 7, while its history stays in 3. For a period equal to the consumer's lag, two partitions hold events for the same key and there is no ordering between them — which is how a "shipped" event overtakes a "paid" event. Kafka never moves existing data when you add partitions, and it does not let you remove partitions at all. The safe procedures are: add partitions during a quiet window after draining consumer lag to zero (acceptable for many systems and the pragmatic default), or create a new topic with the target partition count and migrate producers and consumers with a dual-read cutover (correct, and what you propose when ordering is contractual). If the topic is compacted the problem is worse, because the compaction key's history is stranded on the old partition and a rebuild from the topic will produce the wrong state.

Example

key "cust-42": murmur2 % 6  = 3      before
               murmur2 % 12 = 9      after adding 6 partitions

p3: [created] [paid]        <- old events, still being consumed
p9: [shipped]               <- new events, consumed by a different member
Result: "shipped" can be processed before "paid". Silent, data-dependent.

Follow-ups

  • Can you avoid the remap? — With a custom partitioner that uses consistent hashing or an explicit key-to-partition map, yes, but then you own the mapping forever and every client language needs the same implementation. Most teams choose the new-topic migration instead.
  • How do you decide the initial partition count? — Target peak throughput divided by realistic per-partition throughput (10-50 MB/s is typical, but consumer processing usually binds first), rounded up, then doubled for headroom, and cross-checked against consumer parallelism needs.
  • What about compacted topics specifically? — Treat repartitioning as a rebuild: produce the compacted state into the new topic before cutting over, or accept a full re-derivation from the source of truth.

Common wrong answer

"Adding partitions is an online operation, so it is safe." It is online in the sense that it does not stop the broker; it is not safe in the sense that it silently breaks per-key ordering for the duration of consumer lag. The distinction is exactly what the question tests.

seniorOne partition is at 90% utilisation and the rest are idle. Diagnose and fix.

Answer

That is key skew, and the diagnosis is quick: compare per-partition message rate and byte rate, then sample the keys on the hot partition. There are three usual causes — a genuinely dominant entity (one tenant, one device, one popular product), a low-cardinality key (status, country, type), or a bug producing a constant or null-ish key. The fixes differ. For a dominant entity, split the key with a bucket suffix so the entity spreads across N partitions, accepting the loss of ordering across its buckets, or route it to a dedicated topic with its own consumers so its load is isolated. For low cardinality, change the key — that is a new-topic migration. For a bug, fix the producer. What you must add unprompted is the consumer-side consequence: a hot partition means one consumer instance is the bottleneck no matter how many you add, so lag on that partition grows without any global signal, which is why per-partition lag must be a first-class dashboard, not an aggregate.

Follow-ups

  • Does increasing partitions help? — No. The hot key still hashes to exactly one partition; you have just added empty ones. This is the trap in the question.
  • What is the cost of bucketing a key? — Loss of ordering across that entity's buckets, so it only works when ordering is required per sub-entity (per order, not per tenant) or when the handler is order-tolerant.
  • Any broker-side help? — Kafka 4.3 added per-partition size metrics (part of the operator-focused release) which make skew visible without scraping; a share group also sidesteps consumer-count limits, but not the single-partition write bottleneck.

Common wrong answer

"Scale out the consumer group." The most common answer and the wrong one: a partition is consumed by exactly one member of a group, so extra consumers idle while the hot partition stays at one. Say "the ceiling is one consumer per partition" and move to key design.

seniorThe requirement says "strict global ordering". How do you respond?

Answer

First interrogate the requirement, because global ordering is almost never what is needed and it costs you all parallelism: a single partition, a single consumer, no horizontal scale, and a throughput ceiling set by one machine. Ask what breaks if two unrelated entities are processed out of order — usually nothing, and the real requirement is per-entity ordering, which partitioning gives for free. If some cross-entity ordering genuinely matters (a ledger where balances must be sequenced, a replicated state machine), the options are: a single partition or single-writer with the accepted throughput cap; a sequencer service that assigns a global monotonic number and lets consumers buffer and reorder; or a total-order broadcast built on consensus (Raft), which is what etcd and a Kafka controller do internally and which caps out around tens of thousands of ops per second. State the numbers: a single Kafka partition realistically sustains tens of MB/s and a single-threaded consumer maybe a few thousand messages per second with database work, which is either plenty or a non-starter — and that comparison is the decision.

Example

Ladder, cheapest first:
1. per-key order        key by entity, N partitions        <- 95% of cases
2. per-group order      composite key (tenant#bucket)      <- skew relief
3. windowed order       buffer + sort by event time,       <- late data policy
                        emit after a watermark
4. global order         1 partition / 1 writer / consensus <- last resort

Say out loud what you give up at each step; that is what is being graded.

Follow-ups

  • What does a sequencer cost? — It becomes a single point of coordination and failure; it needs its own HA (leader election plus fencing) and it caps throughput at whatever one leader can stamp, typically 10^4-10^5 per second.
  • Can you fake global order with timestamps? — Only approximately, and only with a bounded-skew clock and a watermark delay. With NTP-quality clocks (millisecond-to-tens-of-milliseconds skew) you can sort within a window; you cannot claim strict order.
  • What if the consumer must apply to a single database row anyway? — Then order is enforced by the database with a version check, and the broker only needs to deliver eventually. That reframing dissolves many "global ordering" requirements.

Common wrong answer

"Use one partition." It is technically an answer but it accepts the requirement without challenging it, and it hides a hard throughput ceiling that will be discovered in production. The graded behaviour is asking what the ordering is for before pricing it.

seniorExplain consistent hashing and why virtual nodes exist.

Answer

With modulo hashing, changing the number of nodes remaps almost every key — adding one node to a ten-node cluster moves roughly 90% of keys, which in a cache means a total miss storm and in a data store means moving nearly all the data. Consistent hashing maps both keys and nodes onto a ring; a key belongs to the first node clockwise from it, so adding or removing a node only moves the keys in that node's arc, about 1/N of the data. The problem with the naive ring is imbalance: with few nodes, arcs are very uneven, and removing a node dumps its entire share onto exactly one neighbour. Virtual nodes fix both — each physical node is placed at many points on the ring (100-256 is typical), so load evens out statistically and a departing node's keys are redistributed across many peers rather than one. It is the mechanism behind Dynamo, Cassandra's token ranges, Riak and most sharded caches, and the modern refinement worth naming is bounded-load consistent hashing, which caps any node at a factor of the average.

Example

modulo:      hash(k) % 10 -> add a node -> hash(k) % 11
             ~90% of keys move.  Cache hit rate falls off a cliff.

ring:        nodes at points on a 2^32 ring, key -> next node clockwise
             add a node -> only its arc moves (~1/N of keys)

vnodes:      each node owns 128 arcs instead of 1
             -> load variance shrinks ~sqrt(vnodes)
             -> a failed node's load spreads over ~128 neighbours

Follow-ups

  • Where does rendezvous hashing fit? — Same goal, simpler: for each key compute a score per node and take the maximum. No ring, no vnodes, uniform by construction; the cost is O(N) per lookup, which is fine for tens or hundreds of nodes.
  • Does Kafka use consistent hashing? — No: partition assignment is hash(key) % partitions, which is exactly why adding partitions remaps keys. Kafka trades remap safety for a trivially reproducible mapping across all clients.
  • What is the failure mode of vnodes in a database? — Repair and streaming cost: more token ranges means more, smaller streams during rebuild, and Cassandra reduced its default from 256 to 16 vnodes for precisely that reason.

Common wrong answer

"Consistent hashing means the same key always goes to the same node." That is true of modulo hashing too. The property that matters is minimal disruption under membership change, and if you cannot state the 1/N versus ~all figure you have not understood why it exists.

seniorHow do you make a consumer tolerant of out-of-order events?

Answer

Three techniques, applied in increasing order of cost. First, make the state transition conditional on a version or sequence carried in the event: apply only if the incoming version is greater than the stored one, which turns a stale duplicate or an overtaking event into a no-op — this covers most cases and costs one column. Second, make the update commutative or last-writer-wins on a field basis, so applying in any order converges; per-field timestamps or a CRDT do this and are how collaborative editors survive. Third, buffer and reorder: hold events in a per-key window keyed by sequence and release them in order, with a timeout that decides what happens to a gap — this is the only technique that can enforce order, and it costs memory, latency and a policy for the message that never arrives. In practice you should also make the events themselves self-describing enough to apply out of order: full-state events ("the address is now X") are naturally order-tolerant, whereas deltas ("append a line") are not.

Example

// 1. version guard - the cheapest and most common
var rows = await db.ExecuteAsync(
  "UPDATE shipment SET status=@s, version=@v WHERE id=@id AND version < @v",
  new { s = e.Status, v = e.Version, id = e.Id });
if (rows == 0) _log.LogDebug("stale or duplicate {Id}@{V}", e.Id, e.Version);

// 3. gap buffering - only when order is genuinely mandatory
if (e.Seq != expected + 1) {
    _buffer.Add(e);                       // hold
    if (_buffer.Age > gapTimeout) await EscalateGapAsync(expected + 1);
    return;
}

Follow-ups

  • What do you do with a permanent gap? — Decide it in advance: skip and alert, or halt the key and page. Silently waiting forever is the failure mode that turns into a one-key outage nobody notices.
  • Where does the version number come from? — The producing aggregate's own version, incremented per state change. A broker offset is not a substitute: it is per-partition and changes on replay to a new topic.
  • Does event-carried state transfer solve it? — Largely: if every event carries the full current state plus a version, consumers become last-writer-wins per entity and out-of-order delivery is harmless. The cost is bigger messages and more coupling to the producer's model.

Common wrong answer

"Sort by the event timestamp." Producer clocks are not synchronised, so two events milliseconds apart can carry timestamps in the wrong order; and a timestamp does not tell you whether you are missing an event in between. Use a monotonic per-entity version, not wall-clock time.

seniorHow do SQS FIFO queues and Azure Service Bus sessions provide ordering, and what does it cost?

Answer

Both give you ordering within a named group rather than globally, which is the same idea as a partition key with different vocabulary. SQS FIFO uses a MessageGroupId: messages within a group are delivered strictly in order and only one in-flight batch per group is released at a time, so a stuck message blocks its group but not the queue; it also offers server-side deduplication via MessageDeduplicationId or content hashing over a fixed five-minute window. The costs are throughput and blocking — historically 300 messages/second per FIFO queue, or 3,000 with batching, with high-throughput mode raising it substantially by allowing more concurrent groups, and always fewer than standard SQS's effectively unlimited rate. Azure Service Bus sessions work similarly: a session id groups messages, and a consumer takes an exclusive lock on the session, which gives ordering plus per-session state, at the cost of one consumer per session and lock-renewal management. The senior point is identical in both: ordering is bought with a serialisation point, and your throughput becomes (groups in flight) x (per-group rate).

Example

SQS FIFO
  MessageGroupId       = customer-42        -> order within the customer
  MessageDeduplicationId =       -> 5-minute server-side dedup
  in-flight: one batch per group; a poison message blocks THAT group

Azure Service Bus
  SessionId = customer-42                   -> exclusive session lock
  SessionState available per session (small state bag)
  needs lock renewal; an abandoned session waits for the lock timeout

Follow-ups

  • What is the failure mode of a group with a poison message? — Head-of-line blocking for that group only. You need a per-group DLQ policy and an alarm on group age, or one bad message freezes one customer indefinitely.
  • Is the 5-minute SQS dedup window enough? — Only if your retries complete inside it. For anything slower, keep application-level idempotency; the broker window is a convenience, not the guarantee.
  • How do you size groups? — Many small groups, never few large ones: throughput scales with concurrent groups, and one big group is a hot partition with a different name.

Common wrong answer

"FIFO queues give exactly-once and full ordering." They give ordering per message group and deduplication within a five-minute window — both bounded. Candidates who quote the marketing line usually then design a single group for the whole queue, which caps the system at a few hundred messages per second.

principalYou need per-customer ordering, but one customer is 30% of the traffic. Design it.

Answer

Start by separating the requirement from the default: ordering is needed per customer, and this one customer's volume means a single-partition-per-customer model gives them a queue that cannot keep up while everyone else idles. The design I would present has three parts. Isolate the whale: route that customer to a dedicated topic (or a dedicated queue with its own consumer deployment) so their load and their incidents do not touch the shared path — this also gives you an independent scaling and retention knob and, importantly, a blast radius boundary. Within the whale's stream, find the real ordering unit: it is almost never the customer, it is the customer's order, account or device, so re-key to that and you regain parallelism with the ordering that actually matters. Where genuine per-customer serialisation remains (a running balance, say), narrow it to a single-writer step over just those events, sized so it fits in one partition, and keep everything else parallel. Then make the routing dynamic — a tenant-to-topic map in configuration, not in code — because next quarter a different customer will be the whale, and add per-tenant lag and rate dashboards so you see the next one coming.

Example

shared path        events            24 partitions, key = customerId
whale path         events.acme       48 partitions, key = acme#accountId
serialised step    acme.ledger        1 partition,  key = acme  (balance only)

routing: tenants.yaml -> {acme: events.acme, "*": events}
guardrails: per-tenant produce quota, per-tenant lag alert, per-topic retention
next whale: promote by config change + backfill, no code deploy

Follow-ups

  • How do you migrate the whale onto its own topic without loss or reorder? — Dual-publish to both topics, let the new consumer catch up from the migration point, stop consuming the whale's keys on the shared path once the new consumer's lag is zero, then stop dual-publishing. State the window where both are live and why it is safe (idempotent handlers).
  • What stops the next whale from repeating this? — Per-tenant quotas (Kafka client quotas by principal or client-id) so one tenant cannot consume the shared cluster's capacity, plus an automated alert when any tenant crosses a share threshold.
  • What is the organisational cost? — A second deployment, a second dashboard set, and a routing decision someone must own. Say it: whale isolation is a real operational surface, justified by the blast-radius argument, not by throughput alone.

Common wrong answer

"Give them more partitions." Partitions do not help a single key, and giving the shared topic more partitions helps least of all. The interviewer is testing whether you re-examine the ordering unit and whether you think about isolation, not whether you can turn a dial.

05Failure, retries, timeouts and backpressure

This is the unit that separates people who have run systems from people who have read about them, and interviewers know it — the questions are deliberately shaped as symptoms ("lag is growing", "the service recovered and immediately fell over again") rather than definitions. Screener depth is "what is a timeout for"; senior depth is retry budgets, cascading failure and load shedding; principal depth is defining the SLO for an asynchronous pipeline and deciding what to sacrifice under load. Have two or three real incidents ready, with numbers.

screenerHow do you choose a timeout?

Answer

From the measured latency distribution of the callee and from the caller's own deadline, not from a round number. The usual starting point is a little above the callee's p99 — often p99 plus 50-100% — so that normal slow requests succeed and genuinely stuck ones are cut loose quickly; then check it against the caller's total budget, because a timeout longer than the caller's own deadline is dead code. The part candidates miss is that timeouts must be propagated: pass a deadline down the call chain (gRPC deadlines, a CancellationToken, an X-Request-Deadline header) so a downstream service does not spend three seconds computing an answer the caller abandoned two seconds ago. Also distinguish connect, read/write and total timeouts; a common production bug is a generous total timeout with no connect timeout, so a black-holed host consumes the full budget doing nothing. And every timeout needs a default: infinite is the default in far too many clients and is never right.

Example

// Deadline propagation beats per-hop guessing
var budget = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2);                   // user-facing SLA
using var cts = CancellationTokenSource.CreateLinkedTokenSource(ct);
cts.CancelAfter(budget);

var http = new HttpClient {
    Timeout = Timeout.InfiniteTimeSpan                  // let the token decide
};
// SocketsHttpHandler: ConnectTimeout = 500ms, PooledConnectionLifetime = 2min
var resp = await http.GetAsync(url, cts.Token);         // total budget enforced

Follow-ups

  • What happens if every hop uses the same timeout? — The outermost caller times out first while inner hops keep working, so you pay full cost for discarded work and your retry storm multiplies it. Budgets should shrink as you go deeper.
  • Does a timeout tell you the work did not happen? — No, and this is the central fact of the whole topic: a timeout is "unknown", not "failed". Which is why retrying requires idempotency.
  • How do you choose a timeout for a brand-new dependency? — Ship a conservative one with high observability, measure for a week, then tighten. Never leave it unset while you gather data.

Common wrong answer

"We use 30 seconds everywhere." A uniform, generous timeout is the same as no timeout: threads and connections pile up, the pool exhausts, and one slow dependency takes down a service that could have shed the calls in 200ms. Interviewers follow up with "what is your thread pool size?" to make the arithmetic visible.

midExponential backoff with jitter — why is the jitter the important part?

Answer

Exponential backoff spaces retries out so a struggling dependency is not hammered; jitter is what stops all the clients from retrying at the same instant. Without jitter, a dependency that fails for every caller at time T produces synchronised retries at T+1, T+2, T+4 — the load arrives in spikes exactly as tall as the original, and the dependency never gets a quiet window to recover. Full jitter (sleep a uniformly random time between 0 and the current cap) is the variant AWS recommends and the one with the best measured behaviour on both total work and completion time; "equal jitter" (half the delay plus a random half) is a reasonable compromise when you want a guaranteed minimum wait. Two things must accompany it: a maximum delay (so retries do not drift into minutes) and a maximum attempt count or, better, a deadline — retrying past the caller's budget is pure waste.

Example

// Full jitter: sleep = random(0, min(cap, base * 2^attempt))
static TimeSpan Delay(int attempt) => TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(
    Random.Shared.Next(0, (int)Math.Min(30_000, 100 * Math.Pow(2, attempt))));

// Polly v8 equivalent
new ResiliencePipelineBuilder().AddRetry(new RetryStrategyOptions {
    MaxRetryAttempts = 5,
    BackoffType = DelayBackoffType.Exponential,
    UseJitter = true,                       // do not omit this
    Delay = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(100),
    MaxDelay = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30)
}).Build();

Follow-ups

  • Which errors do you retry? — Only those a retry can fix: connection failures, 429, 502/503/504, deadlocks and serialisation failures. Never 400, 401, 403, 404, 422, or a validation error — retrying those is pure load with a guaranteed failure.
  • Do you retry a timeout? — Only if the operation is idempotent or carries an idempotency key, because a timeout might mean it succeeded.
  • Should the client or the server decide the delay? — Prefer the server when it can: honour Retry-After, because the server knows its own recovery state and the client is guessing.

Common wrong answer

"Retry three times with a one-second delay." Fixed delays synchronise clients, and three immediate retries multiply load by four at precisely the worst moment. The absence of the word "jitter" is a reliable signal that the candidate has not been on the receiving end of a retry storm.

midExplain the circuit breaker, its states, and when it is the wrong tool.

Answer

A circuit breaker tracks the failure rate of calls to one dependency and, when it crosses a threshold over a sampling window, opens: subsequent calls fail immediately without touching the network. After a break duration it goes half-open and lets a small number of trial calls through; success closes it, failure reopens it. The value is twofold — the caller stops wasting threads and latency on something that is down, and the failing dependency gets a quiet period in which to recover, which retries alone will deny it. It is the wrong tool when the dependency is not shared (a per-tenant or per-key failure trips the breaker for everyone), when failure is partial (some requests fine, some not — you want a per-endpoint or per-shard breaker), and when there is nothing sensible to do while open. The trap is configuring it to trip on a small sample: a threshold of "50% of the last 10 calls" on a low-traffic path will open on ordinary noise, so require a minimum throughput before the breaker is allowed to evaluate at all.

Example

new ResiliencePipelineBuilder()
  .AddCircuitBreaker(new CircuitBreakerStrategyOptions {
      FailureRatio       = 0.5,                       // 50% failures
      MinimumThroughput  = 20,                        // ...over >= 20 calls
      SamplingDuration   = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30),
      BreakDuration      = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15),  // then half-open
  })
  .AddTimeout(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2))                // order matters:
  .Build();                                           // timeout INSIDE breaker

Follow-ups

  • What do you serve while the breaker is open? — A cached or default value, a degraded response, or a fast, honest error. A breaker with no fallback converts slow failures into fast failures, which is still progress but only half the win.
  • Breaker or bulkhead? — Complementary: the bulkhead caps concurrent calls so one dependency cannot consume all your threads; the breaker stops calling it at all. Use both.
  • Does a breaker belong in a message consumer? — Yes, and pair it with pausing consumption: when the breaker opens, pause the partition or stop prefetching, so you stop dequeuing work you cannot do and the backlog stays in the broker where it is durable.

Common wrong answer

"Add a circuit breaker" as a universal reliability answer. A breaker on a non-shared, per-request failure mode is actively harmful — it converts one tenant's bad data into an outage for all tenants. Ask what the breaker is protecting before adding one.

midWhat is backpressure, and how do you apply it in a message pipeline?

Answer

Backpressure is the signal that flows upstream when a consumer cannot keep up, and the design question is always "where does the queue form and who feels it". In a pull-based system it is implicit: the consumer stops polling, and the backlog sits durably in the broker, which is the ideal place for it. In a push-based system you must create it — prefetch limits in AMQP, credit-based flow control in AMQP 1.0 and gRPC, demand in reactive streams — or the buffer moves into the consumer's memory and it dies with an OOM instead of slowing down. Inside a service the same rule applies: bounded channels rather than unbounded ones, and a full channel that blocks or rejects rather than growing. What to say at senior level is the failure taxonomy: unbounded buffering converts a throughput problem into a crash; dropping converts it into data loss; blocking converts it into latency and eventually into upstream timeouts — so choose deliberately per path and make the choice visible in a metric.

Example

// bounded channel: the pipeline slows down instead of exploding
var ch = Channel.CreateBounded<Work>(new BoundedChannelOptions(1_000) {
    FullMode = BoundedChannelFullMode.Wait     // Wait | DropOldest | DropWrite
});

// consumer-side backpressure on the broker
if (_breaker.IsOpen || ch.Reader.Count > 800)
    consumer.Pause(consumer.Assignment);       // Kafka: stop fetching
else
    consumer.Resume(consumer.Assignment);      // backlog stays in the log

Follow-ups

  • Where should the backlog live? — In the broker, which is durable, observable and designed for it — not in a service's heap, and not in a database table you now have to garbage-collect.
  • What is the RabbitMQ equivalent? — Prefetch (basic.qos) plus, at the broker, flow control and memory/disk alarms that block publishers when the node is under pressure. The broker blocking publishers is backpressure reaching the source.
  • When is dropping correct? — For replaceable data with a natural refresh: metrics, presence updates, live positions. Say "drop oldest" explicitly and expose the drop counter, or nobody will know it is happening.

Common wrong answer

"Scale the consumers automatically." Autoscaling is a response to sustained load, not a substitute for backpressure: it takes tens of seconds to minutes, it is bounded by partitions and by the downstream database, and without backpressure the system still falls over in the interval. Also, scaling consumers frequently just moves the overload onto the database.

seniorWhat is a retry storm, and how do retry budgets stop it?

Answer

A retry storm is amplification: when a dependency degrades, every caller retries, and if each layer of a five-service chain retries three times you can multiply the offered load by 3^5 = 243 at exactly the moment the system has least capacity. The dependency then cannot recover because the retry load alone exceeds its capacity — a metastable failure that persists after the original trigger is gone. Retry budgets fix it by capping retries as a fraction of successful traffic, typically 10-20%: a client tracks its success rate and simply refuses to retry when the budget is exhausted, so retries scale with health rather than with failure. Combine that with retrying at one layer only (usually the outermost that has enough context), never retrying a request whose deadline has expired, and marking retries with a header so the server can shed them first. The number worth quoting is that 10% is enough to absorb ordinary transient failure and small enough that a total outage cannot amplify load beyond 1.1x.

Example

Amplification, five hops, three attempts each:
  1 user request -> 3 -> 9 -> 27 -> 81 -> 243 backend calls

With a 10% retry budget at each hop:
  1 -> 1.1 -> 1.21 -> 1.33 -> 1.46 -> 1.61   (bounded, recoverable)

Rules that go with it:
  * retry at ONE layer (the one that owns the user deadline)
  * never retry after the deadline has passed
  * mark retries (x-retry: 1) so the server sheds them first under load

Follow-ups

  • What is metastable failure? — A state where the system stays down after the trigger is removed, because the recovery load (retries, cache misses, reconnections) exceeds capacity. Breaking it needs a deliberate reduction of load: shed traffic, drain queues, restart with a warm cache.
  • How do you recover from one? — Turn off retries, shed or throttle inbound traffic to a fraction, let the dependency drain, then ramp back up. Restarting everything simultaneously usually re-enters the same state.
  • Where do queues make this better or worse? — Better: the backlog is durable and the broker absorbs the burst. Worse: a queue full of work that will fail keeps the failing dependency saturated, so you need to pause consumption, not just retry more slowly.

Common wrong answer

"Retries with backoff prevent overload." Backoff spaces out one client's attempts; it does nothing about the number of clients. Ten thousand clients each politely backing off still deliver a synchronised wall of retries unless the total is budgeted.

seniorConsumer lag is growing steadily. Walk me through the diagnosis.

Answer

Lag growth means arrival rate exceeds processing rate, so the first split is: did production increase, or did processing slow? Look at produce rate versus consume rate over the same window; if production is flat, the consumer changed. Then check whether the lag is uniform across partitions or concentrated — concentrated means key skew or one stuck partition, uniform means a global slowdown. Then look at where the time goes inside the handler: a downstream latency increase is the most common cause by far, followed by a rebalance loop (check group generation id incrementing, and max.poll.interval.ms evictions), a poison message being retried in place, a GC or CPU throttling issue, and a deployment that changed batch size or added a synchronous call. The immediate mitigations differ per cause, and you should name them: scale consumers only if partitions allow and the downstream can take it; increase batch size or parallelism within a partition if the handler is IO-bound; pause and drain if the downstream is the problem; and if the backlog is unrecoverable, decide explicitly whether to skip forward and reconcile.

Example

Decision tree, in the order it resolves fastest:
  produce rate up?           -> capacity problem; scale or shed
  lag on ONE partition?      -> hot key, poison message, or a dead member
  generation id incrementing?-> rebalance loop: poll interval, session timeout,
                                or a consumer crashing on a specific record
  handler p99 up?            -> downstream latency; check DB/API, not Kafka
  CPU throttled / GC?        -> the consumer pod, not the pipeline
  none of the above          -> commit pattern: are you committing per record?

Metrics that must exist before the incident:
  records-lag-max per partition, poll interval, commit latency,
  handler duration histogram, rebalance count, downstream call p99

Follow-ups

  • Lag in records or in time? — Time. A million records of a cheap event is nothing; ten thousand of an expensive one is an hour. Publish lag as estimated seconds behind, and alert on that.
  • How do you catch up faster without changing code? — Raise max.poll.records and fetch sizes, increase in-handler parallelism per partition (with per-key ordering preserved), or temporarily scale up to the partition count. Beyond that you are limited by the downstream.
  • When is skipping the backlog the right call? — When the data is time-valuable and stale processing has no benefit (live pricing, presence) — and only with an explicit record of what was skipped and a reconciliation plan.

Common wrong answer

"Add more consumers." It is right roughly a third of the time and useless the rest — capped by partition count, unhelpful for a hot partition, and actively harmful when the bottleneck is a shared database that more consumers will simply contend on harder. Diagnose the rate split first.

seniorHow does a cascading failure start, and how do you stop one in progress?

Answer

It starts with a local saturation that becomes global through a shared resource: one dependency slows, callers hold threads or connections waiting, their pools exhaust, so they slow for all requests including those that never touch the slow dependency, their callers then time out and retry, and the amplification carries the failure upstream. Common accelerants are unbounded thread pools and connection pools, uniform timeouts, retries at every layer, a shared database, and cache stampedes on restart. Stopping one in progress means reducing load, not adding capacity: shed traffic at the edge (drop the lowest-value traffic first), disable retries, open breakers manually if needed, and only then restart components — restarting first usually deepens it because cold caches and reconnection storms add load. Prevention is architectural: bulkheads so each dependency has its own bounded pool, timeouts short enough that a stuck call frees the thread, load shedding with an explicit priority order, and testing the shed path so you know it works when you need it.

Example

The chain, with the fix at each link:
  slow dependency        -> short timeout, deadline propagation
  threads pile up        -> bulkhead: bounded pool per dependency
  pool exhausted         -> reject fast (queue limit), do not queue forever
  callers time out       -> retry budget, single retry layer
  retries amplify        -> shed marked retries first
  restart everything     -> staged restart, warm caches, ramp traffic

Follow-ups

  • Why is adding capacity often the wrong first move? — New instances start cold, open new connections, and miss caches, so they add load before they add capacity; in a metastable state that can extend the outage.
  • What is a good shed order? — Bulk and batch first, then retries, then anonymous traffic, then non-critical features, then authenticated read traffic; writes that allocate scarce resources are usually last. Decide it in advance and encode it as request priority.
  • How do you rehearse this? — Game days with real load shedding enabled in production at low intensity; a shed path that has never run will fail the first time you need it.

Common wrong answer

"Autoscale and the problem resolves itself." Autoscaling reacts on a timescale of minutes to a failure that propagates in seconds, is bounded by the shared resource that is actually saturated, and can accelerate the cascade by multiplying connections to an already-struggling database.

seniorDesign graceful degradation for a checkout flow that depends on five services.

Answer

Classify each dependency as critical or optional for the specific step, then design the optional ones to fail invisibly and the critical ones to fail honestly. In a checkout, payment authorisation and inventory allocation are critical: if they are unavailable, the correct behaviour is a clear error and a preserved cart, never a silent success. Recommendations, loyalty points, tax-estimate refinement, fraud scoring above a threshold and the confirmation email are optional at the moment of purchase: serve a cached or default value, or queue the work for later, and record that you degraded so the follow-up job can complete it. Implement it with per-dependency timeouts sized to the remaining budget, breakers with concrete fallbacks, and feature flags that let an operator turn off an optional dependency in seconds. Two things to say unprompted: degradation must be observable — a metric per fallback path, so "we are up" does not hide "we are up and half the features are off" — and the degraded path must be tested regularly, because the fallback nobody exercises is the one that throws a null reference during the incident.

Example

Payment authorisationcriticalFail the step, keep the cart, offer retry; never assume success
Inventory allocationcriticalFail closed — overselling is worse than a lost sale here
Fraud scoringdegradableBelow threshold: accept and review async; above: fail closed
Loyalty pointsoptionalQueue an event, apply later, tell the user "points pending"
RecommendationsoptionalServe cached or omit the widget entirely

Follow-ups

  • How do you decide fail-open versus fail-closed? — By the cost of the wrong answer. Fail-closed for anything that allocates a scarce resource or grants access; fail-open for enrichment and presentation.
  • What do you tell the user? — Something specific and true ("points will be applied within an hour"), never a generic error. Honest degradation preserves trust; silent degradation destroys it when discovered.
  • How do you keep the deferred work? — Emit an event for the degraded step and let a consumer complete it; that is the queue earning its keep, and it makes degradation recoverable rather than lossy.

Common wrong answer

"Everything in checkout is critical." It is the safe-sounding answer that guarantees the flow is only as available as the least reliable of five services — multiply five 99.9% dependencies and you have 99.5%, which is over three hours of downtime a month. The graded skill is separating the two classes.

seniorWhat is load shedding, and how does it differ from rate limiting?

Answer

Rate limiting is a policy applied per client to enforce fairness and quota — it is predictable, contractual, and active whether or not the system is under stress. Load shedding is a reaction to the server's own state: when the system is beyond its capacity, it rejects work quickly to protect the work it has already accepted. The distinction matters because they need different signals: a rate limiter counts requests per key, while a shedder watches a saturation signal — queue depth, latency against a target, or CPU — and drops the least valuable traffic first. Good shedding is fast (rejecting must be far cheaper than serving), prioritised (request classes decided in advance), and honest (429 or 503 with Retry-After, so clients back off rather than hammer). The technique worth naming at senior level is controlled-delay queue management: track how long requests wait, and if the minimum wait over a window exceeds a target, start dropping — it responds to genuine saturation rather than to bursts, and it is what CoDel-style admission control in modern proxies does.

Example

// admission control by queue latency, not by queue length
if (_queue.MinWaitOverWindow > TargetWait && request.Priority < Priority.User)
    return Results.StatusCode(503);         // shed cheap, shed early

// rate limiting is a different axis, always on
app.UseRateLimiter(o => o.AddTokenBucketLimiter("per-tenant", b => {
    b.TokenLimit = 200; b.TokensPerPeriod = 200;
    b.ReplenishmentPeriod = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(1); b.QueueLimit = 0;
}));

Follow-ups

  • Where do you shed? — As close to the edge as possible, because work already accepted has consumed resources. Shedding at the database is too late.
  • Is shedding compatible with queues? — Yes, and it is often the right move: refuse to enqueue low-value work when the backlog exceeds the time budget for that work, rather than accumulating a backlog you can never drain.
  • How do you avoid shedding the wrong traffic? — Classify at ingress and carry the class through: user-interactive, retry, batch, internal. Without a class, shedding is random and randomly damaging.

Common wrong answer

"We rate limit, so we are protected." Rate limits are per client; a thousand clients each inside their limit can still exceed your capacity, and a limit tuned for normal capacity is wrong the moment you lose a zone. You need both a per-client policy and a server-state reaction.

principalHow do you define an SLO for an asynchronous pipeline?

Answer

Availability is the wrong primary indicator for a pipeline, because it can be "up" while delivering nothing. The two indicators that matter are freshness (end-to-end latency from the source event to the visible effect, measured at a percentile — "p99 under 60 seconds") and completeness (the fraction of events that produced their effect within the window — "99.99% within 5 minutes"), plus correctness if the pipeline transforms data. Measure them from the data, not from the components: stamp the event with an occurredAt at the source and compute the delta where the effect lands, and reconcile counts from source to sink on a rolling window so completeness is observed rather than assumed. Then attach the consequences: an error budget that governs release velocity, an alert on burn rate rather than on instantaneous lag (so a two-minute spike does not page anyone but a slow leak does), and a documented degradation policy that says what you sacrifice — freshness for completeness, usually — when you are behind. The principal-level addition is that the SLO must be negotiated with the consumers of the pipeline and written where they can see it, because an internal pipeline's freshness is somebody else's user-facing latency.

Example

SLI  freshness   = p99( effectVisibleAt - event.occurredAt )
SLI  completeness= events with an effect within 5m / events produced
SLI  correctness = rows failing the invariant check / rows checked

SLO  freshness p99 <= 60s over 28 days
     completeness >= 99.99% over 28 days
     correctness   = 100% (any violation is an incident, not a budget spend)

Alert on multi-window burn rate (fast: 2% budget in 1h; slow: 10% in 6h),
never on raw lag - lag spikes during deploys and pages nobody usefully.

Follow-ups

  • How do you measure completeness without double-counting? — Reconcile by identifier on a window: source ids minus sink ids, evaluated with a lag allowance. Counting alone hides a compensating loss and duplicate.
  • What if consumers have different needs? — Publish per-consumer SLOs on the same stream, or split into a fast path and a complete path. One number rarely fits an analytics consumer and a user-facing one.
  • What is the trade you name unprompted? — Freshness versus completeness: to catch up you can skip and reconcile (fresh, incomplete) or process everything in order (complete, stale). Decide before the incident and write it in the runbook.

Common wrong answer

"Our SLO is 99.9% uptime of the consumers." Consumer uptime says nothing about whether data arrived. A pipeline can have perfect component uptime and a 45-minute freshness violation because the downstream slowed; the SLO must be measured on the data path, end to end.

06Apache Kafka in depth

Kafka is the broker interviewers assume you know, so this is the largest unit in the bank and the one where vagueness is punished hardest. Screener depth is the data model; mid depth is consumer groups, offsets and replication; senior depth is KRaft, compaction, rebalance protocols, tiered storage and the tuning knobs with their actual defaults. The 2026-specific material — share groups production-ready in 4.2, and the 4.3 operator features — is worth having ready, because naming a recent KIP is the cheapest way to show you follow the project rather than a 2019 tutorial.

screenerDescribe Kafka's data model and architecture.

Answer

A topic is a named stream split into partitions; each partition is an append-only, ordered log stored as segment files on one broker's disk and replicated to others. Every record in a partition has a monotonically increasing offset, and consumers track their position by committing an offset per partition per group, which is what makes replay a first-class operation. Brokers form a cluster; each partition has one leader that serves reads and writes and N-1 followers that replicate from it, with the set that is caught up called the in-sync replica set. Since Kafka 4.0 (2025-03-18) cluster metadata lives in KRaft — an internal Raft quorum of controller nodes — and ZooKeeper is gone entirely. The single sentence that captures the design is that Kafka is a distributed commit log with dumb brokers and smart consumers: the broker does not track per-message delivery state, the consumer owns its cursor, and that is what buys the throughput and the replay.

Example

topic "orders", partitions 3, replication.factor 3

  broker-1        broker-2        broker-3
  p0 LEADER       p0 follower     p0 follower
  p1 follower     p1 LEADER       p1 follower
  p2 follower     p2 follower     p2 LEADER

partition p0 on disk:
  00000000000000000000.log   .index   .timeindex   (segment files)
  00000000000004096000.log   ...      active segment appended to
consumer group "billing" commits offsets to __consumer_offsets

Follow-ups

  • Where are offsets stored? — In the internal compacted topic __consumer_offsets, keyed by (group, topic, partition), since 0.9; before that they were in ZooKeeper, which you will still see referenced in old documentation.
  • Why is Kafka fast? — Sequential IO on append-only segments, page-cache reads with zero-copy transfer to the socket, batching at the producer, and no per-message broker bookkeeping. Not because it is in memory — it writes everything to disk.
  • What is the retention model? — Time or size based (retention.ms default 7 days, retention.bytes unlimited), applied per partition per segment, or cleanup.policy=compact to keep the latest value per key instead.

Common wrong answer

"Kafka is an in-memory message queue." It is a disk-based log that relies on the OS page cache; and it is not a queue in the classic sense, because consuming does not remove anything. Both halves of the sentence signal a candidate who has only used a client library.

midExplain ISR, the high watermark and leader epochs.

Answer

The in-sync replica set is the set of replicas that have fetched up to the leader's log end offset within replica.lag.time.max.ms (default 30 seconds); a slow or dead follower drops out of the ISR and rejoins when it catches up. The high watermark is the highest offset replicated to all members of the ISR, and consumers can only read up to it — that is what guarantees a consumer never sees a record that could be lost if the leader fails. Leader epochs are a monotonically increasing number assigned each time leadership changes; followers use the leader-epoch sequence to truncate their logs correctly after a failover instead of relying on the high watermark alone, which fixed a genuine data-loss and divergence class of bugs in older versions. Together these three give the durability story: with acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2, an acknowledged write exists on at least two replicas, and only a replica that had it can become leader unless you have explicitly enabled unclean election.

Example

leader   LEO=105  (log end offset)
follower A LEO=105  in ISR
follower B LEO=101  in ISR (within replica.lag.time.max.ms)
follower C LEO=40   OUT of ISR

high watermark = min(LEO over ISR) = 101
consumers can read up to offset 100; 101-104 are not yet "committed"

min.insync.replicas=2 and only the leader in ISR
  -> producer with acks=all gets NotEnoughReplicasException. By design:
     Kafka refuses the write rather than accepting an unreplicated one.

Follow-ups

  • What is the point of refusing writes when the ISR shrinks? — It converts silent future data loss into a visible, immediate error. Teams who "fix" it by setting min.insync.replicas=1 have chosen availability and should say so out loud.
  • What are eligible leader replicas? — A newer mechanism (KIP-966, phased in through the 4.x line) that tracks which replicas are eligible to become leader even after falling out of the ISR, improving availability without the data loss of unclean election.
  • Can a consumer read unreplicated data? — Not normally: the high watermark stops it. With read_committed the limit is stricter still — the last stable offset, which also excludes records in open transactions.

Common wrong answer

"ISR means the replicas are identical to the leader." They are within a lag window, not identical — a replica can be several thousand records behind and still be in the ISR. That distinction is exactly why the high watermark exists.

midWhat triggers a consumer-group rebalance, and what happens during one?

Answer

A rebalance is triggered when group membership or the subscription changes: a consumer joins, a consumer leaves cleanly, a consumer is evicted for missing session.timeout.ms heartbeats or exceeding max.poll.interval.ms between polls, the topic's partition count changes, or a subscribed pattern starts matching a new topic. Under the classic eager protocol every consumer revokes all its partitions and stops processing until the new assignment is computed — a stop-the-world pause proportional to the group size, which is why a rolling deploy of twenty consumers used to cause twenty full rebalances. Cooperative sticky assignment (default since 3.0 for the classic protocol) revokes only the partitions that actually move, so most consumers keep working. The new consumer group protocol (KIP-848), GA in Kafka 4.0, moves assignment computation to the broker-side group coordinator and makes rebalances fully incremental, removing the group leader role from clients; Kafka 4.3 (KIP-1274) began logging deprecation warnings for the classic protocol, so migration is now the expected direction.

Example

# classic protocol, tuned to survive deploys
group.instance.id=billing-3           # static membership (KIP-345):
                                      # a restart within session.timeout.ms
                                      # does NOT trigger a rebalance
session.timeout.ms=45000              # default since 3.0
heartbeat.interval.ms=3000
max.poll.interval.ms=300000           # 5 min; the usual eviction cause
partition.assignment.strategy=org.apache.kafka.clients.consumer.CooperativeStickyAssignor

# new protocol (KIP-848, GA in 4.0) - broker computes the assignment
group.protocol=consumer

Follow-ups

  • What is the classic symptom of a rebalance loop? — Generation id incrementing continuously with near-zero throughput, usually because a handler occasionally exceeds max.poll.interval.ms. The fix is smaller max.poll.records or moving slow work off the poll thread, not a longer interval.
  • Why does static membership help? — It gives a consumer a stable identity, so a rolling restart or a pod reschedule inside the session timeout keeps the same assignment and skips the rebalance entirely.
  • Does the new protocol remove all pauses? — It removes the stop-the-world join barrier and the client-side leader; partitions that genuinely move still have to be revoked, so the pause is proportional to the change, not to the group.

Common wrong answer

"Increase session.timeout.ms to stop rebalances." It delays the detection of genuinely dead consumers, so a crashed pod's partitions stay unprocessed for longer, and it does nothing about the max.poll.interval.ms eviction that usually causes the loop. Fix the poll loop instead.

midHow should you commit offsets?

Answer

The rule is: commit after the work is durable, never before, and commit in batches rather than per record. With enable.auto.commit=true (the default) the client commits the offsets of the last poll every auto.commit.interval.ms (default 5 seconds) on the next poll call, which means a crash can lose up to five seconds of processed-but-uncommitted work (redelivery, fine) or — worse — commit records that were polled but not yet processed, which is silent loss. So for anything that matters, turn auto-commit off and call commitSync or commitAsync after the batch is handled, committing the offset of the last processed record plus one. The pattern that gets full marks is committing asynchronously during steady state for throughput and synchronously in the rebalance listener and on shutdown for correctness, plus storing the offset alongside the effect when the sink is transactional — an inbox row or a Kafka transaction — so that the commit and the work cannot disagree.

Example

consumer.subscribe(List.of("orders"), new ConsumerRebalanceListener() {
    public void onPartitionsRevoked(Collection<TopicPartition> p) {
        consumer.commitSync(currentOffsets);      // do not lose work on revoke
    }
    public void onPartitionsAssigned(Collection<TopicPartition> p) { }
});

while (running) {
    var records = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(500));
    for (var r : records) { handle(r); track(r); }   // handler is idempotent
    consumer.commitAsync();                          // steady state
}
consumer.commitSync();                               // shutdown

Follow-ups

  • Why not commit after every record? — Each commit is a round trip to the group coordinator; per-record commits typically cut throughput by an order of magnitude and buy you almost nothing, since your handler must be idempotent anyway.
  • What does committing offset N mean? — "Resume from N", so you commit lastProcessed + 1. Committing the processed offset itself replays one record every restart, which is a classic off-by-one in home-grown loops.
  • Where do you store offsets when the sink is a database? — In the database, in the same transaction as the effect, and seek to them on assignment. That makes the pair atomic without Kafka transactions.

Common wrong answer

"Auto-commit is fine because it commits only what was processed." It commits what was polled, at a timer, on the next poll. If your handler is asynchronous or you hand records to a thread pool, auto-commit will mark unprocessed records as done and you will lose data with no error.

seniorWhat is KRaft, why did it replace ZooKeeper, and what changed operationally?

Answer

KRaft is Kafka's own Raft implementation storing cluster metadata — topics, partitions, ISR, configs, ACLs — in an internal metadata log replicated across a quorum of controller nodes, with brokers consuming that log as event-sourced state instead of reading ZooKeeper. It replaced ZooKeeper because the old design had a scaling wall and a slow failure path: metadata was fetched into the controller on failover, so a controller change on a large cluster took minutes, and the practical partition ceiling was in the low hundreds of thousands. KRaft makes controller failover near-instant (the new leader already has the log), pushes the ceiling to millions of partitions, and removes an entire second distributed system from your operational surface. It became the default in 3.3, production-ready for all features by 3.5-3.6, and ZooKeeper support was removed entirely in Kafka 4.0 (2025-03-18) — so in 2026 the migration question is historical for new clusters and urgent for anyone still on 3.x. Operationally you now size and monitor a controller quorum (3 or 5 nodes, dedicated in production), watch metadata log lag, and use kafka-metadata-quorum rather than a ZooKeeper CLI.

Example

# dedicated controller (production shape)
process.roles=controller
node.id=1
controller.quorum.voters=1@c1:9093,2@c2:9093,3@c3:9093
metadata.log.dir=/var/lib/kafka/metadata

# broker
process.roles=broker
controller.quorum.voters=1@c1:9093,2@c2:9093,3@c3:9093

# combined roles are for dev only: a controller that is also under
# broker load will lose elections when the broker GCs.

Follow-ups

  • What was the 3.x-to-4.x migration path? — Upgrade to 3.9 (the last release with ZooKeeper), run the dual-write ZooKeeper-to-KRaft migration to move metadata, finalise, then upgrade to 4.x. Skipping straight to 4.0 from a ZooKeeper cluster is not possible.
  • How many controllers? — Three for most clusters, five when you want to survive two simultaneous losses; more is slower, because every metadata write needs a majority.
  • What breaks if the controller quorum is down? — Metadata changes stop — no topic creation, no leader election, no ISR updates — but existing leaders keep serving produce and consume traffic. That degradation shape is worth knowing.

Common wrong answer

"KRaft is optional; we still run ZooKeeper." Since 4.0 there is no ZooKeeper mode to run. A candidate saying this in 2026 has not touched a cluster in two years, and the follow-up ("what version are you on?") is where it becomes visible.

seniorExplain log compaction. When do you use it and what are its traps?

Answer

With cleanup.policy=compact, Kafka retains at least the most recent value for every key rather than deleting by age, so the topic converges to a snapshot of current state while still being a log you can replay. A background cleaner rewrites segments, keeping the latest record per key and dropping older ones; a record with a null value is a tombstone, which deletes the key and is itself retained for delete.retention.ms (default 24 hours) so consumers have a chance to see the deletion. Use it for changelog and state topics: Kafka Streams state-store changelogs, __consumer_offsets, entity snapshots, configuration and reference data that a new consumer must bootstrap from. The traps interviewers probe are all about timing: compaction never touches the active segment, so recent duplicates are always visible; a consumer offline longer than delete.retention.ms can miss a tombstone entirely and keep a deleted key forever; and compaction is not a GDPR erasure mechanism on its own, because the old records survive until the cleaner runs and any downstream copy is unaffected.

Example

before compaction (offsets):
  10 k1=A   11 k2=B   12 k1=C   13 k3=D   14 k2=null   15 k1=E
after compaction:
  13 k3=D   14 k2=null(tombstone, kept 24h)   15 k1=E

settings that matter:
  cleanup.policy=compact              (or compact,delete for both)
  min.cleanable.dirty.ratio=0.5       lower = compact sooner, more IO
  delete.retention.ms=86400000        tombstone visibility window
  min.compaction.lag.ms / max.compaction.lag.ms   bound how late it runs

Follow-ups

  • Can a compacted topic guarantee one record per key? — No. It guarantees at least the latest; duplicates in the active segment and in not-yet-cleaned segments are normal, so consumers must be last-writer-wins.
  • What is compact,delete for? — Keeping the latest value per key and expiring the whole topic after a retention period — useful for state that is only relevant for a bounded time, such as per-session state.
  • What is the operational failure? — The cleaner falling behind or dying on a corrupt record: the topic grows, and rebuilding a Streams state store from a bloated changelog takes hours. Alert on cleaner lag and on uncleanable partitions.

Common wrong answer

"Compaction gives you a key-value store." It gives you a log that converges toward one; there is no random access by key, no read-your-writes, and no guarantee about when a duplicate disappears. If you want lookups, materialise it into a state store or a database.

seniorCompare the classic and new consumer group protocols, and explain static membership.

Answer

The classic protocol elects one consumer as group leader, which computes the assignment client-side and distributes it through the coordinator; every member must rejoin at a synchronisation barrier, so the whole group pauses. Eager assignment revokes everything on every rebalance; cooperative sticky (default since 3.0) revokes only what moves, over two rounds. The new protocol, KIP-848, moves the assignment to the broker's group coordinator and makes reconciliation incremental and asynchronous, so there is no global barrier and no client-side leader — it went GA in Kafka 4.0, and 4.3's KIP-1274 started deprecating the classic protocol with warnings, while KIP-1251 (also 4.3) added assignment epochs so members are fenced less often for no reason. Static membership (KIP-345) is orthogonal and still valuable: giving each consumer a stable group.instance.id means a restart within the session timeout retains the same partitions and triggers no rebalance at all, which is what makes rolling deploys cheap. Practical guidance in 2026: use the new protocol on 4.x, keep static membership for stateful consumers, and stop tuning session timeouts as a rebalance workaround.

  • Is the migration to the new protocol online? — Yes: it was designed for a mixed group during upgrade, so members can switch over a rolling deploy rather than a big-bang cutover.
  • What is the cost of static membership? — A crashed instance's partitions stay unassigned until the session timeout expires, so you trade faster deploys for slower failure detection. Size the timeout for that trade deliberately.
  • When does stickiness matter most? — For consumers with local state (Streams state stores, warm caches): a moved partition means rebuilding state, which can take minutes, so an assignment that stays put is worth far more than one that is perfectly balanced.

Common wrong answer

"Rebalancing is just Kafka rebalancing load; it is not a problem." For stateless consumers it is nearly free; for stateful ones a rebalance means restoring a changelog, and a rebalance loop during an incident is the difference between a blip and an hour of lag. Say what your consumers hold before you say rebalancing is cheap.

seniorWhat does tiered storage change about how you design a Kafka cluster?

Answer

Tiered storage (KIP-405, generally available since 3.9) moves closed log segments from broker disks to object storage, keeping only recent data local while the topic's logical retention can extend to months or years. It decouples storage from compute, which changes three design decisions: brokers can be sized for throughput rather than for retention, so you stop adding brokers just to hold data; long retention becomes economically ordinary, which makes replay and late-joining consumers a design option rather than a luxury; and rebalancing or replacing a broker gets dramatically faster, because there is much less local data to re-replicate — historically the slowest operation in Kafka. Kafka 4.3 (KIP-1023) went further by letting a follower bootstrap from the last tiered offset via follower.fetch.last.tiered.offset.enable, so a new replica does not have to stream the whole history from the leader. The trade-offs to name: reads of tiered data are slower and hit object-storage costs and rate limits, so a consumer resetting to the earliest offset on a big topic is now a spend event as well as a latency event.

Example

# topic level
remote.storage.enable=true
local.retention.ms=86400000        # 1 day hot on broker disks
retention.ms=15552000000           # 180 days total, rest in object storage

# what it buys
#   broker disk sized for 1 day, not 180
#   replacing a broker re-replicates 1 day of data
#   4.3: follower.fetch.last.tiered.offset.enable=true
#        -> new replicas bootstrap from the tier, not from the leader

Follow-ups

  • What is the latency of reading tiered data? — Object-store latency plus fetch overhead: tens to hundreds of milliseconds per segment rather than page-cache microseconds. Fine for backfill, wrong for a real-time consumer that has fallen behind into the tier.
  • Does it work with compacted topics? — Support has been narrower than for delete-policy topics; check the version you are on before designing around it, and treat compacted changelogs as local by default.
  • How does it change the "how long do we keep data" conversation? — It turns a capacity argument into a cost-and-compliance argument, which is a better conversation to be having.

Common wrong answer

"Tiered storage makes Kafka a data lake." It makes retention cheap; it does not add query, indexing or schema-on-read. Reading a year of a topic is still a sequential scan from offset zero, and if that is your access pattern you want a table format, not a log.

seniorWhat problem do share groups solve, and what do you give up by using them?

Answer

Share groups (KIP-932) give Kafka genuine queue semantics: consumers in a share group cooperate on the same partitions with per-record acknowledgement and delivery counting, so the number of consumers is no longer capped by the partition count and a single poison record no longer blocks a partition. They went early access in 4.0, preview in 4.1, and production-ready in 4.2 (2026-02-17) with the RENEW acknowledgement type, adaptive batching and lag metrics (KIP-1222/1224/1226); 4.3 added the broker and group configuration surface to tune them (KIP-1240). They solve the classic "we need a work queue but our platform is Kafka" problem without bolting on a second broker. What you give up is ordering — records are handed to whichever consumer is free, so per-key order is gone — plus the simple offset model, since progress is tracked per record rather than as a single cursor, which changes how you reason about lag and replay. Use them for task distribution with variable processing time; keep classic consumer groups where ordering or state locality matters.

Example

// share consumer: per-record acknowledgement, no partition ownership
var consumer = new KafkaShareConsumer<String, String>(props);  // group.id = share group
consumer.subscribe(List.of("tasks"));
var records = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(500));
for (var r : records) {
    try { handle(r); consumer.acknowledge(r, AcknowledgeType.ACCEPT); }
    catch (TransientException e) { consumer.acknowledge(r, AcknowledgeType.RELEASE); }
    catch (Exception e)          { consumer.acknowledge(r, AcknowledgeType.REJECT); }
}
consumer.commitSync();
// 12 partitions, 60 consumers: all 60 work. Ordering: none.

Follow-ups

  • When would you still choose RabbitMQ or SQS? — When you want priorities, per-message TTL, delayed delivery or a mature DLQ story out of the box, or when you have no Kafka cluster to begin with. Share groups close the gap; they do not erase it.
  • How does delivery counting work? — Each record carries a delivery count per share group; exceeding the configured limit moves it out of the way (archived/rejected) instead of looping forever, which is the queue behaviour Kafka previously lacked.
  • Do share groups replace consumer groups? — No. They are a second, parallel model on the same topic; a topic can have classic groups and share groups simultaneously, which makes incremental adoption easy.

Common wrong answer

"Kafka cannot do queues; you need RabbitMQ for that." True until 4.0, arguable in 4.1, and wrong from 4.2 (February 2026). Saying it flatly is the clearest signal that your Kafka knowledge predates the 4.x line.

seniorTune a producer for throughput. Which knobs, and what do they cost?

Answer

Throughput on the producer side is mostly about batching, and the two knobs are batch.size (bytes per partition batch, default 16 KB) and linger.ms (how long to wait for a batch to fill, default 0). Raising linger to 5-50ms and batch size to 64-256 KB typically multiplies throughput several times because it amortises request overhead and makes compression far more effective — the cost is exactly that added latency, paid on every message. Compression is the second lever: compression.type=zstd is the current default recommendation, giving strong ratios on JSON at good CPU cost, with lz4 preferred when CPU is scarce and gzip essentially legacy. Then size buffer.memory (default 32 MB) so bursts do not block the send path, keep idempotence on (default since 3.0) with max.in.flight.requests.per.connection at 5, and use acks=all because with batching the durability cost is small. The honest framing for an interview is that these are a latency-for-throughput trade with one exception: compression usually improves both, because network and disk are the constraint more often than CPU.

Example

# high-throughput pipeline (batch/ETL feed)
linger.ms=25
batch.size=262144
compression.type=zstd
buffer.memory=134217728
acks=all
enable.idempotence=true

# low-latency path (user-facing event)
linger.ms=0
batch.size=16384
compression.type=lz4
# expect ~5-10x fewer messages/sec per producer than the batched config

Follow-ups

  • What if messages are spread thinly over many partitions? — Batching suffers, because batches are per partition. Sticky partitioning (2.4+) helps for null-keyed records by filling one partition's batch before moving on; for keyed records, fewer partitions or a longer linger is the lever.
  • Where does the consumer side matter? — fetch.min.bytes and fetch.max.wait.ms are the mirror image: raising them batches fetches and reduces broker load, at the cost of latency.
  • How do you know you are producer-bound at all? — Compare producer record-send-rate and request-latency-avg against broker request queue time. Most "Kafka is slow" tickets are a slow consumer or an undersized downstream, not the producer.

Common wrong answer

"Set acks=1 for throughput." The gain is small once batching is in place, and the cost is losing acknowledged data on a leader failure. Interviewers treat this as a durability judgment question disguised as a tuning question.

seniorHow do you upgrade a Kafka cluster, or migrate to a new one, without downtime?

Answer

For an in-place upgrade, the sequence is: read the release notes for removals (4.0 removed ZooKeeper and the old Scala clients; 4.2 and 4.3 carry deprecations aimed at 5.0), upgrade clients before brokers where the compatibility matrix allows, then roll brokers one at a time waiting for all partitions to be fully in-sync between each — the under-replicated partition count returning to zero is the gate, not a timer. In KRaft you upgrade controllers and brokers separately and then bump the metadata version with kafka-features once you are confident, because that step is not reversible. For a cluster migration, run MirrorMaker 2 to replicate topics, consumer group offsets and ACLs into the new cluster, let it reach steady state, then cut over consumers first (they can lag behind safely), verify, then producers, keeping the mirror running in reverse or in place until you are sure. The things to say unprompted: offset translation across clusters is approximate, so consumers must be idempotent through the cutover; and you rehearse the rollback path before you start, because "we cannot go back" is the sentence that turns a migration into an incident.

Example

rolling broker upgrade gate (per broker, not per batch):
  1. graceful shutdown -> controlled leader migration
  2. upgrade binaries, start
  3. WAIT: UnderReplicatedPartitions == 0 AND OfflinePartitions == 0
  4. next broker

migration with MirrorMaker 2:
  topics + offsets + ACLs replicated; monitor replication lag
  cut over consumers  -> verify lag and correctness on the new cluster
  cut over producers  -> verify produce rate; keep the mirror briefly
  rollback = point producers back; consumers still have their offsets

Follow-ups

  • Why cut over consumers before producers? — Consumers can tolerate lag and can be moved back cheaply; producers moving first would strand data on the new cluster with nobody reading it.
  • What is irreversible? — Bumping the metadata or inter-broker protocol version. Do it after a soak, not as part of the roll.
  • How do you verify correctness during the cutover? — Compare end-to-end counts and a sampled checksum of records per partition between clusters, and keep an eye on the consumer group's committed offsets on both sides.

Common wrong answer

"Restart all brokers with the new version." Restarting more than one at a time with replication factor 3 can take partitions offline, and skipping the in-sync gate between brokers is how a routine upgrade produces under-replicated partitions and, if unclean election is enabled, data loss.

principalWhen would you not choose Kafka in 2026?

Answer

When the operational cost exceeds the value of the log. Concretely: a team of five with no platform engineering support should take a managed queue (SQS, Azure Service Bus, Google Pub/Sub) over any self-managed Kafka, because the failure modes — rebalance storms, disk pressure, controller quorum, client version drift — need someone whose job that is. When the workload is task distribution with per-message priority, delay and TTL, RabbitMQ remains a better fit despite share groups. When the volume is genuinely small — a few hundred messages a second and no replay requirement — the log's advantages never materialise and you carry the cost anyway. When you need request/reply with low latency, use RPC. When the requirement is fan-out to mobile or browser clients, a broker for services is the wrong layer. And when the organisation is already standardised on a cloud-native stack, the integration and IAM story of the native service often beats a technically superior Kafka. The principal-level framing is that Kafka's real value is the durable, replayable log as an organisational asset — if you are not going to use replay, retention and multiple independent consumers, you are paying for a distributed log and using it as a queue.

  • How do you decide as a lead? — Write down the three things you need (replay? ordering? per-message retry?) and the team's operational capacity, then pick. The technology comparison without the capacity line is how teams end up with a cluster nobody can upgrade.

07RabbitMQ, AMQP and classic brokers

RabbitMQ comes up in almost every enterprise loop, and it is the unit where stale knowledge shows fastest: the 4.x line removed classic mirrored queues, made quorum queues the durable default, and in 4.3 (2026-04-23) removed Mnesia in favour of Khepri along with the old partition-handling strategies. Screener depth is the exchange model; mid depth is queue types, confirms and acks; senior depth is partition behaviour, prefetch and the migration story. If your experience is from 3.8, say so and then say what changed — that is a better answer than pretending.

screenerExplain exchanges, bindings and routing keys.

Answer

Publishers never publish to a queue; they publish to an exchange with a routing key, and the exchange uses its bindings to decide which queues get a copy. A direct exchange matches the routing key exactly, a topic exchange matches patterns with * (one word) and # (zero or more words), a fanout exchange ignores the key and copies to every bound queue, and a headers exchange matches on header values instead of the key. The indirection is the point: publishers are decoupled from consumers, so a new subscriber is a new queue with a new binding and requires no publisher change. Two operational facts round out the answer — a message routed to no queue is discarded silently unless the publisher sets the mandatory flag or the exchange has an alternate exchange, and queues, not exchanges, are where messages actually live and where durability is configured.

Example

exchange "events" (topic)
  binding  order.*.eu     -> queue eu-orders
  binding  order.created.# -> queue order-created-all
  binding  #              -> queue audit-everything

publish routing key "order.created.eu"
  -> eu-orders, order-created-all, audit-everything   (three copies)

publish routing key "invoice.paid.us"
  -> audit-everything only
  (if nothing matched and mandatory=false: silently dropped)

Follow-ups

  • What is the default exchange? — The nameless direct exchange to which every queue is automatically bound by its own name; publishing with routing key "my-queue" goes straight there. Convenient for demos, and the reason people think they publish to queues.
  • Alternate exchange versus mandatory? — Mandatory returns the unroutable message to the publisher; an alternate exchange routes it to a catch-all queue. The second is generally better because it survives a publisher that ignores returns.
  • Where is durability set? — On the queue (durable), on the message (persistent delivery mode) and, for real safety, in the queue type: a quorum queue is durable and replicated by definition.

Common wrong answer

"A topic exchange is like a Kafka topic." They are unrelated: a RabbitMQ topic exchange is a pattern-matching router, while a Kafka topic is a partitioned log. The routing happens at publish time in RabbitMQ and at subscribe time in Kafka, which is why RabbitMQ can do content-based routing and Kafka cannot without a stream processor.

midQuorum queues, classic queues, or streams — how do you choose in 2026?

Answer

Quorum queues are the default for anything that matters: they are replicated with Raft, so a message acknowledged by a majority survives node loss, and they have a well-defined behaviour under partition. Classic queues remain for transient, non-replicated work — short-lived RPC reply queues, per-connection ephemeral queues — and classic mirrored queues were removed in RabbitMQ 4.0 (September 2024), which is the fact that dates a candidate's knowledge. Streams (since 3.9) are an append-only, replayable log with an offset-based consumer, so they cover the Kafka-shaped use cases inside RabbitMQ: multiple independent readers, replay, and very high throughput fan-out. Choose by the semantics you need: per-message acknowledgement, retry and priority means a quorum queue; replay and many independent consumers means a stream; anything transient and cheap means a classic queue. RabbitMQ 4.3 also removed most of the historical reasons to avoid quorum queues — it added 32 strict priority levels, delayed retries, and halved per-message memory overhead for messages up to 32 KiB.

Follow-ups

  • What is the cost of a quorum queue? — Raft replication means more disk IO and memory per message than a classic queue, and every queue is a Raft cluster, so tens of thousands of quorum queues on a small cluster is a real load. Consolidate queues rather than creating one per entity.
  • Do quorum queues support everything classic queues did? — Nearly, and 4.3 closed most of the remaining gaps (strict priorities, delayed retries, unlimited returns with acquired-count separated from delivery-count). Historic gaps such as per-message TTL semantics differ in detail — check before assuming.
  • How do quorum queues handle poison messages? — Natively, with x-delivery-limit: after N redeliveries the message is dead-lettered instead of looping, which classic queues never did by themselves.

Common wrong answer

"Use mirrored queues for high availability." They were deprecated in 3.9 and removed in 4.0. Recommending them in 2026 tells the interviewer your RabbitMQ experience stopped several years ago; the current answer is quorum queues, and streams where you need replay.

midPublisher confirms and consumer acknowledgements: what exactly do they guarantee?

Answer

Publisher confirms are an asynchronous acknowledgement from the broker that it has taken responsibility for a message — for a quorum queue that means a Raft majority has it, for a durable classic queue it means it has been persisted (or at least handed to the OS), and for a mandatory message that matched no queue you get a basic.return instead. Without confirms enabled, a publish is fire-and-forget over TCP: a broker restart loses in-flight messages and your application never knows. Consumer acknowledgements are the other half: with autoAck=false the broker keeps the message as unacknowledged until you ack, and if the channel or connection drops before the ack the message is requeued and redelivered — which is what makes RabbitMQ at-least-once. The two settings that must go together for durability are a quorum (or durable) queue plus persistent delivery mode plus confirms on the publisher and manual acks on the consumer; missing any one of them creates a loss window, and interviewers ask which one you would notice first.

Example

// publisher: confirms, awaited (RabbitMQ .NET client v7 is async-first)
var props = new BasicProperties { Persistent = true, MessageId = id };
await channel.BasicPublishAsync(exchange: "events", routingKey: "order.created",
    mandatory: true, basicProperties: props, body: body);   // awaits the confirm

// consumer: manual ack after the work is durable
var consumer = new AsyncEventingBasicConsumer(channel);
consumer.ReceivedAsync += async (_, ea) => {
    await HandleAsync(ea);                                   // idempotent
    await channel.BasicAckAsync(ea.DeliveryTag, multiple: false);
};
await channel.BasicConsumeAsync("orders", autoAck: false, consumer);

Follow-ups

  • What does basicNack with requeue do? — Returns the message for redelivery, which without a delivery limit becomes an infinite hot loop. On quorum queues set x-delivery-limit; on classic queues implement the counter yourself.
  • Transactions or confirms? — Confirms. AMQP transactions (tx.select) are synchronous and roughly two orders of magnitude slower; they exist and nobody should use them for throughput.
  • What happens to unacked messages when a consumer dies? — They are requeued once the broker notices the connection is gone, which is why the consumer timeout matters: RabbitMQ 4.3 added graceful consumer timeout handling with AMQP 1.0 link recovery instead of simply closing the channel.

Common wrong answer

"We set the queue to durable, so messages survive a restart." Durable is only one of three flags: a durable queue holding non-persistent messages loses them on restart, and without publisher confirms the publisher cannot know whether the broker ever got them. Name all three.

midHow do you schedule a message for later delivery in RabbitMQ?

Answer

Historically there were two approaches: the dead-letter TTL trick — publish to a queue with a per-queue message TTL and no consumer, so expiry dead-letters the message into the real queue — or the community rabbitmq-delayed-message-exchange plugin, which held messages in the exchange until their delay elapsed. Both have known problems: the TTL trick only works reliably with a per-queue TTL (per-message TTL expires in publish order, so one long-delay message blocks shorter ones behind it), and the plugin keeps schedule state outside the queue, which historically did not replicate well. As of RabbitMQ 4.3 quorum queues support delayed retries natively: a message can be delayed and redelivered internally without being rewritten, configured per queue or per message, and the old delayed-message-exchange plugin is deprecated in its favour. So the 2026 answer is: native delayed retries on quorum queues for retry backoff, a dedicated scheduler (a database table plus a timer, or a workflow engine) for business-level scheduling that must survive weeks and be cancellable.

Example

Legacy TTL trick (still everywhere in existing codebases):
  queue retry.30s  { x-message-ttl: 30000,
                     x-dead-letter-exchange: main,
                     x-dead-letter-routing-key: work }
  publish to retry.30s -> 30s later it appears on the main queue
  Trap: per-MESSAGE TTL expires in order, so a 10-minute message at the
        head blocks a 10-second message behind it.

Modern (4.3): quorum queue delayed retries - no side queues, no plugin.

Follow-ups

  • Why not just sleep in the consumer? — It holds the delivery and the prefetch slot, risks the consumer timeout, and blocks the queue's throughput. It only makes sense for delays under a second.
  • What about business scheduling — "remind me in 30 days"? — Not a broker's job. Use a durable store with a due timestamp and a scheduler that publishes when due; brokers are bad databases and 30-day-old messages defeat every monitoring signal you have.
  • Kafka equivalent? — Tiered retry topics with a consumer that pauses until the record's timestamp plus delay, or an external scheduler. There is no native per-message delay.

Common wrong answer

"Set a per-message TTL for the delay." It is the most-copied Stack Overflow answer in RabbitMQ, and it is a head-of-line bug: messages expire in queue order, so a single long delay stalls every shorter one behind it. Use per-queue TTL tiers, or 4.3's native delayed retries.

seniorWhat happens to a RabbitMQ cluster during a network partition?

Answer

It depends on the queue type and, since 4.3, much less on configuration. A quorum queue is a Raft group: the majority side elects a leader and keeps serving, and the minority side refuses writes — CP behaviour, no divergence, and the minority's clients see errors until the partition heals. Classic queues are not replicated at all now that mirroring is gone, so a queue is simply unavailable if its node is on the wrong side. The historical complexity was Mnesia's partition-handling strategies — pause_minority, autoheal and pause_if_all_down — which decided how the metadata store recovered and which could, with autoheal, discard one side's state. RabbitMQ 4.3 removed all of them along with Mnesia, because Khepri is Raft-based and therefore has one correct behaviour: the minority stops accepting metadata changes. Operationally this is a simplification worth stating in an interview — cluster sizing is now genuinely about majorities, so odd node counts (3 or 5) and awareness that a 2-node cluster has no useful majority are the practical consequences.

Example

3-node cluster, partition 2 | 1

Khepri metadata (4.3+): majority side accepts changes; minority read-only.
Quorum queue with leader on the majority side: keeps serving.
Quorum queue with leader on the minority side: re-elected on the majority.
Classic queue on the minority node: unavailable, no replica exists.

Removed in 4.3 and no longer configurable:
  cluster_partition_handling = pause_minority | autoheal | pause_if_all_down

Follow-ups

  • Why is a two-node cluster a bad idea? — There is no majority: losing either node leaves one that cannot form a quorum, so you get the cost of clustering with none of the availability. Three is the minimum useful size.
  • Can you stretch a cluster across regions? — Don't. Raft across high-latency links makes every write pay the round trip and partitions become routine. Use federation or the shovel plugin for cross-region movement instead.
  • How do clients behave during an election? — Publishes to the affected queue fail or block briefly; clients must handle connection recovery and retry with idempotency, which is why the client library's automatic recovery settings matter.

Common wrong answer

"Set autoheal so the cluster recovers automatically." Besides being removed in 4.3, autoheal recovered by picking a winning partition and discarding the other side's state — automatic recovery that could silently lose messages. Any answer that treats partition handling as a convenience setting misses that it is a data-loss decision.

seniorExplain prefetch, unacknowledged messages and consumer timeouts, and how they interact.

Answer

Prefetch (basic.qos) is the maximum number of unacknowledged messages the broker will push to a consumer; it is the only backpressure mechanism in the AMQP 0.9.1 push model. Too low and you are latency-bound, spending a round trip per message; too high and one consumer buffers a large share of the queue, so work distribution becomes unfair and a consumer crash requeues a large batch at once. Unacknowledged messages are held in broker memory for the duration, so a high prefetch multiplied by many consumers is a memory-planning decision, not just a throughput one. The consumer timeout (consumer_timeout, 30 minutes by default) closes the channel of a consumer that has held a delivery without acking for too long — it exists to catch stuck consumers, and it catches legitimate long-running handlers too, which is the incident people actually hit. RabbitMQ 4.3 made this less brutal by adding graceful consumer timeout handling with protocol-specific recovery (AMQP 1.0 link recovery, selective consumer cancellation on 0.9.1) instead of just dropping the channel.

Example

prefetch heuristics:
  fast handler (<10ms), low RTT      -> 50-200
  medium handler (100ms-1s)          -> 10-30
  slow handler (>10s)                -> 1-5
  formula: ceil(RTT + processing) / processing, times concurrency

memory: unacked <= prefetch x consumers x avg message size
  200 prefetch x 50 consumers x 100 KB = 1 GB held in the broker

long handler + consumer_timeout (default 30 min) -> channel closed,
message requeued, work repeated. Either raise the timeout deliberately
or move the long work off the delivery (claim it, ack, process, report).

Follow-ups

  • What is global versus per-consumer QoS? — global=false applies the count per consumer (what you almost always want); global=true applies it across the whole channel, which surprises people running several consumers on one channel.
  • How does prefetch interact with fairness? — Prefetch 1 gives perfect fairness and poor throughput; high prefetch gives good throughput and lets a fast consumer hoard. Pick by whether your messages have uniform cost.
  • What is the equivalent problem in Kafka? — max.poll.records and max.poll.interval.ms: fetch too much and a slow batch exceeds the interval, gets you evicted, and triggers a rebalance. Same shape, different names.

Common wrong answer

"Set prefetch high for throughput." It is half true and the other half is the failure: a consumer holding 5,000 unacked messages will requeue all of them on a crash, spike broker memory, and starve its peers. Say what the handler's duration is before you name a number.

seniorRabbitMQ streams versus Kafka: when is the RabbitMQ option the right one?

Answer

RabbitMQ streams (3.9+) are an append-only replicated log with offset-based, non-destructive consumption, a dedicated binary protocol for high throughput, and retention by size or time — the same shape as a Kafka topic. They are the right choice when you already run RabbitMQ and need replay or many independent readers for a subset of your traffic: you avoid operating a second broker, you keep one authentication and monitoring surface, and you can mix streams and queues in one virtual host, even publishing to a stream through the ordinary AMQP exchange model. They are the wrong choice when you need Kafka's ecosystem rather than its data model — Connect, Streams, ksqlDB, schema registry, the entire tooling economy — or when you are operating at the scale where Kafka's partition-and-broker model and tiered storage have been proven for a decade. The honest interview answer is that the decision is rarely about the log semantics, which are similar; it is about the ecosystem and about how many brokers your team can competently run.

  • What is a superstream? — RabbitMQ's partitioned stream: a logical stream backed by several streams with a routing key, giving Kafka-style parallelism with per-key ordering.
  • Do streams reduce the case for Kafka? — For a single team's replay needs, yes. For an organisation-wide event backbone with dozens of consumers and connectors, the ecosystem argument usually still wins.
  • Anything similar happening in the other direction? — Yes, symmetrically: Kafka 4.2 share groups bring queue semantics to Kafka. The two products are converging on each other's strengths, which is worth saying — it shows you track the space rather than a 2018 comparison table.

Common wrong answer

"RabbitMQ cannot do event streaming." It has since 3.9. The current, defensible statement is that RabbitMQ streams cover the data model but not the ecosystem, and that the choice usually turns on tooling and operational capacity rather than on capability.

seniorAMQP 0.9.1 versus AMQP 1.0: what actually changed, and does it matter to you?

Answer

They share a name and almost nothing else. AMQP 0.9.1 is the protocol most people mean by "AMQP": it specifies the broker model — exchanges, queues, bindings — so a client can declare topology, and it is what the classic RabbitMQ clients speak. AMQP 1.0 is an OASIS and ISO standard that defines only the wire protocol for moving messages between two nodes: symmetric peers, links with credit-based flow control, and no opinion at all about exchanges or queues, which is why it is the protocol used by Azure Service Bus, ActiveMQ Artemis and Solace. RabbitMQ has supported AMQP 1.0 for years via a plugin and made it a first-class, natively supported protocol in 4.0, with 4.3 adding rejection reasons and queue names returned to publishers plus consumer-activity notifications through flow frames. It matters to you in three places: credit-based flow control gives cleaner backpressure than 0.9.1's prefetch, interoperability across vendors becomes real, and link recovery (used by 4.3's graceful consumer timeouts) is a better failure model than closing a channel.

Example

AMQP 0.9.1                       AMQP 1.0
  broker model in the protocol     wire protocol only
  channels, exchanges, queues      sessions, links, credit
  prefetch (basic.qos)             link credit (flow frames)
  client declares topology         topology is out of band
  RabbitMQ-centric                 Azure Service Bus, Artemis, Solace, RabbitMQ 4.x

RabbitMQ 4.3 additions on 1.0: rejection reasons + queue name to the
publisher; consumer activity signalled via flow frame properties.

Follow-ups

  • Should new RabbitMQ applications use 1.0? — Increasingly yes on 4.x, especially where you want standard interoperability or the better flow-control and recovery semantics; 0.9.1 remains fully supported and is what most existing code speaks.
  • What about MQTT and STOMP? — RabbitMQ speaks both via plugins; MQTT matters for IoT and mobile fan-out, where its QoS levels and small footprint beat AMQP.
  • Is JMS a protocol? — No, it is a Java API; it can run over AMQP 1.0, over a vendor protocol, or over anything else. Conflating the two is a common muddle in enterprise contexts.

Common wrong answer

"AMQP 1.0 is just a newer version of 0.9.1." They are incompatible protocols with different models and different working groups; a 0.9.1 client cannot talk 1.0. Interviewers in enterprise integration ask this specifically because the assumption causes real integration failures.

principalPlan the migration of a large estate from RabbitMQ 3.8 mirrored queues to 4.x quorum queues.

Answer

Treat it as a two-axis migration — version and queue type — and never do both in one step. Sequence: inventory every queue with its owner, throughput, message size, TTL and priority use, because the migration risk is concentrated in the queues with unusual settings; upgrade through the supported chain to 3.13 first (mirroring is deprecated but present), so version and queue type are not changing at once; then convert queues to quorum type in waves, lowest-risk first, using new queue names plus a shovel or a dual-consume period rather than in-place conversion, because a quorum queue cannot simply replace a classic queue with the same name and arguments. Watch for the specific incompatibilities — per-message TTL differences, priority handling (32 strict levels in 4.3 rather than the classic model), memory profile changes, and the absence of some classic-only arguments — and validate each wave with a load test at production message size, since quorum queues have a different IO profile. Only once every queue is quorum do you move to 4.x, where mirroring no longer exists; and plan the 4.3 step separately again, because Khepri replacing Mnesia is its own metadata migration. Throughout, the thing to say to an interviewer is that the rollback plan is per wave and the blast radius is one team's queues, not the estate.

Example

Wave plan (each wave is independently revertible):
  0  inventory + dashboards per queue (rate, size, unacked, TTL, priority)
  1  upgrade 3.8 -> 3.13 (mirroring still available, no semantic change)
  2  convert non-critical queues to quorum, new names, dual consume,
     drain the old, delete. Load test at production message size.
  3  convert critical queues, one team at a time, with the owner on call
  4  upgrade to 4.x  (mirroring is gone: nothing left to break)
  5  upgrade to 4.3  (Mnesia -> Khepri metadata migration, its own change
     window; partition-handling settings must be removed from config)

Follow-ups

  • Why new queue names rather than in-place conversion? — Queue type is immutable at declaration, and a rename gives you a reversible cutover: consumers read both, producers switch, the old queue drains, then it is deleted.
  • What surprises teams most? — Memory and disk profile: quorum queues write everything through Raft, so a queue that ran happily as a deep classic queue may need a shorter backlog or larger nodes. 4.3's halved per-message overhead below 32 KiB helps materially here.
  • How do you keep this from taking a year? — Automate the inventory and the conversion, publish a dashboard of remaining classic queues by team, and set a date after which new classic queues are rejected by policy. Migrations without a deadline and a visible burndown do not finish.

Common wrong answer

"Upgrade to 4.x and the queues convert automatically." They do not: mirrored queues are gone in 4.0 and an unmigrated estate loses its replication model at the moment of upgrade. This is the question where interviewers check whether you read release notes before planning a migration.

08Sagas and distributed transactions

Once services own their own data, "how do you keep two services consistent" becomes the design question, and the saga is the standard answer. Interviewers use this unit to test whether you can hold a multi-step failure model in your head: what happens when step three fails after step two committed, what a compensation actually does, and who owns the state machine. At principal level the questions become organisational — orchestration versus choreography is as much a team-boundary decision as a technical one — and the strongest candidates are the ones who ask whether the transaction should have been distributed at all.

screenerWhat is a saga?

Answer

A saga is a sequence of local transactions across several services, where each step commits independently and each has a compensating action that semantically undoes it if a later step fails. It gives you atomicity in the "all or nothing eventually" sense — either every step completes or the completed ones are compensated — without holding locks across services, which is what makes it viable where two-phase commit is not. The property it does not give you is isolation: between the first step and the last, other transactions can observe the intermediate state, so a partially completed saga is visible and must be either tolerated or masked. Sagas come in two shapes, choreography (each service reacts to events) and orchestration (a coordinator drives the steps), and the choice between them is the follow-up you should expect immediately.

Example

Order saga, forward path and compensations:

  1 create order (pending)      compensate: cancel order
  2 reserve stock               compensate: release stock
  3 authorise payment           compensate: void authorisation
  4 schedule shipment           compensate: cancel shipment
  5 confirm order               (pivot: after this, no going back)

step 3 fails -> run 2's and 1's compensations, in reverse order
step 5 is the PIVOT: everything after it must be retried forward, not undone

Follow-ups

  • What is a pivot transaction? — The step after which compensation is impossible or unacceptable (the money left the building, the email went out). Steps before it are compensatable; steps after must be retriable until they succeed. Identifying the pivot is the core design act.
  • Does a saga guarantee consistency? — Eventual, semantic consistency: the invariant holds once the saga terminates. In between it does not hold, and the design must say what a reader sees during that window.
  • Where does the saga state live? — Somewhere durable and queryable, always. A saga whose state exists only as in-flight messages cannot be operated: you cannot answer "how many sagas are stuck".

Common wrong answer

"A saga is a distributed transaction." It is explicitly not one: there is no atomic commit and no isolation. Calling it a distributed transaction leads people to assume rollback semantics they do not have, and then to be surprised that a customer saw a half-completed order.

midChoreography or orchestration — how do you choose?

Answer

Choreography means each service listens for events and decides its own next action; there is no central component, coupling is low, and adding a participant is a new subscriber. Orchestration means one component holds the workflow, sends commands and waits for replies; the process is explicit in one place, observable, and testable. Choreography wins for short flows with two or three participants and for flows that are genuinely reactive; it degrades badly beyond that, because the process exists nowhere — you reconstruct it from logs and from reading five services' subscriptions, and cyclic event chains become possible. Orchestration wins for anything with more than a handful of steps, with timeouts, with compensation ordering, or with a business owner who will ask "where is order 12345 stuck" — because that question has an answer. My default at lead level is orchestration for business processes and choreography for notifications and side effects, and I say out loud that the orchestrator must not become a god service: it owns the sequence, not the business rules.

Example

Choreography                         Orchestration
  Order -> OrderPlaced                 Orchestrator -> ReserveStock (cmd)
  Stock hears it -> StockReserved      Stock -> StockReserved (reply)
  Payment hears it -> PaymentTaken     Orchestrator -> AuthorisePayment (cmd)
  Shipping hears it -> Shipped         Payment -> PaymentAuthorised (reply)
                                       Orchestrator -> ScheduleShipment (cmd)

"Where is order 12345?"
  choreography: grep four services' logs and correlate by id
  orchestration: SELECT state FROM saga WHERE order_id = 12345

Follow-ups

  • How do you keep an orchestrator from becoming a monolith? — It sends commands and records state; it must not contain the participants' business rules. If the orchestrator needs to know how stock is allocated, the boundary is wrong.
  • Can you mix them? — Yes, and most real systems do: an orchestrated core process that emits events which other services react to freely. State it explicitly rather than drifting into it.
  • What is the cost of choreography at scale? — Emergent behaviour: an event that triggers a chain nobody designed, cycles, and a process whose true definition lives in the union of everyone's subscriptions. It is the "we have no idea what happens when an order is placed" failure.

Common wrong answer

"Choreography is better because it is more decoupled." Decoupled at the code level, coupled at the semantic level, and unobservable at the operational level. Interviewers push back with "the saga is stuck, find out where" — and a candidate who cannot answer that quickly has just demonstrated the cost.

midWhat makes a good compensating transaction?

Answer

It must be semantic rather than a rollback, idempotent, and eventually guaranteed to succeed. Semantic means it undoes the business effect, not the bytes: you do not delete the payment row, you post a refund; you do not remove the shipment, you cancel it and record why. Idempotent because compensations are retried like everything else, so "release the reservation" must be safe to run five times. Guaranteed to succeed means it must not itself depend on a fragile external call whose failure leaves the saga in limbo — and where it does, the compensation needs its own retry-forever policy plus an escalation to a human. The design constraints that follow are worth naming: compensations run in reverse order, they must be possible at all (so identify the pivot before you design the flow), and they should be visible in the domain rather than hidden — a cancelled order with a reason is better than an order that silently disappears, both for the customer and for the auditor.

Example

// bad: a "rollback" that loses the audit trail and is not idempotent
await _db.Payments.Where(p => p.OrderId == id).ExecuteDeleteAsync();

// good: semantic, idempotent, auditable
await _payments.RefundAsync(new RefundCommand {
    IdempotencyKey = $"saga:{sagaId}:compensate:payment",
    OriginalPaymentId = paymentId,
    Reason = "saga-compensation:stock-unavailable"
});
// a second execution with the same key returns the first refund, not a second

Follow-ups

  • What if the compensation fails permanently? — The saga enters a "needs intervention" state with an alert and a human-facing view. It must be a first-class state, not an exception in a log: unattended stuck sagas are how money goes missing.
  • Can you always compensate? — No, and pretending otherwise is the design error. An email sent, a physical package shipped, an external settlement — these are pivots, so order the saga to put irreversible steps last.
  • Do compensations need to be ordered? — Usually reverse order, because later steps may depend on earlier ones. Where steps are independent you may compensate in parallel, which is a small but genuine speedup for wide sagas.

Common wrong answer

"Undo the database writes." A saga's steps are already committed and visible; deleting rows destroys the audit trail, breaks anything that read them, and cannot undo effects outside the database. Compensation is a new forward transaction, and saying that sentence is what shows you understand the model.

seniorWhy not two-phase commit?

Answer

Two-phase commit gives real atomicity, and it pays for it with blocking: participants hold locks between prepare and commit, and if the coordinator fails after prepare, participants are stuck holding those locks with no way to decide — the classic blocking problem, which three-phase commit addresses only under synchronous-network assumptions that do not hold. In practice it also requires every participant to support the protocol (which cloud services, HTTP APIs and most modern brokers do not), it couples availability multiplicatively (all participants must be up for the duration), and it scales poorly because lock hold times grow with the slowest participant. That is why it survives mainly inside a single database or in legacy on-premises estates with a shared transaction manager. The nuance worth adding is that consensus-based systems solve the coordinator failure — Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos groups so the coordinator itself is fault-tolerant, which is exactly why it can offer cross-shard transactions — so the honest statement is not "2PC is broken" but "2PC without a fault-tolerant coordinator blocks, and across organisational and service boundaries you cannot have one".

Example

coordinator          A            B
   prepare  --------> ok
   prepare  ----------------------> ok        (locks held on A and B)
   *** coordinator dies ***
   A and B: cannot commit, cannot abort, must hold locks and wait.

Spanner's answer: the coordinator is a Paxos group, not a process.
Microservices' answer: don't. Use a saga, accept eventual consistency.

Follow-ups

  • Where is 2PC still correct? — Inside one database across shards, and in legacy estates with XA and a hardened transaction manager. If you already have one and it works, replacing it with a saga is not automatically an improvement.
  • Does the outbox use 2PC? — No, and that is its whole point: it turns a two-resource problem into one local transaction plus an asynchronous relay.
  • What about "try-confirm-cancel"? — TCC is a saga variant where the first phase reserves rather than commits (a hold on the stock, an authorisation rather than a capture). It gives better isolation than a plain saga and needs participants to model reservations explicitly.

Common wrong answer

"2PC is slow." Slow is a symptom; blocking is the reason. The failure that matters is a coordinator crash between prepare and commit leaving participants unable to decide — and a candidate who only says "slow" usually then proposes 3PC, which does not solve it in an asynchronous network.

seniorSagas have no isolation. How do you deal with the anomalies?

Answer

The anomalies have names — lost updates, dirty reads, and fuzzy or non-repeatable reads across the saga's lifetime — and the countermeasures are standard. A semantic lock marks the entity with an in-progress state (order status "pending", funds "on hold") so other transactions can see that a saga owns it and behave accordingly; that is the most common and the cheapest. Commutative updates make the operations order-insensitive (increment and decrement rather than set), so a concurrent saga cannot lose an update. Pessimistic view reorders the saga's steps so the risky, hard-to-compensate step happens where its dirty read matters least. Re-read value checks that the data has not changed before writing, which turns a lost update into a detectable conflict. And by-value routes high-value transactions to a stricter mechanism entirely — a synchronous, coordinated path for the 1% where the money justifies it. The lead-level move is choosing per flow rather than globally, and saying which anomaly you have accepted.

Example

-- semantic lock: the in-progress state is part of the domain, and visible
UPDATE orders SET status = 'payment_pending', saga_id = @saga
WHERE id = @id AND status = 'created';         -- 0 rows = another saga owns it

-- commutative: safe under concurrency, no lost update
UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + @qty WHERE sku = @sku;

-- re-read value: detect the conflict rather than overwrite it
UPDATE prices SET amount = @new WHERE sku = @sku AND amount = @observed;

Follow-ups

  • What does the UI show during a semantic lock? — The pending state, honestly ("payment processing"), plus what the user can and cannot do. Hiding it produces support tickets; showing it is usually fine.
  • How long may a semantic lock last? — Bounded by a timeout, always, with an automatic release path — otherwise a crashed orchestrator leaves entities locked forever, which is the most common saga incident.
  • Do these anomalies matter for reporting? — Yes, and it is where they surface first: a report run mid-saga counts an order that will be cancelled. Either filter by terminal states or accept and document the skew.

Common wrong answer

"Use a distributed lock across the services." It reintroduces the coupling and the blocking that the saga existed to avoid, and a distributed lock without fencing tokens is not even safe. The saga answer is a semantic, domain-visible state, not an infrastructure lock.

seniorHow do you implement a durable orchestrator, and when do you reach for a workflow engine?

Answer

The hand-rolled version is a persisted state machine: a row per saga instance with its state, the correlation ids, a version for optimistic concurrency and a next-timeout column; a handler that loads the instance, applies an event, decides the next command, and persists the new state and the outgoing command in one transaction (an outbox row, so the command cannot be lost); and a timer sweep that finds instances whose timeout has passed and drives them forward. That is perhaps 300 lines and it is well within a team's ability, which is why frameworks such as MassTransit, NServiceBus and Rebus provide it as a saga abstraction on .NET. You reach for a workflow engine — Temporal, Azure Durable Functions, AWS Step Functions, Camunda — when the process is long-running (days to months), has many steps, needs versioning of in-flight instances, human tasks, or first-class visibility; those engines give durable execution, retries, timers and a history you can query, and the price is a new platform dependency and a programming model your team must learn. The decision heuristic I would state: fewer than five steps and minutes-long, hand-roll it; longer or business-visible, use an engine.

Example

CREATE TABLE saga_instance (
  id            uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  type          text NOT NULL,
  state         text NOT NULL,             -- awaiting_stock, awaiting_payment...
  correlation   jsonb NOT NULL,            -- orderId, paymentId, shipmentId
  data          jsonb NOT NULL,
  version       int  NOT NULL,             -- optimistic concurrency
  timeout_at    timestamptz,               -- driven by the sweeper
  updated_at    timestamptz NOT NULL
);
CREATE INDEX ON saga_instance (timeout_at) WHERE timeout_at IS NOT NULL;
-- handler: load -> apply -> persist state + outbox command in ONE transaction

Follow-ups

  • How do you version a saga while instances are in flight? — Either keep the old definition running for existing instances (engines do this by pinning a version) or design states so a new definition can pick up an old instance. Never deploy a change that leaves in-flight instances in an unknown state.
  • What is the .NET landscape in 2026? — MassTransit v9 moved to commercial licensing (v8 stays Apache 2.0 but loses support at the end of 2026), which pushed teams toward Wolverine and Rebus (both MIT) or NServiceBus (commercial, with tooling). It is a live architectural decision worth mentioning if the role is .NET.
  • Where does durable execution differ from a saga framework? — Temporal-style engines replay your code deterministically from an event history, so the workflow reads as ordinary sequential code; a saga framework has you write explicit state transitions. Durable execution is more ergonomic and more opinionated about determinism.

Common wrong answer

"Keep the saga state in memory and rebuild from events on restart." In-memory state plus a process restart equals lost sagas, and rebuilding by replaying an entire topic is neither fast nor safe for compensations that already ran. The state must be durable and transactional with the commands it emits.

seniorHow do you handle timeouts and stuck sagas?

Answer

Every state that waits for something must have a timeout, and the timeout must be persisted rather than held in a process — an in-memory timer dies with the pod and the saga waits forever. On expiry the saga takes a defined transition: retry the outstanding command (if the participant may simply have missed it and the command is idempotent), compensate and terminate, or move to a needs-intervention state. Then you need the operational layer: a metric for sagas by state and age, an alert when any instance exceeds its expected duration by a margin, and a query or admin view that lets a human see and act on stuck instances. The subtlety interviewers probe is the race between the timeout and a late reply: if the saga compensates at T+60s and the participant's success reply arrives at T+61s, you now have a completed step in a compensated saga — so the saga must handle late replies explicitly, either by compensating the late step or by refusing it with a saga-level version check.

Example

state              timeout   on timeout
awaiting_stock     30s       retry ReserveStock (idempotent), 3x, then compensate
awaiting_payment   120s      query payment status first, THEN decide
awaiting_shipment  24h       needs-intervention (a human decides)
compensating       10m       alert; never auto-terminate a failed compensation

late reply after compensation:
  saga is in 'cancelled'; reply carries saga version 4, saga is at 5
  -> reject and compensate the late step, do not resurrect the saga

Follow-ups

  • Why "query then decide" for payments? — Because a timeout is not a failure. Compensating a payment that actually succeeded creates a refund the customer did not ask for; asking the provider for the status of the idempotency key is the correct first move.
  • Where do the timers live? — In the saga store with a sweeper, in a broker-native delayed message, or in the workflow engine. All three are fine; a Task.Delay is not.
  • What is the SLO for a saga? — Completion within a target time for a percentile, plus zero instances in needs-intervention older than the response target. Both belong on the same dashboard as the queue metrics.

Common wrong answer

"If it times out, we compensate." Sometimes right, and dangerous as a blanket rule: a timeout means unknown, so compensating without checking can undo a step that succeeded. Query the participant's state first wherever the participant can answer.

seniorHow do you test a saga?

Answer

Four layers, and the interesting ones are the last two. Unit-test the state machine as a pure function — given state and event, assert the next state and the emitted commands — which is fast and covers the transition table exhaustively including the compensation paths. Then contract-test each participant so the commands and replies the orchestrator expects match what the participant actually accepts and emits; that catches the schema drift that integration tests find late and expensively. Then failure-inject systematically: for each step, assert that a failure produces the correct compensations in the correct order, and specifically test duplicate delivery, out-of-order replies, and a late reply arriving after a timeout, because those three are where real sagas break. Finally, property-test if you can: generate random interleavings of replies, failures and duplicates, and assert the invariant (no stock reserved without a paid order, no payment without an order) holds in every terminal state. The line to say in an interview is that the transition table is testable in milliseconds, so there is no excuse for the compensation paths being untested — and in practice they usually are.

Example

[Theory]
[InlineData(SagaState.AwaitingStock,   typeof(StockUnavailable),
            SagaState.Compensating,    new[] { typeof(CancelOrder) })]
[InlineData(SagaState.AwaitingPayment, typeof(PaymentDeclined),
            SagaState.Compensating,    new[] { typeof(ReleaseStock), typeof(CancelOrder) })]
public void Transition(SagaState from, Type evt, SagaState to, Type[] commands)
{
    var (next, emitted) = OrderSaga.Apply(from, Sample(evt));
    Assert.Equal(to, next);
    Assert.Equal(commands, emitted.Select(c => c.GetType()));
}

// and the three that actually break in production:
//   duplicate reply, reply for an old saga version, reply after timeout

Follow-ups

  • Do you need an end-to-end test? — One or two happy-path and one compensation path, as a smoke test. Trying to cover the matrix end to end is slow and flaky; cover the matrix at the unit level.
  • How do you test the timers? — Inject the clock. A saga that reads DateTime.UtcNow directly cannot have its timeout behaviour tested, which is why the timeout path is the least-tested part of most sagas.
  • What about production verification? — A continuous invariant check over the data (no reservation without an order in a valid state) is worth more than another integration test, because it catches the interleavings you did not imagine.

Common wrong answer

"We test it end to end in the staging environment." Staging exercises the happy path and rarely the compensations, never the late reply, and gives no coverage signal. Interviewers hear it as "the compensation code has never run".

principalWhen is a saga the wrong answer?

Answer

When the steps belong to one consistency boundary and the split is accidental rather than essential. If two services always change together, always fail together, and are owned by the same team, the saga is compensating for a bad boundary — and the right fix is to merge them or move the data, which removes the distributed transaction instead of managing it. It is also wrong when the intermediate state is unacceptable to the business (a regulated flow where a half-completed state is a compliance breach), when compensations are impossible for most steps so you would be building a retry-forever pipeline with saga vocabulary, and when the flow is short and synchronous and the participants can genuinely be called in a request with an idempotency key. At principal level the strongest version of this answer names the diagnostic: count how often a change to one participant forces a coordinated change to another, and count how many sagas end in needs-intervention. High numbers on either mean the boundary is wrong, and the architectural work is redrawing it, not adding compensation logic.

Example

Signals that the boundary, not the saga, is the problem:
  * every saga step touches the same aggregate's lifecycle
  * compensations are near-total (undo everything, always)
  * participants are deployed together and owned by one team
  * the "distributed" transaction has no participant outside your process
  * >1% of instances end in needs-intervention

The alternative to a saga is often a single transaction after moving
one table - which is a smaller change than the saga you were about to write.

Follow-ups

  • How do you argue for merging two services? — With the numbers: coordinated releases per quarter, incidents caused by partial completion, engineer-hours spent on compensation logic. Boundary arguments made on aesthetics lose; made on cost they win.
  • Is a saga ever a stepping stone? — Yes: a legitimate use is to make an existing distributed flow safe while you fix the boundary, as long as you write down that it is temporary and what "done" looks like.
  • What if the participants are external? — Then the saga is essential — you cannot merge with a payment provider — and the design question shifts to idempotency keys, status queries and reconciliation with their ledger.

Common wrong answer

"Microservices need sagas, so we use them everywhere." That is a pattern applied by category rather than by need, and it produces compensation code paths that never run correctly because they are never exercised. The senior instinct is to ask whether the transaction should cross the boundary at all.

09Consensus, replication and leader election

Consensus is where interviewers check for depth rather than breadth: you are not expected to implement Raft, but you are expected to explain why a majority is required, what a fencing token is for, and why the distributed lock in the codebase is probably unsafe. Screener loops skip this unit; senior and staff loops usually spend ten minutes here, and the reliable discriminator is whether you understand that a process pause is indistinguishable from a crash, and design accordingly.

midWhat problem does consensus solve, and which algorithms should you be able to name?

Answer

Consensus is a set of nodes agreeing on a single value — in practice, on the next entry in a replicated log — such that the agreement holds despite crashes and message loss, and every node that decides decides the same thing. It underpins leader election, cluster membership, distributed locks, configuration stores and any replicated state machine. The algorithms to name are Paxos (the original, notoriously hard to implement, with Multi-Paxos for a stream of decisions), Raft (designed for understandability, with explicit leader election, log replication and safety rules; the basis of etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, RabbitMQ quorum queues and Kafka's KRaft), Zab (ZooKeeper's protocol), and Viewstamped Replication (predates Paxos, closely related). It is also worth naming the boundary: FLP proves that no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus in a fully asynchronous system with even one crash failure, which is why real systems use timeouts as an imperfect failure detector and therefore accept an availability gap during elections rather than a correctness gap.

Example

Where you meet consensus without calling it that:
  etcd / Consul          Raft      Kubernetes' entire control plane
  Kafka KRaft            Raft      controller quorum, metadata log
  RabbitMQ quorum queues Raft      one Raft group PER QUEUE
  ZooKeeper              Zab       HBase, older Kafka, Hadoop
  Spanner / CockroachDB  Paxos/Raft per-shard replication groups

Cost, roughly: one majority round trip per decision. Within an AZ a few ms;
across regions 70-150ms, which is why nobody puts a global Raft group
in a user-facing write path.

Follow-ups

  • What is FLP in one sentence? — In an asynchronous system you cannot distinguish a slow node from a dead one, so no algorithm can guarantee both safety and termination; real systems keep safety and give up guaranteed termination by using timeouts.
  • Do you need consensus for everything? — No, and the design skill is minimising it: consensus for membership, leadership and metadata; eventual consistency or CRDTs for the high-volume data path.
  • What is Byzantine fault tolerance? — Consensus that tolerates nodes lying rather than merely crashing; it needs 3f+1 nodes to tolerate f faulty ones and is relevant to blockchains and adversarial settings, not to your internal cluster.

Common wrong answer

"Consensus means all nodes agree." All live nodes eventually agree, and only a majority is needed to decide — requiring all of them would make the system unavailable on any single failure, which is the opposite of the goal.

midExplain Raft: election, replication, and what keeps it safe.

Answer

Time is divided into terms; each term has at most one leader. A follower that hears no heartbeat within a randomised election timeout becomes a candidate, increments the term and requests votes; a node grants at most one vote per term and only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own, so a candidate needs a majority to win and any winner necessarily has all committed entries. The leader then serves all client writes: it appends to its log, replicates to followers, and marks an entry committed once a majority have stored it, after which it is applied to the state machine and the result returned. Safety comes from three rules — election restriction (only an up-to-date log can win), log matching (if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, all preceding entries are identical), and the rule that a leader never commits an entry from a previous term by counting replicas, only by committing an entry of its own term. Randomised timeouts (commonly 150-300ms) are what prevent repeated split votes.

Example

5 nodes, majority = 3

term 7  leader L, followers A B C D
        L appends entry idx=42, replicates
        A, B ack   -> 3 copies including L  -> COMMITTED, apply, reply
        C, D catch up later

L dies. A's election timer fires first (randomised), term 8, requests votes.
B and C grant (A's log is up to date). A wins with 3 of 5, becomes leader.
D, which was behind, cannot win: its log is not up to date -> safety.

Availability gap = one election timeout, typically 150ms-1s.

Follow-ups

  • Why randomised timeouts? — Identical timeouts make every follower campaign simultaneously, split the vote, and repeat. Randomisation makes one node reliably go first.
  • How does a Raft cluster change membership safely? — Joint consensus (or single-node-at-a-time changes): the configuration change goes through the log itself, so there is never a moment when two disjoint majorities exist.
  • How do you serve a linearizable read? — Read index (leader confirms leadership with a heartbeat round, then reads locally once applied) or a leader lease. Reading from a follower without either can return stale data.

Common wrong answer

"The node with the most recent data becomes leader." Close but imprecise: any node whose log is at least as up to date as a majority can win — Raft does not find the most advanced node, it merely excludes ones that are behind. The distinction is what makes elections fast.

seniorWhy an odd number of nodes, and how much failure does each size tolerate?

Answer

A quorum is a majority, so a cluster of N tolerates floor((N-1)/2) failures. Three tolerates one, five tolerates two, seven tolerates three. Even sizes give you nothing extra: four tolerates one, exactly like three, while costing an extra node and making every write wait for three acknowledgements instead of two — so odd sizes are strictly better on both cost and latency. The other half of the answer is why you should not simply keep growing: every decision needs a majority, so latency is bounded by the slowest node in the majority and write throughput degrades as N rises; five is the usual maximum for a control plane, and seven is rare. Where you place them matters as much as how many: three nodes in three availability zones survives one zone; three nodes in two zones does not, because losing the zone with two nodes destroys the majority. That last point is the one interviewers most often use to separate memorised arithmetic from actual design.

Example

N   majority   tolerates   notes
1   1          0           no fault tolerance at all
2   2          0           WORSE than 1: both must be up
3   2          1           the standard
4   3          1           same tolerance as 3, slower, more expensive
5   3          2           control planes that must survive a double fault
7   4          3           rare; latency and throughput cost is real

Placement:
  3 nodes / 3 AZs  -> survives one AZ
  3 nodes / 2 AZs  -> losing the 2-node AZ loses the quorum
  5 nodes / 2 regions -> the 2-node region is useless alone; use 3 regions
                         or accept that one region is the majority holder

Follow-ups

  • What does a witness or arbiter node do? — Participates in voting without storing data, letting a two-datacentre deployment get an odd count and a tiebreaker cheaply. MongoDB arbiters and similar mechanisms exist for exactly this.
  • Can you survive losing the majority? — Not automatically, and any tool that lets you "force" a new quorum is a data-loss button. Use it only as a documented disaster procedure with an accepted RPO.
  • Why does write latency grow with N? — A write commits when a majority acknowledges, so it waits for the (N+1)/2-th fastest node; more nodes means a further-out order statistic and more tail exposure.

Common wrong answer

"More nodes means more availability." For a consensus group it means more fault tolerance up to a point and less throughput and higher latency throughout. Availability for reads scales with replicas; availability for writes is a majority question, and beyond five the trade is almost always negative.

seniorWhat is split brain, and how do fencing tokens prevent damage?

Answer

Split brain is two nodes both believing they are the leader, which happens whenever a leader is isolated or paused long enough for the cluster to elect a replacement while the old leader has not yet noticed. You cannot prevent the old leader from believing it is still the leader — it has no way to know — so the fix is to make the resource reject its writes. A fencing token is a monotonically increasing number issued with leadership (Raft's term, ZooKeeper's zxid, a database sequence); the leader includes it in every request to the shared resource, and the resource remembers the highest token it has seen and rejects anything lower. Now the deposed leader's late write is refused deterministically, regardless of what it believes. The essential insight, and the one interviewers listen for, is that leadership is not something the leader can verify locally: a process that was paused for 40 seconds by a GC or by the hypervisor wakes up with a valid-looking lease and no idea that time has passed.

Example

t=0    L1 is leader (token 33), starts writing
t=1    L1 pauses (GC / VM migration / SIGSTOP)
t=12   cluster times L1 out, elects L2 (token 34)
t=13   L2 writes to storage with token 34   -> accepted, highest seen = 34
t=15   L1 resumes, completes its write with token 33
       -> storage REJECTS: 33 < 34. Damage prevented.

Without fencing, L1's write silently overwrites L2's. This is the
classic dual-writer corruption, and it is why "check the lock, then act"
is not sufficient.

Follow-ups

  • What if the resource cannot check tokens? — Then you cannot fence it, and you must reduce exposure instead: make the operation idempotent and conditional (compare-and-set on a version), or route all access through something that can check. S3 conditional writes and DynamoDB condition expressions are the usual modern answers.
  • Is STONITH still a thing? — In on-premises HA, yes: shoot the other node in the head — power-fence it — because that is the only way to be sure it stops. It is heavy, and fencing tokens are preferable where the resource can cooperate.
  • Where do you get a token? — Raft term or index, ZooKeeper's zxid or a sequential znode, etcd's revision, a database sequence, or a lease with a version in a conditional-write store.

Common wrong answer

"We use a lock with a TTL, so a dead leader releases it automatically." A TTL bounds how long the lock is held; it does not stop the previous holder from acting after it expired. Without fencing, a paused holder wakes up and writes into a lock somebody else now owns.

seniorIs a Redis-based distributed lock safe? Make the case.

Answer

A single-instance Redis lock (SET key value NX PX ttl with a unique value, released by a Lua script that checks the value) is a fine efficiency lock: if it occasionally fails, you do duplicate work and nothing is corrupted. It is not a correctness lock, for two reasons. First, Redis replication is asynchronous, so a failover can lose the lock and grant it to a second holder. Second — and this applies to any lock without fencing — a process pause longer than the TTL means the holder acts after expiry. Redlock, the multi-instance algorithm, addresses the failover point but not the pause point, and the well-known Kleppmann critique argues its timing assumptions do not hold in an asynchronous system; Antirez's response is that it is fine for the efficiency case, which is roughly where the industry landed. So the honest interview answer is: use Redis locks to avoid duplicate work, use a fencing token plus a conditional write at the resource for anything where two writers would corrupt data, and use etcd, ZooKeeper or a database transaction when you need real mutual exclusion.

Example

acquire:  SET lock:job1  NX PX 30000
release:  EVAL "if redis.call('get',KEYS[1])==ARGV[1]
                then return redis.call('del',KEYS[1]) else return 0 end"
                1 lock:job1 
          (never a bare DEL: you would delete someone else's lock)

Safe for:   "only one worker should do this expensive job" (efficiency)
Unsafe for: "only one writer may touch this file" (correctness)
Correct:    lease + fencing token, resource enforces monotonicity
            or: a database transaction / etcd lease with revision

Follow-ups

  • What is the minimum bar for any lock implementation? — Unique owner value, atomic acquire with expiry, owner-checked release, and a plan for what happens when the holder is slower than the TTL.
  • How do you extend a lock safely? — A watchdog that renews while the work is running, and a check after each renewal that you still hold it; if a renewal fails you must stop the work, not carry on.
  • What is the alternative in a database? — SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, a PostgreSQL advisory lock, or simply a conditional update that makes the operation idempotent so the lock is unnecessary. Removing the need for a lock is usually the better answer.

Common wrong answer

"Redlock gives you a correct distributed lock." It is a contested claim and the interviewer probably knows the debate. The stronger move is to classify the lock as efficiency or correctness and design accordingly — that shows judgment rather than allegiance.

seniorCompare synchronous, asynchronous and semi-synchronous replication.

Answer

Synchronous replication acknowledges a write only after replicas have it, giving zero RPO for the replicated set at the cost of write latency bounded by the slowest replica and of availability — if a synchronous replica is down, writes stop unless you degrade. Asynchronous acknowledges immediately and ships later, giving the best latency and availability and a non-zero RPO: whatever was in flight is lost on failover, which is typically milliseconds to seconds but can be minutes if the replica was lagging. Semi-synchronous is the practical middle: wait for at least one (or k of n) replicas, so you survive a single node loss without paying for all of them — this is exactly what Kafka's acks=all with min.insync.replicas=2 and PostgreSQL's synchronous_standby_names with ANY 1 (...) express. The design guidance is to be synchronous within a low-latency failure domain (across AZs, single-digit milliseconds) and asynchronous across regions, then to state the resulting RPO explicitly — because "we replicate to another region" without a number is not a disaster-recovery plan.

Example

PostgreSQL
  synchronous_commit = off          fastest, loses recent commits on crash
                     = local        durable locally, async to standbys
                     = on           standby has it in WAL (default meaning)
                     = remote_apply standby has APPLIED it (read-your-writes
                                    on replicas, highest latency)
  synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (s1, s2, s3)'   -> semi-sync

Kafka
  acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 on RF=3  -> semi-sync equivalent
Typical numbers: same-AZ 0.3-1ms, cross-AZ 1-3ms, cross-region 30-150ms.

Follow-ups

  • Single-leader, multi-leader or leaderless? — Single-leader for almost everything (simple, no write conflicts, the standard). Multi-leader when you need local writes in several regions and can resolve conflicts (or use CRDTs). Leaderless (Dynamo-style) when you want availability and tunable quorums and can live with read repair and conflict resolution.
  • What is the danger of async replication plus automatic failover? — Silent data loss on promotion, and split brain if the old leader returns. Automatic failover needs fencing and an explicit statement of the acceptable RPO.
  • How do you measure lag properly? — In bytes and in time: bytes tell you the backlog, time tells you your actual RPO. Alert on time.

Common wrong answer

"We use synchronous replication so we cannot lose data." Only within the synchronous set, and only while it is healthy; the moment you allow a degraded mode (or the set is one replica) you are asynchronous with extra steps. Ask what happens when a synchronous replica is down — the answer reveals the real design.

seniorWhat is a replicated state machine, and why is the idea worth knowing?

Answer

A replicated state machine is the standard construction for fault-tolerant services: agree on a totally ordered log of commands via consensus, then have every replica apply the same commands in the same order to a deterministic state machine, so all replicas reach identical state. It is worth knowing because it is the shape of almost every system you rely on — etcd, ZooKeeper, Kafka's KRaft metadata, a RabbitMQ quorum queue, a CockroachDB range — and because it explains the constraints those systems impose. Determinism is the constraint that bites in application code: if the state machine reads the clock, generates a random number, iterates a hash map in nondeterministic order or calls an external service, replicas diverge. That is why event-sourced aggregates must not call out to the world when applying events, why Temporal-style durable execution imposes determinism rules on workflow code, and why "just replay the events" fails when a handler enriched its state from an API that has since changed its answer.

Example

// Deterministic apply: safe to replay on any replica, at any time
public void Apply(OrderPaid e) { Status = OrderStatus.Paid; PaidAt = e.OccurredAt; }

// Non-deterministic: three separate divergence bugs in one method
public void Apply(OrderPaid e) {
    Status  = OrderStatus.Paid;
    PaidAt  = DateTime.UtcNow;                     // clock
    Ref     = Guid.NewGuid();                      // randomness
    Fx      = _rates.GetAsync("USD").Result;       // external call
}
// Rule: decisions (and their inputs) go in the event; apply only applies.

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle a genuinely needed timestamp or id? — Generate it once, at decision time, and put it in the event. Applying the event then just copies it.
  • What is snapshotting for? — Bounding replay cost: periodically persist the state plus the log position, so recovery starts from the snapshot. Every long-lived replicated log needs it, including event-sourced aggregates.
  • Where does this appear in stream processing? — Kafka Streams state stores are replicated state machines over a changelog topic; the changelog is the log, the store is the state machine, and restore is replay.

Common wrong answer

"Replicas stay in sync because they replicate the data." They replicate the log and recompute the data; if the computation is not deterministic, identical logs produce different state. That distinction is precisely why the pattern has rules.

seniorYou need exactly one instance of a background job running across a Kubernetes deployment. Design it.

Answer

First push back on "exactly one", because it is unattainable: what you can have is at most one effective writer, which is one holder of a lease plus fencing at the resource. The standard implementation is a lease in a store with a consensus backend — the Kubernetes coordination.k8s.io/Lease object, which is what controller-manager and scheduler use, or an etcd lease directly. Each replica tries to acquire or renew the lease with its identity; the holder does the work and renews well inside the lease duration (renew at roughly a third of it); a replica that fails to renew must stop working immediately, before the lease can be granted elsewhere. Then add fencing: carry the lease's resource version or a monotonic epoch into every write, and have the target reject stale epochs — because a paused pod will wake up believing it still holds the lease. Finally, make the job itself idempotent and restartable, because a handover mid-work is normal, not exceptional. If the job is genuinely a singleton with no fencing possible, a StatefulSet of one replica narrows the window but does not close it.

Example

// lease-based leader election, with the two rules people skip
var lease = new LeaseLock(ns: "prod", name: "reindex-job", identity: podName);
await lease.RunAsync(new LeaderElectionConfig {
    LeaseDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15),
    RenewDeadline = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(10),
    RetryPeriod   = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2),
    OnStartedLeading = async ct => await RunJobAsync(epoch: lease.Epoch, ct),
    OnStoppedLeading = () => cts.Cancel()   // RULE 1: stop work at once
});
// RULE 2: every write carries the epoch; the sink rejects a lower one
await sink.WriteAsync(batch, ifEpochAtLeast: epoch);

Follow-ups

  • Why not just run one replica? — Because a pod is not a singleton either: during a rolling update, a node drain or a network partition, two pods can briefly exist. One replica reduces the probability; it does not change the correctness argument.
  • How do you size the lease duration? — Long enough to survive a GC pause and a scheduling hiccup (10-30 seconds is common), short enough that failover is acceptable. Renew at about a third of the duration.
  • What if the job takes longer than the lease? — Then it must checkpoint and be interruptible, or renew continuously and abort the moment renewal fails. A long uninterruptible job under a short lease is a dual-writer waiting to happen.

Common wrong answer

"Use a Kubernetes CronJob with concurrencyPolicy: Forbid." That prevents overlapping scheduled runs from the same controller; it does not give leader election, does not fence a paused pod, and does not help a long-running daemon. It is the right tool for periodic batch and the wrong one for a singleton service.

seniorWhy is a GC pause the most dangerous failure mode in a leader-based system?

Answer

Because it is indistinguishable from a crash while it lasts, and unlike a crash the process comes back believing nothing happened. A full GC, a hypervisor migration, a container CPU-throttling event or a swap storm can stop a process for seconds; during that time it misses heartbeats, the cluster elects a new leader, and when the process resumes it continues from the instruction after the pause with a lease it believes is valid. Every "impossible" dual-writer incident traces back to this. The defences are layered: fencing tokens so the resource rejects stale epochs; re-checking leadership after any operation that could have blocked, not just at the start; bounding pause length (a modern low-latency collector, right-sized heaps, no swap, generous CPU limits so the container is not throttled); and lease durations chosen with the observed p99.9 pause in mind rather than a round number. The sentence that lands in an interview is that you cannot detect your own pause, so correctness must live at the resource, not in the process that thinks it is the leader.

Example

Pauses that actually happen, order of magnitude:
  young GC                    < 10ms      harmless
  full GC, large heap         0.5-10s     misses heartbeats
  container CPU throttling    10ms-2s     repeated, invisible without cgroup metrics
  VM live migration           0.1-1s      no signal inside the guest
  swap / page-cache thrash    seconds     the worst: everything is slow, nothing errors
  SIGSTOP (debugger, kill)    unbounded   the fault-injection test to run

Defence: fencing token + short work units + re-check after every blocking call.

Follow-ups

  • How do you detect that this happened? — Look for a gap in your own metrics or logs, GC pause metrics, and the cgroup throttling counters. A leader-change event with no corresponding restart is the tell.
  • Does a shorter lease help? — It shortens the window in which a paused leader can do damage, but it also increases spurious failovers. Without fencing it does not fix anything; with fencing it is a tuning knob.
  • Is this only a JVM problem? — No. .NET, Go and Node all have collectors, and the non-GC causes (throttling, migration, swap) are runtime-independent. Language choice narrows the distribution; it does not remove the tail.

Common wrong answer

"We tuned the GC, so pauses are under 10ms." That is a distribution claim about a system whose tail is what matters, and it does not address throttling, migration or swap. The design must survive an arbitrary pause; tuning only makes it rarer.

10Event-driven architecture and schema evolution

This unit is where lead and principal loops spend their architecture time, because the questions have no single right answer and reveal how you think about other teams. Interviewers probe three things: whether you can distinguish the styles of event-driven design and pick deliberately, whether you treat a schema as a public contract with a lifecycle, and whether you have actually run an event system at organisational scale — which shows up as strong opinions about governance and about who is allowed to break whom.

midDistinguish event notification, event-carried state transfer, event sourcing and CQRS.

Answer

They are four independent choices that people conflate. Event notification publishes a thin fact ("order 42 changed") and consumers call back for details — low coupling to the producer's model, but a callback storm and availability coupling at read time. Event-carried state transfer puts the relevant state in the event, so consumers keep a local copy and never call back — resilient and fast, at the cost of larger events, duplicated data and staleness. Event sourcing is a persistence decision inside one service: the events are the state, current state is a fold over them, and nothing outside needs to know. CQRS separates the write model from one or more read models, which is often paired with event sourcing but is entirely usable without it. The point to make in an interview is that you can adopt any of them alone: most systems benefit from event-carried state transfer between services, most do not need event sourcing, and CQRS is worth it exactly when the read and write shapes genuinely differ.

Example

// notification - thin; consumer must call back
{ "type": "order.updated", "orderId": "42", "occurredAt": "..." }

// event-carried state transfer - self-sufficient
{ "type": "order.shipped", "orderId": "42", "version": 7,
  "customerId": "c-9", "address": { ... }, "lines": [ ... ],
  "shippedAt": "2026-09-08T09:14:02Z", "occurredAt": "..." }

// event sourcing - internal to the producing service
//   append: OrderCreated, LineAdded, OrderPaid, OrderShipped
//   state:  fold(events) + periodic snapshot

Follow-ups

  • How do you choose between notification and state transfer? — By how much your consumers would call back, and by whether they can tolerate the producer being down. If more than a small fraction of events cause an immediate callback, carry the state.
  • Does state transfer duplicate data everywhere? — Yes, deliberately: each consumer keeps the projection it needs. That duplication is what buys independence, and it is only acceptable because the events give you a way to rebuild it.
  • Can you do CQRS without event sourcing? — Absolutely, and it is the common case: write to a normalised store, project to a read store via CDC or an outbox. Far less machinery, most of the benefit.

Common wrong answer

"We do event-driven, so we use event sourcing and CQRS." Bundling all three is the classic over-adoption, and it usually shows up as a team maintaining an event store, projections and versioning machinery for a CRUD service. Name the three decisions separately and justify each.

midHow do you evolve an event schema without breaking consumers?

Answer

Treat the schema as a published API with a compatibility policy enforced automatically. The safe changes are additive: add optional fields with defaults, add new event types, widen an enum only if consumers are written to ignore unknown values. The unsafe ones are removing or renaming a field, narrowing a type, changing units or meaning, and making an optional field required — and the sneaky one is a semantic change with no structural change, such as switching a monetary amount from major units to minor units, which no compatibility checker can catch. For anything unsafe you version explicitly: publish v2 alongside v1, migrate consumers, then retire v1 after a stated deprecation window, with a metric showing who is still on v1 so the retirement is a fact rather than an argument. Carry the version in the message (a header or a registry schema id, not a guess from shape) and, if your organisation is big enough that you cannot see all consumers, assume every field you publish is depended upon by someone.

Follow-ups

  • How do you retire v1 when you cannot see all consumers? — Instrument it: per-schema-version consumption metrics, then a deprecation window with reminders, then a scheduled brownout (stop publishing v1 for an hour) before the real removal. Brownouts find the consumers that ignored the emails.
  • Where does the version live? — In a header or in the registry's schema id; never inferred from the payload shape, and preferably not only in the topic name, because that forces a topic split for every version.
  • Should v1 and v2 share a topic? — Usually yes, with the version in the header and consumers filtering, because a topic split multiplies operational surface. Split only when the shapes have diverged so far that they are different facts.

Common wrong answer

"We coordinate a release with all consumers." That is the definition of a distributed monolith and it does not scale past a handful of teams — and it fails outright when a consumer is replaying old data. Compatibility rules exist precisely so that releases do not need coordinating.

midWhat does a schema registry do, and which compatibility mode do you set?

Answer

A schema registry stores versioned schemas, hands out ids, and refuses registrations that violate the configured compatibility rule — so a producer that tries to publish a breaking change fails in CI rather than in a consumer at 2am. The modes are: BACKWARD (a new schema can read data written by the previous one — the default, and what you want when consumers upgrade first), FORWARD (old schemas can read new data — right when producers upgrade first), FULL (both), and the TRANSITIVE variants which check against every previous version rather than just the last, which matters enormously when consumers replay history. My default is BACKWARD_TRANSITIVE for event topics, because replaying a compacted or long-retention topic means a consumer will meet schemas from a year ago, and non-transitive checking allows a chain of individually compatible changes that is collectively incompatible. Also worth saying: the registry is a runtime dependency for producers and consumers, so it needs its own availability story and a client-side cache.

Example

BACKWARD              new schema reads old data       consumers upgrade first
FORWARD               old schema reads new data       producers upgrade first
FULL                  both                            safest for shared topics
*_TRANSITIVE          checked against ALL versions    required if you replay

Wire format (Confluent): magic byte 0x00 + 4-byte schema id + payload
  -> the consumer fetches schema  once and caches it
  -> a registry outage does not stop cached consumers, but it does stop
     a cold start, so treat it as a tier-1 dependency

Follow-ups

  • Why transitive? — Three changes each compatible with their immediate predecessor can be incompatible with version 1. Any topic you might replay from the beginning needs transitive checking.
  • Do you need a registry with JSON? — You need the discipline either way. JSON Schema is supported by registries too; without one you are relying on documentation and hope, which fails at the third team.
  • Where should compatibility be checked? — In CI, on the producer's pull request, against the registry's current version. Discovering incompatibility at deploy time is late; at runtime is too late.

Common wrong answer

"We use JSON so we do not need schemas." You still have a schema; it is just undocumented and unenforced. The first consumer that depends on a field you thought was internal turns your next refactor into an incident.

seniorAvro, Protobuf or JSON for events — and what does the choice actually cost?

Answer

Avro's strength is schema evolution: the writer's and reader's schemas are resolved at read time with defaults and aliases, so field addition and removal are genuinely first class, and it is the native format in most Kafka ecosystems and in Hadoop-descended tooling. Protobuf's strength is ubiquity and code generation: field numbers make evolution explicit and safe, it is excellent across many languages, and if you already use gRPC you have one IDL for synchronous and asynchronous alike. JSON's strength is that a human can read it in a terminal and any tool can consume it, which is worth more in practice than engineers usually admit — with JSON Schema in a registry you get most of the safety at a real cost in size and CPU. The numbers to have ready: binary formats are typically 3-10x smaller than JSON before compression and noticeably cheaper to parse; after zstd the size gap narrows but the CPU gap does not. My default is Protobuf for polyglot organisations, Avro where the ecosystem is Kafka-and-analytics-heavy, and JSON for low-volume integration and public-facing events where debuggability wins.

  • What is the Protobuf rule people break? — Reusing a retired field number, or changing a field's type in place. Mark removed numbers reserved; the compiler will then protect you.
  • Does CloudEvents help? — Yes, for envelope consistency: id, source, type, time, subject, datacontenttype as a standard header set, with your format inside. It solves the metadata argument once so teams argue only about payloads.
  • What about compression? — zstd at the producer narrows JSON's size penalty substantially; it does not remove the parse cost, which at high volume is often the larger bill.

Common wrong answer

"Binary formats are premature optimisation; use JSON everywhere." At a few hundred messages a second, correct. At a hundred thousand, the difference is real money in network, storage and CPU — and the evolution safety of a typed IDL matters more than the byte count anyway.

seniorWhen is event sourcing worth it, and what does it cost?

Answer

It is worth it when the history is part of the product rather than a byproduct: regulated domains that must show how a state was reached, financial ledgers, anything with audit or dispute requirements, collaborative or temporal domains where "what did it look like on the 3rd" is a real query, and systems where you expect to derive several different views from the same facts. The costs are consistently underestimated. You need schema versioning for events that will be read for years, snapshotting to bound replay, an upcasting strategy for old events, a way to correct a mistake (a compensating event, never an edit), and a plan for GDPR erasure in an append-only store — normally crypto-shredding, where personal data is encrypted per subject and the key is destroyed. You also need the team to think in events, which is a genuine learning curve and shows up as aggregates designed too large. The rule I would state is: event-source the two or three aggregates where history is the requirement, and use ordinary state storage everywhere else — event sourcing a whole system is where teams get into trouble.

Example

Fits                                  Does not fit
  ledgers, positions, balances          CRUD reference data
  regulated audit trails                a CMS
  order/claim/case lifecycles           a cache
  collaborative editing                 anything where nobody asks "why"

Machinery you must build or buy:
  append-only store with per-stream optimistic concurrency (expected version)
  snapshots + snapshot invalidation
  upcasters for old event versions
  projections + rebuild tooling (and the ability to rebuild in production)
  correction events, never edits
  crypto-shredding for erasure

Follow-ups

  • How do you fix a bad event? — Append a correction; never edit or delete, because projections and downstream consumers have already seen it. If the event was catastrophically wrong you may rewrite the stream as a documented, exceptional data-repair operation.
  • How do you handle GDPR erasure? — Crypto-shredding: encrypt personal fields with a per-subject key held outside the log, then delete the key. The events remain, the personal data is unrecoverable.
  • Is Kafka an event store? — It is a log, not a store: no per-stream optimistic concurrency, no efficient read-one-stream, and retention is a policy rather than a promise. Use a purpose-built store (EventStoreDB, Marten on Postgres, a DynamoDB design) and publish to Kafka for distribution.

Common wrong answer

"Event sourcing gives you a free audit log." The audit log is real, and it is not free: you have bought versioning, upcasting, snapshotting, replay and erasure problems. Teams that adopt it for the audit trail alone usually discover an append-only table with a JSON diff would have sufficed.

seniorHow do you rebuild a read model without downtime?

Answer

Build the new version alongside the old and switch readers when it is ready — a blue-green projection. Create the new projection under a new name or version, run a rebuild consumer from the beginning of the source (a topic reset, or the event store's stream), let it catch up to live while the old projection continues serving, verify by comparing samples and aggregate counts between old and new, then flip the read path atomically behind a version pointer or a feature flag, and keep the old one for a rollback window before dropping it. The details that make this work in practice: the projection must be idempotent so a restart mid-rebuild is harmless, the rebuild consumer should be in its own consumer group so it cannot disturb live lag, and rebuild throughput needs its own capacity plan — rebuilding a year of events at production consumer speed can take longer than the retention window, which is when you discover you needed a snapshot. Say the number: if a rebuild takes more than a few hours, it is a design constraint and should be tested regularly rather than discovered during an incident.

Example

1. create projection v2 (new table / index / store)
2. consumer group "proj-v2-rebuild" from offset earliest -> writes v2 only
3. monitor: rebuild lag, rows written, error rate
4. when lag ~0: verify  (row counts, checksums, N sampled entities vs v1)
5. flip reads: config/feature flag points at v2   <- the only risky moment
6. keep v1 for the rollback window (a day), then drop

Rebuild rate matters: 2 billion events at 50k/s = ~11 hours.
Test it quarterly, or the first real rebuild will be during an incident.

Follow-ups

  • What if the source no longer has the history? — Then you cannot rebuild, and the projection is your system of record whether you meant it to be or not. That is the moment to add retention, tiered storage, or an archival copy in object storage.
  • How do you verify equivalence? — Aggregate counts and sums first (cheap, catches gross errors), then a sampled entity-by-entity comparison, then a shadow-read comparison in production where both are queried and differences logged.
  • Can you rebuild in place? — Only if you can afford the read model being wrong while it rebuilds. For anything user-facing, build beside and flip.

Common wrong answer

"Truncate the table and replay." That is a self-inflicted outage for the duration of the replay, with no rollback if the new projection logic is wrong. Interviewers ask this specifically to see whether you reach for blue-green instinctively.

seniorA new team wants to consume an existing stream and needs the last two years of history. What do you do?

Answer

Separate the two problems: bootstrapping the history, and joining the live stream without a gap or a duplicate window. If the topic still holds two years (likely only with tiered storage or compaction), the simple path is a new consumer group starting at the earliest offset, with rate limiting so the backfill does not saturate the cluster or the new team's downstream. If the topic does not hold it, you bootstrap from the system of record — a snapshot export to object storage or a database dump — and then start consuming from an offset that corresponds to the snapshot's point in time, using the source's own watermark (a commit LSN, a snapshot timestamp) rather than guessing; Debezium's snapshot-then-stream mode is exactly this pattern packaged. Either way, insist that the new consumer is idempotent, because the safe cutover overlaps the snapshot and the stream. And treat this as a governance moment, not just an ops task: a new consumer means a new dependency on your schema, so it is when you agree the contract, the compatibility policy, and who is called when the stream stops.

Example

Topic still has the history (tiered storage):
  new group, auto.offset.reset=earliest, throttled consumer
  watch: object-storage read cost, broker network, their downstream DB

Topic does not (7-day retention):
  1. export snapshot from the source DB at LSN L  -> object storage
  2. load the snapshot into the new consumer's store
  3. start consuming from the offset corresponding to L (overlap on purpose)
  4. idempotent apply absorbs the overlap
Never: "we will start from now and backfill later" - the gap is permanent.

Follow-ups

  • How do you find the offset matching the snapshot? — Use time-based offset lookup as an approximation, or better, have the producer stamp the source LSN/version in the event so you can seek deterministically.
  • How do you keep the backfill from hurting everyone? — Kafka client quotas per principal, a separate consumer group, and off-peak scheduling. A backfill reading a year of tiered data is also a cost event worth mentioning to whoever owns the bill.
  • What if the events are not sufficient to build their view? — Then the events are notifications, not state transfer, and the right conversation is whether to enrich the events or give them a query API. Do not let them scrape your database.

Common wrong answer

"Reset the existing consumer group to earliest." That replays history to the current consumers, re-firing side effects and probably causing an incident. New consumer, new group, always — and it is the mistake people actually make under time pressure.

principalYou own the event platform for forty teams. What governance do you put in place?

Answer

Enough to prevent the failures that cross team boundaries, and no more — governance that slows a team down without preventing a shared failure will be routed around. My minimum set: every topic has a named owning team and a documented purpose in a catalogue that is generated from configuration, not maintained by hand; schemas live in a registry with transitive compatibility enforced in CI, so a breaking change fails a pull request; a naming and envelope standard (CloudEvents-style metadata, correlation and causation ids, schema version, producing service) so tooling works everywhere; a documented tiering — which topics are public contracts and which are a team's internal business — enforced with ACLs rather than convention; and per-tenant quotas so no single producer can take the cluster down. Then the operational half: a platform SLO for the bus itself, a self-service onboarding path that takes minutes, and a deprecation process with usage metrics so retirement is evidence-based. What I would emphasise at principal level is that the paved road has to be the fastest route: teams follow governance when the compliant path is easier than the non-compliant one, and enforcement without ergonomics produces shadow infrastructure.

Example

Enforced automatically (no human in the loop):
  schema compatibility (BACKWARD_TRANSITIVE) in CI
  topic naming + required envelope headers, validated at publish
  ACLs: producers write their own topics; public topics are readable
  quotas per principal (produce/fetch byte rate)
  every topic has owner + on-call + purpose in the catalogue

Provided so the rules are cheap to follow:
  one-command topic creation with sane defaults (RF=3, min.insync=2)
  a client library that sets the envelope, tracing and retries correctly
  a dashboard per topic: rate, lag by consumer, schema versions in use

Reviewed by humans only for: new public contracts, and deprecations.

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle a team that breaks the rules? — You mostly do not have to, because CI does. Where a human decision is needed, the lever is the catalogue and the on-call rota: a topic with no owner gets no support and is scheduled for deletion.
  • Central platform team or federated ownership? — Federated ownership of topics and schemas, central ownership of the cluster, the tooling and the standards. Centralising schema design becomes a bottleneck within a quarter.
  • How do you know the governance is working? — Measure it: number of breaking-change incidents, mean time to onboard a new consumer, percentage of topics with an owner and a schema, number of topics on a deprecated version. Governance without metrics is folklore.

Common wrong answer

"An architecture review board approves every new topic." It becomes a queue, teams route around it, and the review adds little because the failures are mechanical — compatibility, ownership, quotas — and mechanical failures should be caught by machines. Reserve human review for genuinely public contracts.

11Time, clocks and causality

Clock questions are asked less often than the units above, but they are a favourite of interviewers who want to find the ceiling of a strong candidate, and the answers are short enough to prepare properly. The theme throughout is that wall-clock time is a measurement with error bars, not a fact, and that most "impossible" bugs in distributed systems are a clock assumption someone did not know they had made.

midWhy can you not trust wall-clock time across machines?

Answer

Because each machine's clock is an independent, drifting oscillator being corrected by NTP over a network with variable delay. Typical NTP synchronisation is within a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds on a well-run network, and much worse when a machine has just booted, when it is a virtual machine that was paused or migrated, or when an NTP source is misconfigured — skews of seconds and even hours occur in the wild. Worse, corrections can move the clock backwards, so a timestamp read twice can go down, which breaks any code that computes a duration or assumes monotonicity. The practical consequences interviewers probe: last-write-wins conflict resolution can silently pick the wrong write, TTL and lease expiry computed on wall clocks can fire early or late, "created after" comparisons between machines are unreliable, and log correlation across services can show an effect before its cause. The rule is to use wall clocks for display and for coarse-grained business timestamps, and never as the basis of a correctness decision.

Example

// wrong: duration from a clock that can jump or go backwards
var start = DateTime.UtcNow;
await DoWork();
var elapsed = DateTime.UtcNow - start;      // can be negative. Yes, really.

// right: monotonic source for durations
var sw = Stopwatch.StartNew();
await DoWork();
var elapsed2 = sw.Elapsed;                  // never goes backwards

// wall clock is fine for: "occurredAt" on an event, shown to a human
// wall clock is NOT fine for: ordering, lease expiry, LWW conflict resolution

Follow-ups

  • What is the difference between a monotonic and a wall clock? — Monotonic measures elapsed time from an arbitrary origin and never jumps (Stopwatch, CLOCK_MONOTONIC, System.nanoTime); wall clock names an instant and is adjusted. Use monotonic for durations, wall clock for instants.
  • How do leap seconds show up? — Historically as duplicated or skipped seconds causing hangs and crashes; the modern mitigation is smearing (spreading the adjustment over hours), which major cloud NTP services do — and which means their clocks deliberately disagree with UTC for a day.
  • What should you monitor? — NTP offset and stratum per host, with an alert well below the skew your logic tolerates. Most teams do not monitor it and discover the skew through a data bug.

Common wrong answer

"We use NTP, so clocks are synchronised." NTP reduces skew; it does not eliminate it, it can fail silently, and it can step the clock backwards. Any design that needs "synchronised" rather than "approximately synchronised with a known bound" is broken.

midExplain Lamport clocks and vector clocks.

Answer

A Lamport clock is a single counter per process: increment on every local event, and on receiving a message set the counter to max(local, received) + 1. It guarantees that if A happened-before B then L(A) < L(B) — but not the converse, so a smaller counter does not prove causality and concurrent events are indistinguishable from ordered ones. A vector clock keeps a counter per process, so comparing two vectors tells you exactly which relationship holds: A before B if every element of A is less than or equal to B's and at least one is strictly less; concurrent if neither dominates. That precision is what lets Dynamo-style stores detect genuine conflicts instead of silently overwriting, and it is why they can hand siblings back to the application. The cost is metadata proportional to the number of writers, which is why production systems bound it — version vectors keyed by replica rather than by client, dotted version vectors, or pruning with a timestamp.

Example

Vector clocks, processes A B C

A: [1,0,0] write x=1
A -> B message
B: [1,1,0] write x=2        depends on A's write
C: [0,0,1] write x=3        independent

compare [1,1,0] and [0,0,1]:
  neither dominates -> CONCURRENT -> a genuine conflict
  last-write-wins would silently discard one. Vector clocks let you know.

Follow-ups

  • Where would you actually use a Lamport clock? — Any time you need a cheap total order consistent with causality: ordering operations in a CRDT, tie-breaking with (lamport, nodeId), sequencing in a single-writer log.
  • Why are vector clocks rare in application code? — Metadata growth and the need to expose sibling values to the user. Most systems choose LWW plus a monotonic version per entity and accept the loss.
  • What replaced them in practice? — Hybrid logical clocks for systems that want causality and human-meaningful timestamps in one 64-bit value; CRDTs where the merge is defined so conflicts do not need detecting.

Common wrong answer

"Lamport clocks give you the order of events." They give an order consistent with causality, which is a much weaker statement: two unrelated events get different Lamport values that say nothing about which really happened first. Only vector clocks can tell you "concurrent".

seniorWhat are TrueTime and hybrid logical clocks, and what do they buy?

Answer

TrueTime is Google's clock API for Spanner: instead of a timestamp it returns an interval [earliest, latest] with a bounded uncertainty, backed by GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacentre, typically a few milliseconds wide. Spanner uses it to get external consistency by waiting out the uncertainty — a transaction commits at timestamp T and then waits until TrueTime says T is definitely in the past, so any transaction that starts later gets a strictly larger timestamp. That is a remarkable trade: it converts a hard ordering problem into a small, bounded latency cost, and it requires hardware most organisations do not have (though AWS and others now offer bounded-error clocks in a similar spirit). Hybrid logical clocks are the software answer: a timestamp that combines physical time with a logical counter, so it stays close to wall clock (useful and human-readable) while guaranteeing monotonicity and capturing causality — used by CockroachDB, MongoDB and YugabyteDB. If you are asked to design ordering across regions without atomic clocks, HLC is the answer to name.

Example

TrueTime:  TT.now() -> [earliest, latest], uncertainty e (a few ms)
  commit at T = latest; then WAIT e before releasing locks
  -> any later transaction observes a strictly greater timestamp
  -> external consistency, at the cost of ~2e latency per commit

HLC:  timestamp = (physical_ms, logical_counter)
  send:    l = max(l, now); c++
  receive: l = max(l_local, l_msg, now); c = ... ; ensures monotonic + causal
  -> ~wall-clock readable, never goes backwards, captures happened-before

Follow-ups

  • What happens to Spanner when clock uncertainty grows? — Commit wait grows with it, so write latency rises; if a node's uncertainty exceeds a threshold it removes itself rather than risk correctness. Degrading latency rather than consistency is the deliberate choice.
  • Can you use an HLC in application code? — Yes, and it is a good answer for ordering events across services: store (physical, counter) per entity and use it for last-writer-wins that respects causality.
  • Is a database's commit timestamp enough? — Within one database, usually yes. Across databases it is meaningless, which is exactly the gap HLCs fill.

Common wrong answer

"Spanner has atomic clocks so it does not have clock problems." It has the same problems and chose to bound and pay for the uncertainty rather than ignore it. The interesting part of the answer is commit wait, not the hardware.

seniorEvent time versus processing time: why does the distinction matter?

Answer

Event time is when something happened at the source; processing time is when your system saw it. They diverge for entirely ordinary reasons — a mobile client was offline for two hours, a consumer was lagging, a partition was being rebalanced, an upstream batch job ran late — and the divergence is unbounded in the general case. Computing on processing time is easy and gives non-reproducible answers: replaying the same data produces different windows, so yesterday's report changes when you rerun it. Computing on event time gives deterministic, reproducible results but forces you to decide when a window is complete, which is what watermarks are for: a watermark asserts that no event with a timestamp earlier than W is expected any more, and everything later than that is late data with an explicit policy (drop, or update the result and emit a retraction). The trade to state is latency against completeness: a conservative watermark waits longer and is more nearly correct, an aggressive one emits sooner and handles more late data.

Example

Event at 09:59:58 (event time), arrives at 10:04:12 (processing time)

Processing-time window 10:00-10:05  -> counted in the WRONG hour,
                                        and differently on every replay.
Event-time window 09:55-10:00       -> counted correctly, IF the window
                                        is still open when it arrives.

watermark = max event time seen - allowed lateness (say 5 minutes)
  window closes when the watermark passes its end
  later arrivals: drop (and count them) or update + retract downstream

Follow-ups

  • How do you choose allowed lateness? — From the measured distribution of (processing time − event time): pick a high percentile, and monitor the events you drop as a first-class metric. If the drop count is not on a dashboard, you do not know your answers are wrong.
  • What if a client's clock is wrong? — Event time from an untrusted device must be sanity-checked and clamped, and it is usually worth recording both the client's claim and the server's ingest time so you can reprocess if you later learn the clock was off.
  • What is the Kafka mechanism? — Records carry a timestamp that is either CreateTime (the producer's) or LogAppendTime (the broker's), configured per topic. That choice is exactly the event-time/processing-time decision, made at the topic level.

Common wrong answer

"We use the ingestion timestamp; it is close enough." Close enough until a consumer lags or a mobile client syncs after a flight, and then your hourly aggregates are wrong in a way nobody can reproduce. Any pipeline whose output must be re-derivable has to use event time.

seniorHow would you order events from two services without a shared clock?

Answer

You cannot order genuinely concurrent events, so the first move is to establish what ordering is actually needed. If the events are causally related, capture the causality explicitly: propagate a causation id and a per-entity version, so the consumer can order by version rather than by time. If they touch the same entity, route them through one partition or one writer, which makes the ordering an artefact of the mechanism rather than of the clock. If you need an approximate global order for analysis rather than for correctness, a hybrid logical clock gives you monotonic, causality-respecting timestamps that are also close to wall clock, and ties break deterministically on node id. If nothing else is available, sorting by wall clock within a tolerance window is acceptable only for reporting, and you should say so out loud — never for a correctness decision. The framing that impresses is that ordering should be a property of your design, not something you attempt to recover after the fact from timestamps.

Example

{
  "eventId":    "01JB0V0Z7Q...",
  "correlationId": "01JAZZ...",         // the whole business flow
  "causationId":   "01JB0U...",         // the event/command that caused this
  "entityId":   "order-42",
  "entityVersion": 7,                    // ORDER BY this, not by time
  "occurredAt": "2026-09-08T09:14:02.117Z",
  "hlc":        "1757322842117:3"        // physical:logical, monotonic
}

Follow-ups

  • What do correlation and causation ids give you? — Correlation groups everything in one business flow for tracing; causation records the direct parent, so you can reconstruct the actual tree of cause and effect rather than guessing from timestamps.
  • Is a database sequence a global clock? — Within that database, yes, and it is often the cheapest sequencer available. Across databases it is meaningless, and gaps from rolled-back transactions mean it is not gapless either.
  • What breaks if two services both claim to be first? — Nothing, if they are genuinely concurrent — the honest answer is that no order exists. Forcing one invents information, and the invented order will eventually be wrong in a way that matters.

Common wrong answer

"Sort by timestamp and use the service name as a tiebreaker." It produces a deterministic order that is not a causal order, so an effect can sort before its cause. Deterministic and correct are different properties, and only one of them is what the requirement asked for.

seniorTell me about a clock-related failure and how you would design against it.

Answer

The canonical shape: a node's clock jumps forward (a bad NTP source, or a VM resumed after suspension), and everything time-derived on that node misbehaves at once — cache entries expire immediately, leases are considered expired so a second leader appears, tokens are rejected as expired, TTL-based dedup windows close early so duplicates get through, and any last-write-wins field written by that node wins forever because its timestamps are in the future. The reverse jump is quieter and worse: entries never expire, and LWW writes from that node are permanently ignored, so a subset of users' updates silently disappear. Designing against it means: never use wall clocks for durations (monotonic sources only), never use wall clocks as the sole basis for mutual exclusion (fencing tokens), clamp and sanity-check any externally supplied timestamp, prefer logical versions to timestamps for conflict resolution, and monitor NTP offset per host with an alert threshold tighter than any tolerance your logic assumes. The tell of a candidate who has lived through one is that they check clock skew early in an investigation of an "impossible" bug.

Example

Symptom checklist when something is impossible:
  * an effect logged before its cause  -> clock skew between hosts
  * tokens "expired" on one node only  -> that node's clock is ahead
  * cache never expires on one node    -> that node's clock is behind
  * one replica always wins LWW        -> its clock is in the future
  * duplicate leader with no restart   -> pause or clock jump, not a crash

Design rules:
  durations      -> monotonic clock only
  mutual exclusion-> lease + FENCING TOKEN, never time alone
  conflict resolution -> logical version, or HLC, not wall clock
  external timestamps -> clamp to [now - d, now + d] and record both

Follow-ups

  • How do you test for this? — Inject it: step a node's clock forward and backward in a test environment and watch what breaks. It is one of the highest-yield fault injections and almost nobody runs it.
  • What is the cheapest broad mitigation? — Stop using wall clocks for decisions. Most of the failure modes above disappear once durations are monotonic and expiry is enforced by a store that has its own clock.
  • What about containers? — They share the host clock, so a host with bad time affects every pod on it — which produces the confusing pattern where a failure follows a node rather than a service.

Common wrong answer

"That cannot happen, we run in a managed cloud." Managed clocks are good and still not perfect: VM suspension, live migration, misconfigured NTP in a private subnet and leap-second smearing all occur. Interviewers asking this want to see the diagnostic instinct, not a claim of immunity.

Docs: TrueTime and external consistency · Hybrid logical clocks (paper) · Kafka Streams — time semantics

12Stream processing, state and CDC

Stream processing turns up whenever the role touches analytics, real-time features or data integration, and it is where messaging meets state management. Interviewers test whether you know that a stateful operator is a distributed database you are now operating: it has a changelog, a restore time, a rebalance cost and a failure mode. CDC is the adjacent topic and the one most likely to produce a war story — replication slots, snapshot storms and schema changes are the classic three.

midWhat changes when a stream processor becomes stateful?

Answer

A stateless processor (map, filter, enrich from a call) can be restarted anywhere at any time; its only state is the offset. A stateful one — aggregation, join, deduplication, windowing, session tracking — keeps local state per key, usually in an embedded store such as RocksDB, and that changes everything operationally. The state must be made durable, which is done by writing every change to a compacted changelog topic, so recovery means replaying that changelog into a fresh local store; the time that takes is your real recovery time objective, and it can be minutes to hours for a large store. It also makes partition assignment expensive, because a partition that moves takes its state with it — which is why sticky assignment and standby replicas exist. So the answer to give is: statefulness converts a stateless, freely-schedulable consumer into something with data gravity, and you must then design for restore time, for rebalance cost, and for state size growth.

Example

// stateless: restart anywhere, no recovery cost
stream.filter((k, v) -> v.amount() > 100).to("large-orders");

// stateful: local RocksDB store + compacted changelog topic
stream.groupByKey()
      .windowedBy(TimeWindows.ofSizeWithNoGrace(Duration.ofMinutes(5)))
      .count(Materialized.as("counts-store"))   // changelog: app-counts-store-changelog
      .toStream().to("counts");

// operational consequences
num.standby.replicas=1        // warm copy elsewhere -> failover in seconds
// otherwise: failover = replay the whole changelog into a new RocksDB

Follow-ups

  • What are standby replicas for? — Keeping a warm copy of the state on another instance so a failover does not require a full restore. They cost extra storage and network and are usually worth it for anything user-facing.
  • How do you bound state growth? — Retention on windows, TTL on session state, and periodic pruning of keys that will never be seen again. Unbounded state is the most common cause of a stream job that worked for six months and then stopped.
  • What makes restore slow? — The changelog's size, which is a function of update rate rather than key count, and RocksDB write amplification. Compaction lag on the changelog topic makes it dramatically worse, so monitor it.

Common wrong answer

"State is in memory, so it is fast and we just rebuild on restart." Rebuild time is the thing you were asked about, and "just rebuild" for a 200 GB store is a multi-hour outage. If you cannot quote your restore time, you do not know your recovery time objective.

midExplain the window types and when each is right.

Answer

Tumbling windows are fixed, non-overlapping intervals — every event belongs to exactly one, which makes them the right choice for periodic reporting ("orders per five minutes"). Hopping (sliding, in some vocabularies) windows are fixed-size and overlap by a hop, so each event appears in several windows; they smooth a moving average at the cost of multiplying state and output. Session windows group events separated by less than a gap timeout, so their size is data-driven; they are the right model for user activity, where "a session" means "until they stopped for 30 minutes". Global windows have no natural end and rely on a custom trigger, which you need for cumulative counters. The dimension people miss is the interaction with lateness and state: a hopping window with a one-second hop over a one-hour window keeps 3,600 open windows per key, which is a state-size decision disguised as an analytics one.

Example

tumbling(5m)          |-----|-----|-----|      each event in exactly 1
hopping(1h, hop 5m)   |--------|                each event in 12 windows
                          |--------|            12x the state and output
                              |--------|
session(gap 30m)      |--|      |-------|       data-driven boundaries

Choose by the question:
  "per hour"                 -> tumbling
  "moving average"           -> hopping (watch the state multiplier)
  "user visit"               -> session
  "total ever, emit on N"    -> global + custom trigger

Follow-ups

  • What is a grace period? — How long after a window's end you still accept late events into it; in Kafka Streams it is explicit (ofSizeAndGrace) because the older implicit 24-hour default silently kept enormous amounts of state.
  • Do session windows merge? — Yes: a late event landing between two sessions merges them, which means emitted results have to be retractable downstream. That is the hidden complexity of sessions.
  • Where does the state actually live? — A window store keyed by (key, window start), so state size is keys × open windows × value size — worth computing before you ship, not after.

Common wrong answer

"Use a sliding window for everything, it is more accurate." It multiplies state and output by the overlap factor, and for most reporting questions the answer wanted is a tumbling window. Accuracy is not the axis; the question being asked is.

seniorHow do watermarks work, and what do you do with late data?

Answer

A watermark is an assertion flowing through the pipeline that says "no event with an event time earlier than W is expected any more", which is what lets an event-time window decide it is complete and emit. It is usually derived heuristically — maximum event time seen minus an allowed-lateness margin, computed per partition and then taken as the minimum across partitions, which is why one idle or lagging partition holds the whole watermark back and stalls output (the classic "no results are being emitted" incident). Once the watermark passes a window's end plus its grace period, the window closes; events arriving after that are late, and you need an explicit policy: drop them and count the drops, route them to a side output for reprocessing, or update the result and emit a retraction or an updated aggregate downstream. The trade to state is completeness against latency, and it is a product decision as much as a technical one — a dashboard can tolerate a corrected number, a billing run usually cannot.

Example

watermark = min over partitions( max event time in partition ) - allowedLateness

Failure that looks like a bug:
  partition 3 has no traffic -> its max event time never advances
  -> global watermark frozen -> NO windows close -> zero output
  Fix: idle-partition detection / idleness timeout so an idle partition
       stops holding the watermark back. Flink and Kafka Streams both
       provide this and it is off or conservative by default.

Late data policies:
  drop + count      simple; the counter MUST be alarmed
  side output       reprocess in batch later ("lambda"-style repair)
  update + retract  correct results, harder consumers

Follow-ups

  • How do you pick allowed lateness? — From the observed distribution of arrival delay, at a percentile you can defend, and revisit it after any change to upstream buffering. It is a measured number, not a default.
  • What is a retraction? — An emitted correction that tells downstream "the previous result for this window is superseded". It requires downstream consumers designed to accept updates, which is why many pipelines choose drop-and-count instead.
  • Where does the trade appear in the product? — Directly: "the 09:00 number may change until 09:10" is a statement the business either accepts or does not, and if it does not, you must delay publication rather than pretend.

Common wrong answer

"Late data is rare so we ignore it." Rare in aggregate and concentrated in exactly the cases that matter — a mobile client reconnecting, an upstream retry after an outage, a backfill. Ignoring it without counting it means you cannot tell a healthy pipeline from one silently discarding a percent of its input.

seniorKafka Streams, Flink, or a plain consumer — how do you choose?

Answer

A plain consumer is right for stateless or lightly stateful work where the state fits in a database you already run: enrichment, routing, writing to a sink, simple per-key upserts. It has no extra framework, no extra cluster, and the failure modes are ones your team already understands. Kafka Streams is right when you want stateful processing embedded in your own service: it is a library, not a cluster, so it deploys like an ordinary application, and it gives you windows, joins, state stores, exactly-once within Kafka and (since 4.2, KIP-1071) a GA broker-side rebalance protocol for Streams groups plus dead-letter support in exception handlers (KIP-1034). Flink is right when you need a genuine processing platform: complex event-time semantics, large state with incremental checkpointing, batch and stream unified, connectors to many systems, and SQL over streams — at the price of operating a cluster and a job-management story. My heuristic is: plain consumer until you need windows or joins, Kafka Streams if the workload is Kafka-to-Kafka and fits in your service, Flink when the state or the semantics outgrow that.

DeploymentYour serviceYour service (a library)A cluster with job management

Event timeDo it yourselfYesBest in class

Follow-ups

  • When is Kafka Connect the answer instead? — When the job is purely moving data between Kafka and another system with light transformation. Writing a bespoke consumer to do what a well-maintained connector does is a common waste.
  • What about ksqlDB or Flink SQL? — Excellent for analyst-facing and rapid iteration; the caution is operational — a SQL job is still a stateful distributed application, and it needs the same lifecycle discipline as code.
  • How do you migrate off a framework later? — Assume you will: keep business logic in plain functions the framework calls, so the framework is an adapter rather than the architecture.

Common wrong answer

"Use Flink, it is the most powerful." Power you do not need is operational cost you do pay: a cluster to run, checkpoint storage to size, savepoints to manage on every upgrade, and an expertise dependency. Choose the least machinery that meets the semantics.

seniorExplain stream-table duality and how a stream-table join works.

Answer

A stream is a sequence of changes and a table is the current state; each can be derived from the other — fold a stream of changes and you get a table, observe a table's changes and you get a stream. That duality is why a compacted Kafka topic can be read either as a changelog or as a materialised table (a KTable), and it is the mental model behind CDC, event sourcing and materialised views alike. A stream-table join enriches each event with the current value of the table for that key: it is asymmetric (only the stream side triggers output), it requires co-partitioning — both sides keyed the same way with the same partition count, or the join finds nothing — and it is inherently temporal, because the table's value at join time may not be the value that was current when the event occurred. A stream-stream join is different again: it needs a window on both sides and keeps state for both, so its state cost is proportional to the window size times the rate. The co-partitioning requirement is the practical trap and the thing to say unprompted.

Example

// table from a compacted topic: current state per key
KTable<String, Customer> customers = builder.table("customers");   // compacted

// stream-table join: only orders trigger output
orders.join(customers, (order, customer) -> enrich(order, customer))
      .to("orders-enriched");
// REQUIRES: both keyed by customerId, SAME partition count.
// Otherwise: silent, partial results - the join simply misses.

// stream-stream join: windowed, state on both sides
clicks.join(impressions, ..., JoinWindows.ofTimeDifferenceWithNoGrace(
        Duration.ofMinutes(10)));   // state ~ rate x 10 minutes x 2

Follow-ups

  • What is a GlobalKTable for? — Small reference data replicated in full to every instance, so no co-partitioning is needed and you can join on a non-key field. The cost is that every instance holds the whole table.
  • What happens if the table update arrives after the event? — You enrich with the old value. If that is unacceptable you need a versioned table or a temporal join keyed by event time, which Flink and newer Kafka Streams versioned stores support.
  • How do you fix a co-partitioning mismatch? — Repartition one side through an intermediate topic with the right key and partition count; Streams will insert it for you when you selectKey, and the extra topic is a real cost worth knowing about.

Common wrong answer

"Join the stream to a database lookup." It works and it is often right for low volume, but it couples throughput to database latency, generates a query per event, and gives non-reproducible results on replay because the database has moved on. Say why you chose it if you do.

seniorWhat goes wrong with CDC in production?

Answer

Four things, in roughly the order they will hurt you. Replication slots: a stopped or lagging connector prevents WAL recycling and the primary's disk fills, taking the database down — this is the incident, and the mitigations are alerting on slot lag plus max_slot_wal_keep_size so the slot is dropped rather than the database. Snapshots: a connector restarting with the wrong configuration can trigger a full re-snapshot of a huge table, saturating the source and republishing everything downstream; incremental snapshots (signal-table driven) exist precisely to make this controllable, and Debezium 3.6.2 (2026-09-01) extended them to run against PostgreSQL read-only replicas so the primary is spared. Schema changes: a DDL statement flows through as a schema-change event and can break consumers or the connector itself, so schema history topics and compatibility rules matter as much as they do for application events. And semantics: CDC emits row-level changes, including those from a bulk update or a data-fix script, so a single UPDATE touching two million rows becomes two million events — which your consumers were not sized for.

Example

-- the alarm that prevents the outage
SELECT slot_name, active,
       pg_size_pretty(pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), confirmed_flush_lsn))
         AS retained
FROM pg_replication_slots;
-- alert when retained > a few GB, or when active = false for minutes

-- the safety valve (PostgreSQL 13+): drop the slot, not the database
ALTER SYSTEM SET max_slot_wal_keep_size = '64GB';

Follow-ups

  • Should CDC events be published to other teams? — No: they are your storage schema. Terminate CDC in a service you own and publish domain events from there — otherwise every ALTER TABLE is a cross-team breaking change.
  • How do you handle deletes? — CDC emits a delete event and, for compacted topics, a tombstone. Soft deletes in the source turn into ordinary updates, which consumers must be told about explicitly or they will keep serving deleted rows.
  • What is the current Debezium line? — 3.6 (July 2026) with 3.6.2 the latest patch (2026-09-01), which also fixed a gradual throughput loss in long-running connectors and masked passwords in Debezium Server logs — both worth knowing if you run it.

Common wrong answer

"CDC is transparent; you just point it at the database." It is a replication client with its own state, its own failure modes and a direct line to your primary's disk usage. Teams that adopt it without an owner discover all four failure modes in their first year.

seniorHow do you deploy a change to a stateful stream processor safely?

Answer

Classify the change first: a change that does not alter state layout or semantics is an ordinary rolling deploy, aided by standby replicas and static membership so state does not move. A change that alters the state schema, the windowing, the key, or the meaning of an aggregate is a new application: give it a new application id (which means a new changelog and a new consumer group), run it from the beginning or from a savepoint alongside the old one, verify its output against the old one, and switch consumers over — the same blue-green pattern as a read-model rebuild. In Flink the equivalent is a savepoint plus state migration, and the discipline is to take a savepoint before every upgrade and to test restoring from it. What to say unprompted: never reuse an application id across an incompatible state change, because the framework will attempt to restore old state into new code and the failure mode ranges from a startup crash (good) to silently wrong aggregates (bad).

Example

Compatible change (filter logic, output format, added metric):
  rolling deploy, num.standby.replicas>=1, static group.instance.id
  expected impact: brief per-partition pause, no restore

Incompatible change (new key, changed window, changed aggregate type):
  application.id = orders-enrich-v3     <- NEW id, new changelog, new group
  run from earliest, catch up, compare outputs, flip consumers, retire v2
  cost: a full re-derivation - budget it, and test the runtime quarterly

Follow-ups

  • What signals a bad restore? — Startup exceptions about serde or store format, or — worse — silently different aggregate values. Compare a sample of outputs between old and new before switching; do not trust "it started".
  • How do you shorten the re-derivation? — Bootstrap the new version's state from a snapshot of the old one where the schemas allow it, or keep the source compacted so replay reads current state per key rather than the full history.
  • What about Flink savepoints? — Take one before every upgrade, keep them, and test restore in a lower environment. A savepoint you have never restored from is a backup you have never tested.

Common wrong answer

"Just redeploy; it will rebuild state from the changelog." It restores the old state into new code, which is exactly the incompatible case. And even when it works, a full restore during a deploy is an availability event you did not plan for.

13Operating, observability and incident command

For a lead or principal role this unit often carries as much weight as the architecture questions, because it is where interviewers find out whether you have carried a pager. The questions are scenario-shaped and the grading is on sequence and judgment: what you look at first, what you decide without complete information, and how you communicate. Have real numbers ready — a cluster size, a lag figure, a recovery time — because specificity is what makes these answers credible.

midWhat do you monitor on a messaging system?

Answer

Split it into three layers. The pipeline layer is what matters to the business: consumer lag expressed in time rather than records, end-to-end freshness from event timestamp to visible effect, throughput in and out, and error and dead-letter rates. The broker layer is capacity and health: under-replicated and offline partitions, ISR shrink and expand rates, request latency by type, disk usage and growth rate, network throughput, controller state and, for RabbitMQ, memory and disk alarms plus per-queue depth and unacked counts. The client layer is where most incidents actually originate: rebalance frequency, commit latency, poll interval, handler duration and connection errors. Then the alerting discipline: page on symptoms that affect users (freshness breach, growing lag, DLQ non-zero, offline partitions), and leave the rest as dashboard signals for diagnosis. The number worth quoting is that lag in records is nearly meaningless — a million cheap records is fine, ten thousand expensive ones is an hour — so publish estimated seconds behind and alert on that.

Example

Page on:
  freshness p99 > SLO for 10 minutes        (users are affected)
  consumer lag time growing for 15 minutes  (trend, not a spike)
  offline / under-replicated partitions > 0 (durability at risk)
  DLQ depth > 0 with an owner                (data is stuck)
  broker disk > 75% or growing to full in < 6h

Dashboard only (diagnosis, not paging):
  rebalance rate, commit latency, ISR shrink/expand, request queue time,
  per-partition lag, handler duration histogram, produce/consume rates

Follow-ups

  • How do you compute lag in seconds? — Compare the timestamp of the last consumed record with the timestamp of the log end, or the wall clock minus the event time of the record just processed. Both are approximations; both are far more useful than a record count.
  • What is the most under-monitored signal? — Rebalance rate, closely followed by DLQ depth. Both are quiet until they explain an incident.
  • How do you avoid alert fatigue? — Alert on burn rate against an SLO rather than on instantaneous thresholds, and require an owner and a runbook link for every page. An alert nobody can act on gets muted, and then so does its neighbour.

Common wrong answer

"We alert when lag exceeds 10,000 messages." It fires on every deploy and every traffic spike, and it stays silent when ten records of a slow handler represent thirty minutes of delay. Alert on time behind, and on the trend rather than the instant.

midHow do you trace a request across an asynchronous boundary?

Answer

Propagate the trace context in the message headers, not in the payload, using the W3C traceparent and tracestate headers so any instrumented consumer can continue the trace. The semantic subtlety is the span relationship: a synchronous call is a child span, but a message that may be consumed much later, or by many consumers, is better modelled as a producer span plus a consumer span linked to it — OpenTelemetry's messaging conventions define exactly this, with links rather than parent-child where the consumer's lifetime is decoupled. Alongside the trace context, carry a correlation id for the whole business flow and a causation id for the immediate parent event, because those are what you use in log queries and in incident forensics when the trace has been sampled away. And beware sampling: a trace sampled out at the producer leaves the consumer's work invisible, so use head-based sampling with a consistent decision propagated in tracestate, or tail sampling if you can afford it.

Example

headers on every message:
  traceparent:    00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01
  tracestate:     vendor=...
  correlation-id: 01JAZZ...        the business flow
  causation-id:   01JB0U...        the immediate parent event
  schema-version: 3

span model (OpenTelemetry messaging semconv):
  producer span  "orders publish"   (kind=producer)
  consumer span  "orders process"   (kind=consumer, LINK to producer span)
  batch consume  -> one span with N links, not N nested spans

Follow-ups

  • Why links rather than a parent-child span? — Because the consumer may run hours later or many times; a parent-child relationship implies a nested lifetime that does not exist, and it makes trace durations meaningless.
  • What if a message is replayed? — Start a new trace and link to the original, so the replay is visible as a distinct activity rather than corrupting the original trace's timings.
  • What do you do when tracing is not enough? — Structured logs with the correlation id on every line, and an index on it. In practice most asynchronous forensics is log-based, with the trace giving you the shape and the logs the detail.

Common wrong answer

"Put the trace id in the message body." It couples tracing to your schema, breaks when a payload is transformed, and means intermediaries such as brokers, proxies and connectors cannot see or propagate it. Headers exist for exactly this.

seniorHow do you plan capacity for a Kafka cluster?

Answer

Start from the data: peak ingress in MB/s, average message size, replication factor, retention, and the consumer fan-out. Storage is ingress × retention × replication factor, plus headroom of at least 30-40% because a full disk takes a broker down and recovery needs space; tiered storage changes this equation by moving cold segments out, so you size local disk for the hot window instead. Network is the usually-binding constraint: a broker serves replication traffic plus every consumer group, so egress is roughly ingress × (replication factor − 1 + number of consumer groups), and cross-AZ traffic is both a bandwidth and a billing consideration. Partitions are sized for consumer parallelism and per-partition throughput, then doubled for headroom, remembering that partition count also drives memory, file handles and rebalance time. Then verify by testing rather than arithmetic: run a load test at production message size and shape, and specifically test the degraded case — one broker down, a rebalance in progress — because a cluster sized for the healthy state is not sized at all.

Example

Worked example
  peak ingress            120 MB/s
  replication factor      3
  retention               7 days
  consumer groups         5

  disk  = 120 MB/s x 86400 x 7 x 3 = ~218 TB, +40% headroom = ~305 TB
          (with tiered storage: size local for 1 day = ~31 TB + headroom)
  egress per broker ~ ingress x (RF-1 + groups) = 120 x 7 = 840 MB/s cluster
  partitions: target 120 MB/s / ~20 MB/s realistic per partition = 6,
              but consumer parallelism needs 24 -> take 24, headroom -> 48

Then load-test with ONE BROKER DOWN. That is the number that matters.

Follow-ups

  • What usually binds first? — Network and disk IO, not CPU, unless you are doing broker-side decompression because producers and consumers disagree on compression settings — a surprisingly common and expensive misconfiguration.
  • How much headroom for a broker failure? — Enough that N−1 brokers carry peak load, which for a small cluster is substantial. This is why very small clusters are inefficient: three brokers means 50% headroom to survive one.
  • How does cross-AZ cost enter? — Replication and consumer fetches crossing AZs are charged; rack-aware replica placement plus follower fetching (consumers reading from the nearest replica, KIP-392) can cut that bill substantially.

Common wrong answer

"We will autoscale it." Brokers are stateful: adding one requires partition reassignment and data movement measured in hours, which is not a response to a spike. Kafka capacity is planned and pre-provisioned; only the consumers autoscale.

seniorDesign disaster recovery for a messaging platform. What are your RPO and RTO?

Answer

Start by naming the disaster: a broker loss is handled by replication and is not a disaster; an availability-zone loss should be survived automatically with rack-aware placement across three zones; a region loss is the actual DR case. For a region, the options are active-passive with asynchronous replication (MirrorMaker 2 or a vendor equivalent), giving an RPO of the replication lag — seconds normally, minutes in a bad hour — and an RTO set by how quickly consumers can be repointed and how you handle offset translation, which is approximate across clusters. Active-active is possible with prefixed topic names and careful loop prevention, and it converts the DR problem into a data-conflict problem you must solve in the application. What separates a real plan from a diagram is testing: a documented failover procedure that has never been executed will fail, so the answer should include a schedule for exercising it and an honest statement of the numbers — "RPO 30 seconds, RTO 20 minutes, tested quarterly" is a plan; "we replicate to another region" is not.

Example

Failure domain     Mechanism                         RPO        RTO
broker             replication (RF=3, min.isr=2)     0          seconds
availability zone  rack-aware placement, 3 AZs       0          seconds
region             MirrorMaker 2, async              ~seconds   minutes
                   (consumers repointed, offsets translated approximately)
total data loss    object-storage archive of topics  hours      hours

Non-negotiables:
  consumers idempotent (offset translation is approximate)
  failover runbook exercised quarterly, with a timer running
  the decision to fail over has a named owner and a threshold

Follow-ups

  • Why is offset translation approximate? — Offsets are per-cluster and the mirrored topic's offsets differ from the source's; MirrorMaker maintains a mapping, but at the boundary you can replay or skip a small window — hence the idempotency requirement.
  • What about the schema registry and connectors? — They are part of the plan or the plan is fiction: a failed-over cluster with no registry cannot deserialise anything. Replicate the registry and rehearse it.
  • Who decides to fail over? — A named role with a written threshold, because the worst outcome is an hour spent debating while the region stays down. The decision should be reversible or explicitly one-way, and everyone should know which.

Common wrong answer

"We have a standby cluster in another region." Without stated RPO and RTO, without offset and schema handling, and without a rehearsal, that is a cost centre rather than a recovery plan. Interviewers ask for the numbers precisely because the numbers are what forces the design.

seniorA consumer has processed 200,000 messages incorrectly. How do you repair it?

Answer

Stop first, then decide the repair shape. Pause the consumer group so the damage stops growing and the backlog stays durable in the broker. Then establish the blast radius precisely: which offsets or message ids were affected, which downstream effects were produced, and whether those effects are correctable in place or have propagated further (emails sent, payments taken, events published to other teams). Then choose between three repairs: replay with fixed code (possible if the source still has the data and the handler is idempotent, and preferable because it uses the normal path), a targeted correction job (better when only a subset is affected or when the effects are external), or a full downstream rebuild (right when the projection is derived and the source is intact). Whichever you choose, the repair must be dry-runnable, batched, restartable and audited — and if the bad output was published to other teams, you owe them a correction event and a notification, not a silent fix. Finally, close the loop: the detection gap is usually the bigger failure, so the postmortem action is the invariant check that would have caught it in minutes.

Example

1  pause: kafka-consumer-groups --group billing --reset-offsets ... --dry-run
   (or scale the deployment to zero - fastest, no arguments about flags)
2  scope: SELECT ... WHERE processed_at BETWEEN t0 AND t1  -> exact id list
3  choose:
     replay   - source intact + idempotent handler + effects internal
     correct  - subset affected, or effects are external (refunds, emails)
     rebuild  - derived projection, source intact
4  execute in batches with a dry run, an audit table and a kill switch
5  notify downstream consumers with a correction event if you published
6  add the invariant check that would have caught this in 10 minutes

Follow-ups

  • How do you replay only part of a topic? — Reset the group's offsets to a timestamp or an offset range, or run a separate repair consumer that filters by id; do not reset the live group unless the whole range needs redoing.
  • What if the handler is not idempotent? — Then replay is unsafe and you need the correction job. This is the moment the earlier idempotency work pays for itself, and it is worth saying so.
  • What do you tell downstream teams? — Immediately, with the window, the affected ids and what you will publish. Consumers who learn about a data error from their own users lose trust in the platform, which is expensive for years.

Common wrong answer

"Reset the offsets to earliest and reprocess everything." It reprocesses far more than necessary, re-fires every side effect, and can take longer than the incident itself. Scope the repair before you run it.

seniorWhat belongs in a runbook for a messaging system?

Answer

A runbook is written for someone at 3am who did not build the system, so it is procedures, not prose. For each alert: what it means in one sentence, the two or three most likely causes, the exact commands to diagnose them (copy-pasteable, with the cluster names filled in), the mitigations in order of preference with their side effects, and the escalation path with names. Beyond alerts, the runbook needs the standing procedures — pause and resume a consumer group, reset offsets safely, drain and replay a dead-letter queue, add or remove a broker, fail over a region, rotate credentials — each of which should be a script or a documented command sequence rather than a description. Two qualities separate useful runbooks from decorative ones: they are tested, ideally by having someone who did not write them execute them during a game day, and they are versioned next to the code so a change to the system that invalidates a procedure is caught in review. And every alert must link to its runbook entry, or the runbook will not be found when it is needed.

Example

ALERT: billing-consumer-lag-time > 10m

WHAT   The billing consumer is more than 10 minutes behind. Invoices are
       delayed; no data is lost. Customer impact starts at ~30 minutes.
CHECK  1) kafka-consumer-groups --describe --group billing   (lag by partition)
       2) grafana: billing handler p99, downstream DB p99
       3) grafana: rebalance rate for group billing
LIKELY a) downstream DB slow  b) rebalance loop  c) hot partition  d) traffic up
DO     a) check DB; if it is the cause, pause consumers and page the DB owner
       b) rebalance loop -> reduce max.poll.records to 100, redeploy
       c) hot partition -> see runbook "whale tenant isolation"
       d) traffic up -> scale consumers to at most the partition count (24)
DO NOT reset offsets. Ever. On this consumer it double-invoices.
ESCALATE 20 min without improvement -> @payments-oncall, then @platform-oncall

Follow-ups

  • How do you keep runbooks current? — Store them in the repository, link them from the alert, and require a runbook update in the pull request that changes the relevant behaviour. Wiki runbooks rot within two quarters.
  • What is the highest-value entry? — The "do not do this" list. Most incidents are made worse by a well-intentioned action, and offset resets are the standard example.
  • How do you test them? — Game days, and by having the newest team member run the procedure while someone watches. If they cannot, it is not a runbook.

Common wrong answer

"We have dashboards, the team knows what to do." That is a bus-factor argument disguised as a process, and it fails precisely when the person who knows is asleep or has left. Interviewers hearing this ask who was on call last time and what they did.

principalYou are incident commander. The broker cluster is degraded, publishers are failing, and two teams are proposing different fixes. What do you do?

Answer

Take command explicitly — say "I am incident commander" — because ambiguous ownership is what turns a 20-minute incident into a two-hour one. Then establish the three things in order: current customer impact in plain language, the mitigation that reduces impact fastest regardless of root cause, and a single decision-maker per workstream. With two competing fixes, I do not adjudicate on technical merit in the channel; I ask each proposer for the expected effect, the blast radius if wrong, and the reversibility, then choose the reversible one first and time-box it — five or ten minutes, with a stated success signal. Meanwhile someone is assigned to communications on a fixed cadence, someone to keeping the timeline, and the rest are explicitly told to stop making changes, because uncoordinated simultaneous changes are how you lose the ability to attribute cause. If a decision requires accepting data loss — unclean leader election, skipping a backlog, failing over with a non-zero RPO — that decision is mine to make and to state out loud, with the cost named, rather than allowing it to happen by default while people debate.

Example

First five minutes, in order:
  "I am IC. @a is comms. @b is scribe. Everyone else: no changes without me."
  impact: "publishers failing, ~40% of order events rejected since 09:12"
  fastest mitigation regardless of cause: shed non-critical publishers,
    raise producer retries/buffer so the backlog sits client-side, not lost
  two proposals -> ask each: expected effect? blast radius? reversible?
  pick the reversible one, time-box 10 minutes, state the success signal

Decisions only the IC makes:
  enabling unclean leader election (accepts data loss)
  failing over a region (accepts RPO)
  skipping a backlog (accepts a reconciliation debt)
Each is stated in the channel with its cost, not slipped in as a config change.

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle a senior person overriding you? — Restate the role, ask them to take command formally if they want it, and record the handover. Two commanders is worse than either one alone.
  • When do you stop mitigating and start diagnosing? — When impact is contained. Diagnosis before mitigation is the most common failure in incident command, and it is what the exercise is really testing.
  • What does a good postmortem focus on? — Detection and response time, and the systemic reasons the failure was possible — not the individual who made the change. Blameless in wording and in action, or people stop reporting.

Common wrong answer

Diving into the technical argument yourself. As IC your value is coordination, decision-making and communication; the moment you are deep in a terminal you have stopped being IC and nobody has noticed. Delegate the debugging and keep the room.

14Cloud and managed messaging choices

Most teams in 2026 are not running their own broker, so interviewers increasingly ask you to choose between managed services and to defend the choice on cost and operations rather than on features. The failure mode they are looking for is a candidate who knows Kafka deeply and cannot say when SQS is the better answer. Keep the comparisons concrete — limits, quotas and pricing shapes — because vague preference is indistinguishable from unfamiliarity.

midSQS, SNS, EventBridge or Kinesis — which and why?

Answer

SQS is a queue: at-least-once delivery, effectively unlimited throughput on standard queues, a visibility timeout instead of an ack, 256 KB messages, up to 14 days retention, and FIFO queues when you need ordering per message group and five-minute deduplication. SNS is fan-out: push to many subscribers (SQS queues, Lambda, HTTP, email), with filter policies so subscribers receive only what they care about; the canonical pattern is SNS to several SQS queues, which gives fan-out plus per-consumer buffering and retry. EventBridge is a routing bus with content-based rules, schema discovery, third-party SaaS sources and archive-and-replay — it is the right choice for event-driven integration where routing logic should live in infrastructure rather than in code, at higher per-event cost and latency. Kinesis Data Streams is the log: ordered shards, multiple independent consumers with their own positions, replay within retention, and throughput provisioned per shard (1 MB/s in, 2 MB/s out) or on-demand. The decision rule is the same as everywhere: work queue means SQS, broadcast means SNS or EventBridge, replayable ordered log means Kinesis or MSK.

Example

NeedServiceWatch out for
Work queue, elastic workersSQS standardNo ordering; duplicates are normal
Ordering per entitySQS FIFOThroughput per group; head-of-line blocking
Fan-out to N consumersSNS → N SQSEach queue needs its own DLQ and alarms
Content-based routing, SaaS eventsEventBridgePer-event cost, latency, rule quota
Ordered replayable logKinesis / MSKShard limits; resharding is an operation

Follow-ups

  • What is the visibility timeout gotcha? — If your handler takes longer than the timeout, the message becomes visible again and a second consumer processes it — duplicate work with no error. Extend the visibility heartbeat for long handlers, or shorten the handler.
  • Kinesis or MSK? — Kinesis for simplicity and AWS-native integration; MSK when you want the Kafka ecosystem (Connect, Streams, the client libraries) and are prepared to think about brokers even though they are managed.
  • Does SQS FIFO give exactly-once? — Deduplication within a five-minute window, which is not the same thing. Application-level idempotency is still required.

Common wrong answer

"SQS is basically Kafka without the setup." SQS has no replay, no independent consumer positions, no retention as a feature and no ordering on standard queues. Treating them as interchangeable leads to designs that cannot onboard a second consumer without re-engineering the producer.

midAzure Service Bus, Event Hubs or Event Grid?

Answer

Service Bus is the enterprise broker: queues and topics with subscriptions and SQL-like filters, sessions for ordering and per-session state, scheduled messages, dead-letter queues as a first-class concept, transactions across entities within a namespace, and duplicate detection over a configurable window. Event Hubs is the log: partitioned, high-throughput ingestion with consumer groups and checkpointing, retention measured in days with Capture to blob storage, and a Kafka-protocol endpoint so Kafka clients can connect directly, which makes it a common landing spot for teams migrating. Event Grid is the reactive router: push-based delivery of discrete events to handlers with filtering and retry, designed for reacting to state changes across Azure and your own applications. The decision mirrors AWS: commands and workflow messages go to Service Bus, telemetry and stream ingestion go to Event Hubs, and lightweight reactive notification goes to Event Grid — and the common mistake is using Event Hubs as a work queue, because it has no per-message acknowledgement or retry.

Example

Service Bus   queue/topic + subscriptions, SQL filters, sessions,
              scheduled messages, DLQ, dedup window, transactions
              -> orders, commands, workflow steps, anything needing retry

Event Hubs    partitions + consumer groups + checkpoints, Kafka endpoint,
              Capture to blob, retention in days
              -> telemetry, clickstream, IoT ingestion, replayable log

Event Grid    push delivery + filters + retry to handlers
              -> "blob created", "resource changed", reactive glue

Follow-ups

  • Where do sessions bite? — One consumer per session at a time, so throughput is bounded by concurrent sessions; and a session lock must be renewed, so a slow handler loses the session and the work is redone.
  • Is the Event Hubs Kafka endpoint a drop-in? — For basic produce and consume, largely; not for the whole Kafka surface (transactions, some admin operations, compaction behaviour). Verify the specific features your code uses before assuming a lift-and-shift.
  • What is Service Bus premium for? — Dedicated resources, predictable latency, larger messages (100 MB versus 256 KB on standard) and geo-disaster-recovery pairing. It is also where most production workloads should be.

Common wrong answer

"Event Hubs is Azure's queue." It is Azure's log. Using it for work distribution means writing your own per-message retry, dead-lettering and delivery counting on top of checkpointed offsets, which is exactly the machinery Service Bus already provides.

seniorWhat goes wrong with serverless consumers?

Answer

Three families of problem. Concurrency: a Lambda triggered by SQS scales out with the queue, which is wonderful until it opens two thousand connections to a database sized for fifty — so you cap reserved concurrency, use a proxy or pooling layer, and remember that the cap causes throttling that looks like a queue backup. Batching and partial failure: an event-source mapping delivers a batch, and without partial batch responses a single bad record fails the whole batch and redelivers all of it, multiplying duplicate work; reporting the failed item ids is the fix and it is off by default in older configurations. Ordering and iterators for log sources: with Kinesis or Kafka event sources the function is invoked per shard or partition, so a slow or repeatedly failing invocation blocks that shard — you need a bisect-on-error strategy, a maximum record age, and an on-failure destination, or one poison record halts a partition indefinitely. Add to that cold starts inflating tail latency and the general difficulty of long-running work in a function, and the guidance is: serverless consumers are excellent for spiky, short, independent work and poor for long, stateful or connection-heavy processing.

Example

{
  "EventSourceArn": "...:queue/orders",
  "BatchSize": 10,
  "MaximumBatchingWindowInSeconds": 5,
  "FunctionResponseTypes": ["ReportBatchItemFailures"],
  "ScalingConfig": { "MaximumConcurrency": 50 },
  "DestinationConfig": { "OnFailure": { "Destination": "...:queue/orders-dlq" } }
}
// Kafka/Kinesis sources additionally:
//   "BisectBatchOnFunctionError": true,
//   "MaximumRecordAgeInSeconds": 3600,
//   "MaximumRetryAttempts": 5
// Without these, one poison record can stall a shard for its full retention.

Follow-ups

  • Why does the database melt before the queue drains? — Because the queue's elasticity is not the database's. Concurrency limits are the throttle, and the queue is the buffer; that is the correct division of labour.
  • How do you handle a handler that needs two minutes? — Extend the visibility timeout beyond the function timeout, or move the work to a container-based consumer. Functions with long timeouts and heavy state are a smell.
  • What about cost? — Per-invocation pricing is superb for spiky low volume and can be far more expensive than a running container at sustained high volume. Model both at your actual duty cycle.

Common wrong answer

"Serverless scales automatically, so we do not need capacity planning." You moved the capacity problem to whatever the function calls. The bottleneck becomes the database, the third-party API or a connection limit, and the failure arrives faster because scaling is instant.

seniorWhat actually drives the cost of a messaging platform?

Answer

For self-managed brokers it is compute and storage but overwhelmingly network, and specifically cross-availability-zone traffic: replication multiplies every byte by the replication factor, and every consumer group multiplies it again, so a topic with three replicas and five consumer groups can move seven times its ingress across zones — which is charged in both directions in most clouds. That is why rack-aware placement and fetch-from-closest-replica matter financially and not just for latency, and why the object-storage-native Kafka alternatives that emerged over the last few years pitch on cost rather than throughput. For managed services the shape differs: SQS and EventBridge charge per request or per event, so a chatty design with tiny messages is expensive and batching is a direct saving; Kinesis charges per shard-hour plus payload units, so over-provisioned shards cost money even when idle; Event Hubs charges throughput units. The senior move is to model the cost per business event end to end — including retries, dead letters, replays and cross-zone hops — because the design that looks cheapest per message is often not.

Example

Self-managed Kafka, 100 MB/s ingress, RF=3, 5 consumer groups, 3 AZs:
  replication egress ~ 2 x ingress crossing AZs
  consumer egress    ~ 5 x ingress (less with closest-replica fetching)
  total cross-AZ     ~ 700 MB/s -> at typical per-GB rates this is usually
                       the largest single line item, above instances.

Levers, in order of effect:
  compression (zstd)              often 5-10x on JSON: cuts everything
  fetch from closest replica      removes most consumer cross-AZ traffic
  tiered storage                  cuts local disk and rebalance traffic
  fewer, fatter messages          batching cuts per-request charges
  right-sizing consumer groups    each group is a full copy of the egress

Follow-ups

  • Where do teams waste the most? — Uncompressed JSON, a consumer group per microservice instance rather than per service, and retention set to "forever" because nobody chose a number.
  • Is a replay expensive? — With tiered storage, yes: reading a year of history is an object-storage request and transfer bill. Worth knowing before someone resets to earliest during an incident.
  • How do you attribute cost to teams? — Per-topic and per-consumer-group metrics mapped to owners in the catalogue. Cost that nobody owns never goes down.

Common wrong answer

"Storage is cheap so retention does not matter." Storage is cheap; replicated storage across three zones plus the network to fill it is not, and long retention also lengthens rebalances and broker replacement. Retention is a design decision with a bill attached.

seniorHow do you think about lock-in when choosing a broker?

Answer

Lock-in is not binary, it is a cost of exit that you should estimate rather than fear. The parts that lock you in are the semantics your code depends on — visibility timeouts versus offsets, sessions versus partition keys, native dead-lettering versus retry topics — plus the operational surface: IAM integration, monitoring, connectors and everything built around the service. Abstracting behind a thin interface helps at the margin and is worth doing for the publish and consume calls, but it cannot abstract semantics: a codebase written against at-least-once with per-message retry will not move to a log without redesign, and vice versa. My position in an interview is to choose the best fit for the problem and the team, write down the exit cost honestly, and spend the abstraction effort where it is cheap — keeping business logic free of broker types, keeping the envelope standard (CloudEvents), and keeping the serialisation format portable. The genuinely portable choice, if portability is a hard requirement, is the Kafka protocol, because several vendors implement it; but portability as a first-order requirement usually costs more than the migration it is insuring against.

Example

// worth abstracting: the transport call and the envelope
public interface IEventPublisher {
    Task PublishAsync<T>(string subject, T payload, EventMetadata meta, CancellationToken ct);
}

// NOT abstractable: these are semantics, not APIs
//   offsets vs visibility timeouts
//   per-partition ordering vs session ordering vs none
//   native DLQ vs retry topics
//   replay vs destructive consumption
// If your design depends on one of these, changing broker is a redesign.

Follow-ups

  • What is the cheapest insurance? — A portable serialisation format and a standard envelope, so the data survives even if the transport does not. The messages outlive the broker.
  • Does the Kafka protocol really give portability? — Substantially: MSK, Confluent, Redpanda, WarpStream, AutoMQ and Event Hubs all speak it, though feature coverage varies. It is the closest thing to a portable option in this space.
  • When is lock-in clearly acceptable? — When the managed service removes work you would otherwise do badly. A five-person team choosing SQS is buying capacity to work on their product; that is a good trade even with a stated exit cost.

Common wrong answer

"Abstract everything so we can switch any time." Abstraction over incompatible semantics produces a lowest-common-denominator API that gives up the features you paid for, plus a layer nobody understands. Abstract the call, document the exit cost, and move on.

seniorSelf-managed Kafka or a managed offering — how do you decide, and what has changed recently?

Answer

Decide on the operational load you can genuinely carry: broker upgrades, KRaft controller quorum, partition reassignment, disk pressure, client version drift and 3am recovery. If you do not have at least one person whose job includes that, use a managed offering — MSK, Confluent Cloud, Azure Event Hubs' Kafka endpoint, or one of the object-storage-native services. What has changed recently is worth naming: Kafka 4.x removed ZooKeeper, which halved the operational surface and made self-managing meaningfully easier; tiered storage decoupled storage from brokers, so clusters are smaller and rebalances faster; and a class of Kafka-protocol services built directly on object storage (WarpStream, AutoMQ, Confluent's Freight-style tiers) trade a little latency for much lower cross-AZ cost and near-instant scaling, which is genuinely attractive for high-volume, latency-tolerant workloads. The framing I would give: self-manage when you need control of a big cluster's cost and configuration and have the people; use managed when the cluster is a means rather than an end — and re-evaluate, because the middle ground got much better in the last two years.

Example

Decision inputs, roughly in order of weight:
  1. do we have a named owner for the cluster's on-call? (if no -> managed)
  2. throughput and cross-AZ cost at our volume
  3. latency requirement (object-storage-backed services add 100s of ms)
  4. feature needs (Connect, Streams, EOS, tiered storage, share groups)
  5. compliance / data residency
  6. exit cost (Kafka protocol keeps this comparatively low)

Recent shifts that change the answer:
  4.0 removed ZooKeeper       -> self-managing is simpler than it was
  tiered storage GA (3.9)     -> smaller brokers, faster rebalances
  object-storage-native tiers -> cheaper at high volume, higher latency

Follow-ups

  • What does managed not remove? — Client-side expertise: consumer group tuning, rebalance behaviour, idempotency, schema management and partition design remain yours. Most Kafka incidents are client-side.
  • When is the latency of object-storage-backed Kafka a problem? — Anything user-interactive in the request path. It is fine for pipelines, analytics and asynchronous work, which is most Kafka traffic.
  • How do you evaluate honestly? — Model twelve months of total cost including engineer time and a realistic number of incidents, not just the invoice. Self-managed clusters usually lose that comparison at small scale and win it at very large scale.

Common wrong answer

"Managed Kafka is just Kafka; there is no difference." There is: version availability, configuration limits, which APIs are exposed, and how support incidents are handled. Teams discover the differences during an upgrade or an incident, which is the expensive time to discover them.

15Security in messaging

Security questions are rare in a distributed-systems loop and near-certain in a regulated domain or a platform role, and they are usually shallow-but-broad: can you name the authentication mechanism, do you know where data is unencrypted, and have you thought about personal data in an append-only log. The one that separates senior candidates is GDPR erasure in an immutable stream, because the naive answer is impossible and the real answer — crypto-shredding — has to be designed in from the start.

midHow do you authenticate and authorise clients to a broker?

Answer

In Kafka, authentication is SASL or mTLS: SASL/SCRAM for username-and-password with salted hashing, SASL/GSSAPI for Kerberos in enterprise estates, SASL/OAUTHBEARER for token-based identity — which is where the ecosystem is heading, and Kafka 4.3's KIP-1258 added OAuth client_credentials with client assertions so producers no longer need a long-lived client_secret in configuration — and mTLS where the certificate is the identity. Authorisation is ACLs on resources (topic, group, cluster, transactional id) per principal, or a vendor RBAC layer; the principle to state is least privilege per service, with produce rights only on the topics a service owns and read rights only on what it consumes. RabbitMQ works similarly with users, virtual hosts and per-vhost permissions expressed as configure/write/read regular expressions, plus x509 or OAuth 2 authentication backends. The operational half matters as much: credentials must be rotatable without downtime, which means overlapping validity and a client that re-authenticates, and every service should have its own principal so an audit trail exists.

Example

Kafka: least privilege for one service
  kafka-acls --add --allow-principal User:svc-billing \
             --operation Write --topic billing-events
  kafka-acls --add --allow-principal User:svc-billing \
             --operation Read --topic orders --group billing
  (no cluster-level rights, no wildcard topics, no shared principal)

RabbitMQ: per-vhost regex permissions
  set_permissions -p /prod svc-billing "^billing\." "^billing\." "^orders$"
                                        configure     write        read

Follow-ups

  • Why is a shared principal bad beyond least privilege? — It destroys attribution: you cannot tell which service produced a bad message, cannot apply per-tenant quotas, and cannot revoke one consumer without breaking all of them.
  • How do you rotate credentials with no downtime? — Overlapping validity: issue the new credential, deploy it, verify, then revoke the old. With OAuth this is automatic; with static secrets it needs a documented procedure.
  • Where do quotas fit? — Kafka client quotas are per principal, so identity is also your noisy-neighbour control. Another reason not to share principals.

Common wrong answer

"It is on a private network, so we do not need authentication." Network isolation is one control, not the control: it gives no attribution, no least privilege, no quota enforcement, and it fails completely the first time something inside the perimeter is compromised or misconfigured.

midWhere is message data unencrypted, and when do you need end-to-end encryption?

Answer

TLS protects data in transit between client and broker, and disk encryption protects the bytes at rest, but between those two the broker sees plaintext — in memory, in the page cache, in logs if someone dumps a message, and in every operational tool that inspects a topic. That is fine for most data and unacceptable for some: payment details, health records, credentials, or anything where the platform team must be outside the trust boundary. End-to-end encryption means the producer encrypts the payload (or specific fields) with a key the broker never has, and only authorised consumers can decrypt; field-level encryption is usually the right granularity because it keeps routing keys, headers and non-sensitive fields visible so the platform still works. The costs to name are real: key distribution and rotation become your problem, encrypted fields cannot be filtered, joined or indexed by intermediate processing, and debugging gets harder because nobody can read the message. So apply it to the fields that need it, not to everything.

Example

{
  "orderId": "42",
  "customerId": "c-9",
  "amount": 1999,
  "currency": "GBP",
  "pii": {
    "alg": "AES-256-GCM",
    "keyId": "kms:eu-west-2:...:key/9f2c",
    "iv": "base64...",
    "ciphertext": "base64..."          // name, email, address
  }
}
// routing, aggregation and joins still work on the clear fields;
// only holders of keyId can read the personal data.

Follow-ups

  • Who manages the keys? — A KMS with per-purpose or per-subject keys, audited access, and automated rotation. Keys in configuration files are the failure mode this design exists to avoid.
  • How does rotation work for data already written? — Envelope encryption: a per-message data key encrypted by a master key, so rotating the master does not require rewriting messages. Rotating the data key does, which is why per-subject keys matter for erasure.
  • Does this break stream processing? — On the encrypted fields, yes. Design the schema so processing only needs clear fields, or decrypt inside a processor that is inside the trust boundary.

Common wrong answer

"TLS plus encryption at rest is end-to-end encryption." It is encryption of two hops with a plaintext gap in the middle. If your threat model includes the broker operator, a compromised broker, or an over-broad topic read permission, those controls do not address it.

seniorA customer invokes their right to erasure. Their data is in a seven-year event log. What do you do?

Answer

You cannot delete a record from an append-only log without rewriting it, so the answer designed in advance is crypto-shredding: personal data is encrypted with a key held per data subject in a key store, the events carry only ciphertext, and erasure means destroying that subject's key — the events remain, and the personal data becomes permanently unreadable, which regulators have generally accepted as equivalent to erasure. Alongside that you delete the plaintext copies elsewhere: projections, read models, caches, search indexes, analytics warehouses, backups where feasible, and anything you exported to a third party — and you need a data map to know where those are, which is the part teams underestimate. If crypto-shredding was not designed in, the fallback options are all expensive: rewriting the topic through a filtering process into a new topic and cutting consumers over, tombstoning on a compacted topic where the key structure allows it, or accepting that the log holds pseudonymised identifiers and the personal data lives only in a mutable store you can delete from — which is the design many teams retrofit to. State the deadline too: one month under GDPR, extendable to three in complex cases, so this must be an automated process rather than a project.

Example

Designed in (works):
  event { customerId: "c-9", pii: enc(dataKey_c9, {...}) }
  keystore: dataKey_c9 -> wrapped by KMS master
  erasure  = delete dataKey_c9 + purge projections/caches/indexes/exports
             + record the erasure in an audit log (which itself holds no PII)

Retrofitted (all expensive):
  rewrite topic -> new topic with PII stripped, migrate every consumer
  compaction tombstones -> only if keyed by the subject, and only for the
                            latest value, not the history
  keep PII out of events entirely -> id in the event, data in a mutable store

Follow-ups

  • What about backups? — Generally accepted to be handled on the backup's normal expiry cycle, provided the data is not restored into active use and the position is documented. Get that position agreed with legal in advance rather than during a request.
  • What if a downstream team exported the data? — Your data map must include exports and your contract must oblige them to erase. This is the strongest practical argument for not publishing personal data on shared topics at all.
  • Is pseudonymisation enough? — Only if the identifier cannot be linked back to the person with the data you hold; in practice a customer id that maps to a name in your own database is personal data.

Common wrong answer

"Kafka has compaction, so we send a tombstone." Compaction removes the latest value for a key; the historical events remain until segments are cleaned, and only if the topic is keyed by the subject at all. It is a partial mechanism presented as a complete answer, and it will not survive a data-protection review.

seniorHow do you isolate tenants on a shared messaging platform?

Answer

Isolation has four dimensions and you should be explicit about which ones you provide. Data isolation: separate topics or queues per tenant with ACLs, or a shared topic with the tenant id in the key and enforcement in the consumer — the latter is cheaper and one bug away from a cross-tenant leak, so shared topics are only acceptable when the consumers are yours and the boundary is auditable. Performance isolation: per-tenant quotas on produce and fetch rate so one tenant cannot starve others, plus per-tenant lag monitoring so you can see a whale forming before it becomes an incident. Failure isolation: a poison message or a stuck consumer for one tenant must not block the others, which usually means per-tenant groups or share-group semantics rather than a single ordered partition. And operational isolation: the ability to pause, replay or purge one tenant without touching the rest, which is a design requirement that shows up as a topic-per-tenant or a partition-per-tenant decision. The trade is topic explosion — thousands of topics or queues cost metadata, file handles and rebalance time — so the usual middle ground is shared topics with per-tenant keys, dedicated topics for the largest tenants, and quotas everywhere.

Example

Tiering by tenant size (the pattern that survives growth):
  small tenants   shared topic, key = tenantId#entityId, shared consumers
  large tenants   dedicated topic + dedicated consumer deployment
  all tenants     client quotas per principal, per-tenant lag dashboards

Kafka quota example:
  kafka-configs --alter --add-config \
    'producer_byte_rate=10485760,consumer_byte_rate=20971520' \
    --entity-type users --entity-name svc-tenant-acme

Follow-ups

  • How many topics is too many? — With KRaft the metadata ceiling is high (millions of partitions), but rebalance time, client memory and operational comprehension bite far earlier. Thousands of topics is manageable; a topic per customer at scale is not.
  • What is the compliance angle? — Some contracts require physical separation, which forces dedicated topics or clusters. Find that out before designing, because it is not retrofittable cheaply.
  • How do you purge one tenant? — Trivial with a dedicated topic; on a shared topic it means compaction tombstones per key or crypto-shredding. Another reason the largest and most regulated tenants get their own.

Common wrong answer

"We filter by tenant id in the consumer." That is application-level isolation only: any consumer with topic read access sees every tenant's data, one filter bug is a cross-tenant breach, and there is no performance or failure isolation at all. State it as a deliberate, audited trade-off or do not do it.

seniorWhat are the security risks in message deserialisation and consumption?

Answer

The message is untrusted input, and consumers routinely treat it as trusted because it came from "inside". The headline risk is unsafe deserialisation: any format that can instantiate arbitrary types from the payload — Java native serialisation, .NET BinaryFormatter (removed from .NET 9 onward for exactly this reason), TypeNameHandling.All in Json.NET, unrestricted YAML loaders — turns a message into remote code execution. Use a schema-bound format (Avro, Protobuf, JSON with a schema) that maps to known types, and never let the payload choose the type. Beyond that: validate and bound everything (sizes, array lengths, numeric ranges) because a decompression bomb or a two-million-element array is a denial of service; treat identifiers in the message as claims to be checked, not as authorisation; avoid logging full payloads, which is how personal data ends up in a log aggregator with wider access than the topic; and remember that a message can be replayed by anyone who can produce to the topic, so authorisation must be re-evaluated at consumption time rather than assumed from the producer's identity.

Example

// dangerous: the payload decides the type
var obj = JsonConvert.DeserializeObject<object>(body,
    new JsonSerializerSettings { TypeNameHandling = TypeNameHandling.All });

// safe: schema-bound, known type, bounded
var opts = new JsonSerializerOptions { MaxDepth = 16 };
var evt = JsonSerializer.Deserialize<OrderPlaced>(body, opts)
          ?? throw new InvalidMessage("null payload");
if (evt.Lines.Count > 1000) throw new InvalidMessage("too many lines");
// and: authorise the ACTION now, do not trust evt.UserId as permission

Follow-ups

  • Does a schema registry protect you? — It constrains the shape, which removes the type-confusion class; it does not validate business ranges or stop a valid-but-hostile message.
  • How do you stop a decompression bomb? — Limit the decompressed size explicitly and reject anything beyond it; broker-level message size limits do not bound the decompressed result.
  • What is the audit consideration? — Being able to prove which principal produced a given message. That needs per-service principals and producer identity in the headers, and it is what makes an investigation possible at all.

Common wrong answer

"Only our services can publish, so the payload is trusted." Trust boundaries do not follow network boundaries: a compromised service, a bug in another team's producer, or a replayed message all deliver hostile input from a legitimate principal. Validate at the consumer regardless of who produced it.

16Judgment calls

These have no single right answer, and that is the point: the interviewer is grading the shape of your reasoning, the trade-offs you name without being prompted, and whether you can hold a position while genuinely engaging with the counter-argument. At principal level a confident wrong answer scores better than a hedged non-answer, and a confident answer with its own caveats scores best. Each block below says what a strong answer demonstrates rather than dictating the conclusion.

principalThe team wants to move from synchronous HTTP to events across the board. Do you approve?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you treat "event-driven" as a per-interaction decision, not a house style. The strong answer asks what problem is being solved — coupling, availability, throughput, or fashion — and then approves the parts where asynchrony genuinely helps (long-running work, fan-out, load levelling, failure isolation) while declining the parts where the caller needs the answer to continue. It names the costs the proposal is not accounting for: eventual consistency that product teams will discover as bugs, error reporting with no caller to report to, debugging without a stack trace, an operational surface with its own on-call, and the fact that a mediocre event design is far worse than a mediocre HTTP design because the coupling becomes invisible. It also proposes a way to find out cheaply: convert two or three interactions that clearly benefit, measure, and let the results decide the rest.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Availability versus consistency — you buy decoupled uptime and pay in eventual consistency the UI must express honestly.
  • Debuggability — a trace across a broker is worse than a stack trace; budget for tracing, correlation ids and dashboards up front.
  • Team capability — idempotency, ordering and replay are skills; a team without them will ship at-least-once bugs for a year.
  • Reversibility — a per-interaction migration is reversible; a big-bang rewrite is not, which is the main reason to stage it.

Weak answer

Either "yes, events are more scalable" or "no, it adds complexity". Both skip the work: the interviewer wants the interaction-by-interaction analysis and an explicit statement of what would change your mind.

principalWould you ever accept losing messages?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you can price loss rather than treat it as taboo. The strong answer classifies data by the cost of losing one item: telemetry, presence, cache invalidation hints and approximate analytics are cheap and often correctly served by at-most-once with sampling; orders, payments, ledger entries and audit records are not. It then makes the acceptance explicit and measured — a documented loss budget, a counter for dropped messages on a dashboard, and a statement of who agreed to it — because undocumented loss is indistinguishable from a bug. It also distinguishes designed loss (shedding under overload, dropping stale positions) from accidental loss (a swallowed exception), and insists the second is never acceptable regardless of the data's value, because it is unmeasured.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Cost of durability — replication, confirms and dedup all cost latency and money; for 10^6 events per second of telemetry the bill is real.
  • Detection — accepted loss must be counted, or you cannot tell 0.01% from 5%.
  • Downstream expectations — an analytics consumer that assumes completeness will publish wrong numbers; the loss policy must be part of the contract.
  • Reversibility — data you drop cannot be recovered; prefer shedding at the edge over dropping after acceptance.

Weak answer

"We never lose data." Every system loses data under some failure; the question is which failures and how much. An absolute claim usually means nobody has measured, and the follow-up ("what is your RPO on a region loss?") exposes it.

principalOne shared cluster for the whole company, or a cluster per team?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you weigh blast radius against operational cost and organisational maturity, and that you know the middle grounds. A shared cluster is cheaper, easier to keep current, and gives one place to enforce standards — at the price of one failure domain and noisy-neighbour risk, which quotas mitigate but do not remove. Cluster-per-team isolates failures and lets teams move at their own pace, at the price of N upgrade burdens, N sets of dashboards and fragmented discovery, which in practice means several clusters end up two years out of date. The strong answer usually lands on a small number of clusters split by criticality or domain rather than by team — a tier-1 cluster with strict change control, a general cluster, and a sandbox — plus quotas, ownership metadata and a single platform team owning the tooling. It also names the deciding factor: whether you have the people to operate more than one well.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Blast radius versus efficiency — one cluster means one incident affects everyone; many clusters mean many incidents, each smaller.
  • Upgrade velocity — a shared cluster upgrades once for everyone but needs coordination; per-team clusters drift and become unpatched.
  • Discovery and reuse — events are worth more when they are findable; fragmentation quietly kills reuse.
  • Cost — replication and minimum cluster sizes mean many small clusters are considerably more expensive per byte.

Weak answer

"Give every team their own cluster so they are independent." Independence in operations is dependence on expertise you probably do not have five times over, and it usually produces five differently-configured, differently-aged clusters and no shared standards.

principalA junior engineer proposes adding a queue to fix a performance problem. How do you respond?

What a strong answer demonstrates

Coaching instinct plus technical judgment. The strong answer starts by finding out what the performance problem actually is, because a queue helps burst absorption and slow non-essential work and does nothing for a slow query, an N+1, or a saturated database — and if the downstream is the bottleneck, a queue moves the failure from "the request times out" to "the backlog grows forever", which is worse because it is silent. It treats the proposal as a hypothesis to test rather than a suggestion to accept or reject, asks for the measurement that would confirm it, and — importantly — makes sure the engineer is the one who finds the answer, because the goal is a better engineer, not just a better design. It also names what the queue would cost so the trade is visible: eventual consistency, error reporting, an extra dependency, and a new class of operational work.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Where the queue helps — burst absorption, moving non-essential work out of the request, failure isolation.
  • Where it does not — a slow dependency at steady state; the queue just relocates the backlog.
  • Teaching versus shipping — being right quickly is worth less than the engineer learning to diagnose; say that out loud in the interview.
  • Reversibility — a queue added in haste becomes a contract other teams depend on; that is why the bar is a measurement, not an intuition.

Weak answer

"No, that is not the right fix" without diagnosis, or "sure, queues are good for load" without one either. The interviewer is watching for how you handle a colleague's idea as much as for the technical content.

principalIs exactly-once processing worth pursuing?

What a strong answer demonstrates

Precision about what is achievable and where the boundary sits. The strong answer separates exactly-once delivery (impossible in general, because the acknowledgement can always be lost) from exactly-once processing (achievable within a transactional boundary, and approximable everywhere else with at-least-once plus idempotency). It then argues on cost: Kafka's EOS is cheap and correct inside Kafka, so use it for consume-transform-produce and for Streams; but the moment an external system is involved, idempotency at the sink is both necessary and sufficient, and building elaborate machinery to avoid it is effort spent on the wrong problem. The senior framing is that idempotency is a property you want anyway — it makes retries, replays, backfills and incident repair safe — so the pragmatic route is to invest there first and treat broker-level EOS as an optimisation for the paths where it applies.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Scope — Kafka EOS ends at the Kafka boundary; say this before you are asked.
  • Cost — transactional overhead, control records, and read_committed consumers stalling behind an open transaction.
  • Idempotency has other payoffs — safe replay, safe backfill, safe repair; EOS gives none of those.
  • Team comprehension — a system whose correctness depends on a subtle broker feature is harder to operate than one that is simply idempotent.

Weak answer

"Yes, we enable exactly-once semantics." It is a configuration answer to a design question, and it invites the follow-up that ends badly: "and what happens when the consumer writes to Postgres?"

principalYour event schema needs a breaking change and forty consumers depend on it. What do you do?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you treat this as a product and communication problem with a technical implementation, not the reverse. The strong answer first asks whether the change can be made additive — a new field plus a deprecation of the old one is nearly always possible and turns a coordination problem into a timeline. If it genuinely cannot, the plan is: publish v2 alongside v1 from the same producer, instrument consumption per version so you can see who is where, give a stated deprecation window with the date in writing, help the slowest consumers directly rather than waiting for them, run a brownout before the removal to find the consumers nobody knew about, then remove. It also names the escape hatch — a translation shim that publishes v1 from v2 for laggards — and the political reality that a deadline without an owner and a visible burndown will slip indefinitely.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Additive first — most breaking changes are avoidable; a slightly uglier schema is cheaper than forty migrations.
  • Dual publishing cost — double the storage and network for the window; bounded and worth it.
  • Replay — old data in the topic is still v1, so consumers must handle both while history remains.
  • Trust — a producer that breaks consumers once will be routed around forever; the migration's real currency is credibility.

Weak answer

"Announce the change and give everyone a deadline." Announcements do not migrate consumers; instrumentation, help and brownouts do. And "we told them" is not a defence when the incident lands on a team that never saw the email.

principalBuild a small platform capability in-house, or adopt a heavyweight framework?

What a strong answer demonstrates

An honest total-cost comparison rather than a preference. The strong answer scopes what you would actually build — usually far less than the framework does — and compares against the framework's real costs: learning curve, opinionated structure, upgrade treadmill, debugging through someone else's abstraction, and licensing risk. The 2026 example worth naming is the .NET messaging ecosystem: MassTransit v9 moved to commercial licensing with v8 losing support at the end of 2026, which forced many teams into an unplanned decision between paying, migrating to Wolverine or Rebus, or writing the thin layer they needed. That is the argument for keeping the dependency surface small and the business logic framework-free, and it is also the argument against writing your own: a saga engine or an outbox looks small until you need timers, versioning, poison handling and observability. My default: adopt for genuinely hard things (durable execution, stream processing), build for thin things (an outbox, an idempotent consumer wrapper), and keep the boundary explicit either way.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Maintenance — code you write is code you own at 3am, forever, including when its author leaves.
  • Licensing and sustainability — an open-source dependency can change terms; assess the project's funding model, not just its stars.
  • Hiring and onboarding — a common framework has a talent pool; a bespoke one has a bus factor.
  • Exit — keeping business logic out of framework types is what makes any of these decisions reversible.

Weak answer

"Never reinvent the wheel" or "frameworks are bloat". Both are slogans. The graded content is the cost model and the named exit strategy.

principalHow much consistency should a startup buy on day one?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you can distinguish decisions that are cheap to change later from decisions that are not. The strong answer says: buy the strongest consistency the simplest architecture gives you — a single relational database with real transactions — because it costs almost nothing at low volume and removes an entire class of bugs while you are still discovering the domain. Then spend the design effort on the few things that are expensive to retrofit: an identifier scheme, idempotency keys on external side effects, and a boundary where events could later be published. Do not build sagas, event sourcing or a broker until there is a second service or a genuine load-levelling need. The judgment being tested is whether you can resist premature distribution while still leaving the door open, and whether you can name the specific one-way doors rather than saying "keep it simple" and stopping there.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Speed now versus rework later — a monolith with transactions is faster to build and easier to split later than a distributed system built too early.
  • One-way doors — identifier schemes, tenancy model, and whether external calls carry idempotency keys.
  • Hiring reality — a five-person team cannot operate a broker, a stream processor and a saga engine and also ship product.
  • When to revisit — name the trigger: a second team, a second service, or a load profile the database cannot absorb.

Weak answer

"Design for scale from day one." It is the most expensive advice in the industry: it spends the scarcest resource — time before product-market fit — on a load that may never arrive, and it usually produces a distributed system with a monolith's coupling.

principalA vendor pitches a message broker that promises exactly-once, infinite scale and no operations. How do you evaluate it?

What a strong answer demonstrates

Structured scepticism rather than reflexive dismissal. The strong answer asks for the guarantee's scope in writing (exactly-once between which two points, under which failure modes), for the behaviour under partition and under a zone loss, for published quotas and what happens when you hit them, for the durability model (how many copies, synchronous or not, what is the stated RPO), and for independent verification — a Jepsen-style report or the ability to run your own fault injection against a trial cluster. It also evaluates the non-technical risks that actually decide these things: the company's funding and longevity, the exit path if they change licensing or are acquired, whether the protocol is open, and how support works at 3am. And it insists on a proof of concept with your real message shape and volume, including the degraded case, because vendor benchmarks are run on the happy path.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Marketing versus specification — insist the guarantee be stated in terms of observable client behaviour under named failures.
  • Exit cost — an open protocol (Kafka's, AMQP 1.0) makes the decision reversible; a proprietary one does not.
  • Operational transparency — "no operations" usually means "no visibility"; ask what you can see when it is slow.
  • Concentration risk — a new critical dependency on a young company is a business decision, not just a technical one.

Weak answer

"Exactly-once is impossible, so they are lying." Rude and imprecise: effectively-once processing within a defined boundary is real, and several vendors deliver it honestly. Ask for the boundary; the answer tells you whether they are serious.

principalSix months in, your event-driven migration is half done and the team wants to stop. What now?

What a strong answer demonstrates

That you can make a reversal decision on evidence and without ego. The strong answer separates three possible causes — the approach is wrong, the execution is wrong, or the team is exhausted — because the remedies differ completely. It gathers evidence: which of the original goals have been met, what the incident rate and lead time look like before and after, and which specific pain is driving the request. Then it names the worst outcome explicitly, which is stopping half way and living permanently with two paradigms and double the operational surface — so the choice is finish, revert, or deliberately stabilise at a defensible boundary, and the last of those is often correct. Whatever the decision, it is written down with the reasoning and the revisit trigger, and the team hears that stopping is a legitimate outcome rather than a failure, because otherwise the next honest signal will not come.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Sunk cost — six months spent is not an argument for spending twelve more; decide on the remaining cost and remaining value.
  • Half-migrated is the worst state — two paradigms, double tooling, double on-call knowledge.
  • Credibility — reversing a decision you championed, with evidence, buys more trust than defending it.
  • Partial success is real — the interactions that genuinely benefited can stay; "all or nothing" is a false frame.

Weak answer

"Push through, we have already invested." It is the sunk-cost fallacy with a leadership veneer, and it is exactly the behaviour the question is designed to detect.

17Design exercises

These are the 45-minute whiteboard exercises this topic leads to. Read the prompt, ask the clarifying questions before designing anything — interviewers grade that explicitly — and attempt the design before opening the solution. Each solution outline is deliberately a skeleton plus the trade-offs you are expected to name unprompted, because the interviewer is listening for the reasoning, not for a specific architecture.

Exercise 1 — Order processing for an e-commerce platform

Prompt

Design the order-processing pipeline for a retailer doing 5,000 orders per minute at peak, with services for payment, inventory, fulfilment, notification and analytics. Orders must never be lost, customers must never be double-charged, and the platform must survive the loss of an availability zone. Walk me through the flow, the failure handling and what you would monitor.

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • Is overselling acceptable, and if so at what rate — does inventory fail closed or fail open?
  • What is the acceptable end-to-end latency from "place order" to "customer sees confirmation"? Seconds or minutes?
  • Are payments captured immediately or authorised now and captured on dispatch?
  • Is inventory a single authoritative store, or per-warehouse with its own consistency?
  • What must be synchronous from the customer's point of view — is a "we are processing your order" page acceptable?
  • Peak versus average: is 5,000/min a flash-sale spike or a steady rate?
seniorSolution outline and expected trade-offs

Shape

Synchronous, in the customer's request: validate the cart, take a payment authorisation with a client-supplied idempotency key, and write the order plus an outbox row in one database transaction. Everything after that is asynchronous, driven by an orchestrated saga: reserve inventory, capture payment, create the fulfilment job, notify the customer, feed analytics. The order topic is keyed by order id so all events for one order are ordered; inventory reservation is a conditional update in the inventory service (WHERE available >= qty) so concurrency is handled by the database rather than by a distributed lock. Every consumer is idempotent via an inbox keyed by (message id, handler). The confirmation page shows a pending state and polls or subscribes; the confirmation email is emitted by the saga once the pivot (payment capture) succeeds.

customer -> API  [tx: order row + outbox row]  -> 202 + order id
                     |
              outbox relay (poll or CDC)
                     v
              orders topic (key = orderId, RF=3 across 3 AZs, min.isr=2)
                     |
           orchestrator saga (durable state, timeouts, compensations)
             -> reserve stock   -> capture payment (PIVOT) -> fulfil -> notify
             compensations: release stock, void auth, cancel order
                     |
              analytics consumer group (independent, may lag freely)

capacity: 5,000/min = ~83/s steady; size for 10x flash-sale burst.
partitions: 24 (parallelism, not throughput). Consumers: 24 max per group.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Authorise now, capture later — makes payment compensatable, which moves the pivot later in the saga and dramatically simplifies failure handling.
  • Inventory fails closed — overselling is worse than a lost sale for most retailers; if the business says otherwise, oversell deliberately with a reconciliation and a customer-facing apology path.
  • Asynchronous confirmation — a pending page is a product decision; get it agreed, because it is the thing that makes the whole design possible.
  • At-least-once plus idempotency — not broker exactly-once, because payment and email are outside Kafka.
  • AZ loss — RF=3 across three AZs with min.insync.replicas=2 keeps producing through one AZ loss; consumers reconnect and lag briefly.
  • Monitoring — freshness from order placed to confirmation, saga instances by state and age, DLQ depth per consumer, per-partition lag, and an invariant check that every paid order has exactly one capture.

Follow-ups the interviewer will ask

  • Flash sale at 50,000/min? — The queue absorbs it; consumers scale to the partition count; inventory becomes the contention point, so shard hot SKUs or move to a reservation service with in-memory counters backed by a durable log.
  • Payment provider is down for 20 minutes? — Saga timeouts hold, orders stay in awaiting-payment, breaker opens, backlog stays in the broker; the customer sees pending, and you decide a maximum wait after which you compensate.
  • Duplicate order submission from a double click? — Client-supplied idempotency key at the API, before anything else happens.

Exercise 2 — Real-time analytics for a ride-hailing platform

Prompt

Design a pipeline that ingests location pings from 500,000 active drivers every five seconds, and serves two consumers: a real-time dashboard showing supply and demand per city zone updated every ten seconds, and a batch pipeline computing per-driver daily earnings and utilisation that must be exactly correct. Mobile clients are frequently offline for minutes at a time.

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • How stale may the dashboard be — is a ten-second-old number acceptable, or must it be the last ping?
  • Must the batch numbers be reproducible if rerun a week later? (This decides event time versus processing time.)
  • How late can a ping arrive and still count — minutes, hours, a day?
  • Do we keep raw pings, and for how long? Are they personal data?
  • Is a driver's ping order significant, or is only the latest position needed?
  • What is the read volume on the dashboard, and how many zones are there?
seniorSolution outline and expected trade-offs

Shape

Ingest at 100,000 pings per second into a log keyed by driver id, with the client's event time in the payload and the broker's append time also retained. Two consumers with different semantics from the same topic: the dashboard is a stateful stream job doing a ten-second tumbling aggregation per zone with a short grace period, optimised for latency and explicitly allowed to be approximate; the earnings pipeline is an event-time job with a long allowed lateness (hours) writing to a warehouse, optimised for correctness and reproducibility. Raw pings are retained briefly on the broker (a day or two) and archived to object storage for reprocessing; personal-data handling is by driver id with the location payload encrypted per driver so erasure is possible. Late pings update the earnings result and are dropped-with-a-counter by the dashboard.

500k drivers / 5s = 100k pings/s, ~200 bytes = ~20 MB/s raw
  compression (zstd) ~4-6x -> ~4 MB/s on the wire
  partitions: 64-128, key = driverId (ordering per driver, spread by hash)
  retention: 24-48h hot; archive to object storage for reprocessing

pings topic
  ├─ dashboard job   processing-time-ish, 10s tumbling per zone, grace 5s
  │                   -> in-memory/Redis serving layer, ~1-2s freshness
  └─ earnings job    EVENT TIME, allowed lateness 6h, watermark per partition
                      -> warehouse table, reproducible, retractable
archive (object storage, partitioned by day) -> reprocessing / backfill

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Two consumers, two semantics — the whole point: fast-and-approximate and slow-and-correct read the same log rather than fighting over one pipeline.
  • Event time for earnings — because a driver offline for ten minutes must still be paid correctly, and because reruns must reproduce.
  • Idle-partition watermarks — a quiet partition would otherwise stall the earnings watermark and freeze output; name this, it is the classic bug.
  • Dropped late data is counted — the dashboard's drop counter is a first-class metric, not a silent behaviour.
  • Cost — at 20 MB/s raw with RF=3 and two consumer groups, network and storage dominate; compression and short hot retention are the levers.
  • Privacy — location is personal data; per-driver encryption plus a retention limit makes erasure and compliance tractable.

Follow-ups the interviewer will ask

  • A city has a surge and one zone is 30% of pings? — Zone is not the key (driver id is), so the ingest is fine; the aggregation job's state for that zone is the hot spot — pre-aggregate per partition and combine, rather than shuffling all pings for a zone to one task.
  • A phone reconnects with two hours of buffered pings? — Event time plus allowed lateness handles earnings; the dashboard drops them and counts. Rate-limit per device so a reconnect burst cannot flood the ingest.
  • The earnings number was wrong for a day? — Reprocess from the archive into a new table version, compare, then swap — the blue-green projection rebuild.

Exercise 3 — Multi-region active-active for a payments platform

Prompt

Your payments platform runs in one region. The business wants active-active across two regions, 3,000 km apart, for regulatory resilience: either region must be able to serve all traffic, and a region loss must be survivable with minimal data loss. Design it, and be explicit about what you cannot guarantee.

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • What is the acceptable RPO and RTO on a full region loss — zero, seconds, minutes?
  • Are customers pinned to a region (by residency or by account), or must any region serve any customer?
  • Which operations must be strongly consistent — balance changes only, or also authorisations and limits?
  • Is there a regulatory data-residency constraint that forbids replicating some data?
  • Is "active-active" required for traffic serving, or is fast failover sufficient for the business need?
  • What is the current single-region latency budget, and how much can it grow?
principalSolution outline and expected trade-offs

Shape

The physics first: 3,000 km is roughly 30ms of round-trip light-in-fibre before any processing, so a synchronous cross-region quorum on every payment costs at least that per write. The design that works is partitioned ownership rather than symmetric writes: each account has a home region that owns its balance, both regions serve read and initiation traffic for everyone, and a write for a non-local account is either forwarded to its home region (paying the 30-60ms) or rejected. Replication of the authoritative log is asynchronous cross-region, giving a small non-zero RPO, and failover is a deliberate, fenced operation that re-homes the failed region's accounts with an epoch bump so the old region cannot resume writing. Idempotency keys make forwarding and retry safe; reconciliation runs continuously against both regions' ledgers.

accounts partitioned by home region (hash or residency)
  region A owns accounts A-set   region B owns accounts B-set
  both regions: read anything (may be stale), initiate anything
  write to a non-home account: forward (+30-60ms) or refuse

replication: async log shipping both ways, lag monitored as the RPO
failover: manual or automated with a fencing epoch
  1. confirm the region is really gone (not a partition)
  2. bump the epoch for the re-homed accounts
  3. old region, on return, is refused writes at epoch < current
  RPO = replication lag at the moment of loss (target: single-digit seconds)
  RTO = decision time + re-homing time (target: minutes, rehearsed)

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • You cannot have zero RPO and low latency across 3,000 km — say this explicitly and early; the rest of the design follows from it.
  • Partitioned ownership beats symmetric multi-master — symmetric writes on money means conflict resolution on balances, which nobody wants to explain to an auditor.
  • Failover must be fenced — an epoch the storage checks, or the returning region will double-write.
  • Split brain versus availability — automated failover risks acting on a partition; manual failover costs RTO. Name the choice and the detection threshold.
  • Reconciliation is not optional — continuous invariant checks across both ledgers, because eventual divergence in money is discovered by regulators otherwise.
  • Is active-active even required? — Push back once: active-passive with rehearsed failover meets most "regulatory resilience" asks at a fraction of the complexity.

Follow-ups the interviewer will ask

  • The link between regions is slow but not down? — The worst case. Detect it as lag, not as failure; degrade by refusing cross-region writes and alerting, rather than failing over into a partition.
  • The failed region comes back with unreplicated writes? — They are fenced out and must be reconciled as exceptions with a human decision, which is why you keep them rather than discarding.
  • Data residency forbids replicating EU data to the US? — Then the "either region serves everyone" requirement is legally impossible for those accounts; surface that early — it changes the requirement, not the design.

18Night-before cheat sheet

The ten questions most likely to appear in some form, with a one-sentence answer each. If you can say these ten out loud without hesitation, you will not be caught flat-footed in the first fifteen minutes of any distributed-systems interview.

  1. What does CAP actually say? During a network partition you must choose between availability and linearizable consistency; partitions are not a choice, and the rest of the time the real trade is latency versus consistency, which is why PACELC is the better framing.
  2. At-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once? Build at-least-once and make the handler idempotent; exactly-once delivery is impossible in general, and Kafka's exactly-once ends at the Kafka boundary.
  3. How do you avoid double-charging on a retry? A client-supplied idempotency key recorded in the same transaction as the effect, enforced by a unique constraint — never a check-then-act.
  4. Why can't you write to the database and publish to the broker? That is the dual-write problem: no ordering of the two is safe, so use a transactional outbox (or CDC) and accept at-least-once delivery.
  5. What ordering does Kafka guarantee? Per partition only, and therefore per key with the default partitioner; adding partitions remaps keys and breaks per-key ordering for the length of consumer lag.
  6. How many consumers can a Kafka consumer group use? At most one per partition — extra consumers idle; since 4.2 a share group lifts that limit by acknowledging per record, at the cost of ordering.
  7. What does acks=all promise? That every in-sync replica has appended the record to its page cache — which only means something with min.insync.replicas >= 2; Kafka's durability comes from replication, not from fsync.
  8. Why is a TTL-based distributed lock unsafe? Because a process pause is indistinguishable from a crash, so the old holder can act after expiry — you need a fencing token that the resource checks and rejects if stale.
  9. Choreography or orchestration for a saga? Choreography for two or three reactive steps, orchestration for anything longer or business-visible — because "where is order 12345 stuck?" needs an answer, and orchestration is the one that has it.
  10. Consumer lag is growing — what do you check? Whether production rose or processing slowed, then whether lag is on one partition or all, then rebalance rate and downstream latency; adding consumers is the right answer only about a third of the time.
