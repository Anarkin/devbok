01The consumer lag that only showed at peak

tier · senior performance problem diagnosed

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The alert was not subtle: records-lag-max on the settlement consumer group crossed two million during the 17:00 batch and took until roughly 21:30 to drain, every weekday. Nobody had shipped anything. Throughput on the producer side was flat at about 9,000 records/second all day, with a two-hour shoulder at 17:00 where it went to 24,000. The team's first theory was "the brokers are too small", which is the theory people reach for when lag is the only number on the dashboard. Lag is a level, not a rate; on its own it tells you the queue is growing but not which side of the equation moved, so the first thing I did was put producer rate and consumer rate on the same chart. Consumption was pinned at about 11,000 records/second and would not go higher no matter how much arrived. That flat ceiling is the signature of a bounded resource downstream, not of a broker under strain.

The next question is always the same one: is the ceiling per consumer, or per partition? A Kafka consumer group cannot have more useful members than the topic has partitions, so if the topic had 12 partitions and 12 pods, adding a thirteenth pod would do exactly nothing and would sit idle after the next rebalance. That was half of it — the topic had 12 partitions and we were running 12 pods — but the more interesting half was inside the pod. Our handler processed records one at a time in the poll loop, and each record did a synchronous HTTP call to a pricing service at about 80 ms p50. With a max.poll.records of 500, one poll took 40 seconds of wall clock, which meant each consumer processed roughly 12 records/second, times 12 partitions, gives about 150/second per pod-set — the arithmetic matched the observed ceiling almost exactly. The broker was never the bottleneck; a serial loop with a network call in it was.

I resisted the urge to just raise the partition count, because partitions are a one-way door — you can add them but you cannot remove them, and adding them changes key-to-partition mapping for every existing key, which silently breaks per-key ordering for in-flight work. Instead I fixed the loop. The pricing calls were independent of each other within a batch, so I fanned them out with a bounded degree of parallelism, kept ordering only where the key demanded it, and moved the offset commit to after the whole batch completed. Two numbers moved: per-pod throughput went from about 12 to about 380 records/second, and the 17:00 lag peak fell from two million to under 40,000, draining inside four minutes.

// before: strictly serial, one network round trip per record inside the poll loop
while (!ct.IsCancellationRequested)
{
    var result = consumer.Consume(ct);                 // one record
    var price = await pricing.QuoteAsync(result.Message.Value, ct);
    await sink.WriteAsync(price, ct);
    consumer.Commit(result);                           // commit per record: slow and chatty
}

// after: batch the poll, fan out inside the batch, preserve per-key order, commit once
var batch = Drain(consumer, max: 500, window: TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(250));
await Parallel.ForEachAsync(
    batch.GroupBy(r => r.Message.Key),                 // one lane per key = ordering kept
    new ParallelOptions { MaxDegreeOfParallelism = 32, CancellationToken = ct },
    async (lane, token) =>
    {
        foreach (var r in lane)                        // still serial *within* a key
            await sink.WriteAsync(await pricing.QuoteAsync(r.Message.Value, token), token);
    });
consumer.Commit();                                     // at-least-once, after the work is durable

What I would do differently: I would have alerted on time-to-drain rather than raw lag from the start. Lag of two million means nothing without a consumption rate next to it — two million records at 400,000/second is five seconds and nobody should be paged. I now express the SLO as "end-to-end message age at p99" and alert on that, because it is the number a customer would feel. I would also have caught this before production: our load test used a stubbed pricing service that returned in 1 ms, so the serial loop looked fast enough. Latency in a dependency is the thing that turns a poll loop into a bottleneck, and stubbing it away hides the entire class of bug.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you understand the parallelism model of the broker you use, or whether you just scale pods until the graph looks better. The signal they want: you separated arrival rate from service rate, you knew the partition count caps the group, and you could say why adding partitions is not free. The strongest version of this answer names the ordering guarantee you kept and the one you gave up.

The mistake candidates make on the performance archetype is narrating tools instead of findings — "we looked at Grafana and then at the broker logs" — with no hypothesis and no number. The second one, specific to messaging: quoting lag with no rate beside it, which tells the interviewer you have never actually sized a consumer.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

When did a queue or topic you own fall behind, and what was the actual ceiling?

Hint: name the resource, not the symptom. Partition count, prefetch, a database connection pool, a single-threaded handler, a downstream rate limit, a lock. If you can say "we were capped at X because Y", you have the story; if you can only say "it was slow", keep digging.

What is the largest lag or backlog figure you have personally watched drain?

Hint: reach for a real figure — a lag number, a queue depth, a dead-letter count, hours of catch-up. If the exact number is gone, remember the shape: did it grow linearly, spike and recover, or ratchet up a step every day? Each shape points at a different cause and saying so is itself a strong answer.

Have you ever added consumers and seen no improvement?

Hint: this is the classic partition-cap moment, and almost everyone has lived it. The interesting part is what you did next — repartition, restructure the key, add in-process concurrency, or switch to a queue-shaped consumption model.

Where does your current system trade ordering for throughput, and did anyone decide that deliberately?

Hint: look for parallel handlers, competing consumers on a shared queue, retries that jump ahead of in-order work. Admitting an accidental ordering hazard you later documented reads as maturity — as long as you say what you did about it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not simply add partitions?

Because partition count is effectively a one-way door and it changes semantics, not just capacity. Adding partitions rehashes the default key-to-partition mapping, so records for a key that used to land on partition 3 start landing on partition 17 while the old ones are still unconsumed — per-key ordering breaks precisely at the cutover, which is the worst possible moment. It also multiplies open file handles, replication streams and controller metadata, and you can never shrink back. I add partitions when I am confident the topic's steady-state volume has genuinely outgrown the current fan-out, I do it during a quiet window, and where key order matters I use a sticky or custom partitioner so existing keys keep their home.

At what scale does your fix stop working?

Roughly where the downstream dependency becomes the constraint instead of my loop. Thirty-two concurrent pricing calls per pod across twelve pods is 384 in flight; the pricing service tipped over somewhere near 600, so the next step is not more concurrency but a different shape — batching the pricing API, caching quotes with a short TTL, or moving pricing into the stream as a co-partitioned join so there is no network call at all. The honest second wall is memory: a 500-record batch of 40 KB messages held in flight is 20 MB per pod before any per-record allocation, and that is where a naive "just raise max.poll.records" ends in a pod restart loop.

What did you trade away?

Two things, and I would say both out loud. Global ordering across the partition is gone — I only preserve order within a key, which is what the business actually required, but it is a real narrowing and I wrote it into the topic's contract doc. And committing per batch instead of per record widens the redelivery window: a crash mid-batch replays up to 500 records, so the handler has to be idempotent. That was already true at-least-once, but batching makes the duplicate window visible rather than theoretical, so I paired the change with a dedup key on the sink's upsert.

How did you convince the team this was not a broker sizing problem?

I made the arithmetic public rather than arguing about it. I put producer rate, consumer rate and per-record handler duration on one dashboard, then wrote the three-line calculation — records per poll times handler latency equals seconds per poll — on the incident channel. The predicted ceiling of about 150 records/second matched the measured ceiling within a few percent, and once a number predicts the graph, the debate ends. That is a habit I try to teach: in distributed systems, arguing from a model that reproduces the observed number beats arguing from intuition every time, and it converts a disagreement into a shared calculation.

What would you do differently on Kafka 4.3 today?

I would seriously evaluate share groups, which became production-ready in Kafka 4.2 (KIP-932). They give queue semantics on a Kafka topic — several consumers can read the same partition concurrently with per-record acknowledgement and delivery counting — which is exactly the shape this workload wanted, because the pricing work was per-record independent and only needed order within a key. The trade is explicit: a share group deliberately gives up partition ordering, so I would still route the order-sensitive subset through a classic consumer group. Before 4.2 the only honest answers were more partitions or in-process concurrency; now "this topic is really a work queue" is a supported design, not a workaround.

How would you have caught this before production?

By load-testing with a dependency that has production-shaped latency, not a stub. Our test double answered in a millisecond, which made the serial loop look fine and hid a two-order-of-magnitude bottleneck. I now run the consumer against a fault-injecting proxy that adds the real p50 and p99 latency of each downstream, and I assert on sustained throughput rather than on "no errors". The general rule: in messaging, the interesting failure is almost never the broker, so a test that only exercises the broker proves very little.

02A log, not a queue — and where I was wrong

tier · principal hard architectural trade-off

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were replacing a nightly file exchange between six services with something event-driven, and the room split into two camps within about ten minutes: a partitioned log (Kafka) or a broker with real queues (RabbitMQ quorum queues, or a managed equivalent like Azure Service Bus). Both camps were arguing from taste. I made everyone answer four questions first, because those four decide it and almost nothing else does: do consumers need to replay history, does the work need strict ordering and at what granularity, is fan-out one-to-many or one-of-many, and how much operational surface can this team actually carry.

The distinction that matters is not "streaming vs. messaging", it is who owns the read position. In a log, the broker stores an immutable, retained sequence per partition and every consumer group holds its own offset, so a new consumer can join next year and read from the beginning, and ordering is guaranteed per partition. In a queue, the broker owns delivery: a message is handed to one consumer, acknowledged, and then it is gone, which makes competing consumers trivially easy and makes replay impossible unless you built it yourself. Retention versus consumption is the whole argument. Almost every other difference — consumer groups, prefetch, dead-lettering — follows from it.

For the four analytics and audit consumers, the log won outright: they wanted history, they wanted to add new consumers without asking the producer for anything, and they were fine with per-key ordering. So we published domain events to Kafka with a seven-day retention on the raw topics and compaction on the entity-state topics. Where I was wrong was the fifth consumer: an outbound document-generation worker whose per-job cost varied from 200 ms to 90 seconds. I put it on Kafka too, for consistency, and it was the wrong call for a reason I should have predicted — a partitioned log gives you at most one consumer per partition, so a single 90-second job blocks every subsequent record on that partition. Head-of-line blocking is a property of the log, not a bug in the consumer, and it cost us six weeks of workarounds before I admitted the shape was wrong and moved that worker to a quorum queue with competing consumers and per-message acknowledgement.

The current landscape blurs this less cleanly than it used to, which is a useful thing to say in an interview because it shows you are reading releases and not reciting a 2019 blog post. Kafka added share groups in 4.2 (KIP-932), giving queue-like consumption with per-record acknowledgement and delivery counts on a normal topic; RabbitMQ 4.2 made Khepri the default metadata store and 4.3 added 32 strict priority levels on quorum queues and first-class delayed retries. So "log or queue" is now closer to "which consumption mode per workload" than to "which product for the company". What has not changed is the underlying physics: if a consumer can be slow and the work is independent, you want the broker to own delivery; if consumers need history and per-key order, you want the log.

What I would do differently: I would stop optimising for one-broker consistency. The cost of a second broker technology is real — another thing to patch, monitor, and be on call for — but I priced it as if it were infinite, and it was not. Now I make the decision per workload with an explicit written rationale, and I accept a second transport when the shapes genuinely differ. The other change is procedural: I now write the decision as an ADR with the four questions answered, so that when someone asks in a year "why is this on Kafka", the answer is a document rather than an argument.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can reason from workload properties to a transport, rather than from brand loyalty. The signal: you name the axis that actually decides (who owns the read position, and whether job cost is uniform), you can articulate what each choice costs, and you know what changed recently. At principal level they are also probing whether you can hold a decision that has real downsides and still commit to it.

The mistake on the trade-off archetype is presenting a decision with no downside — "we chose Kafka and it was great". A trade-off story with no trade is not a story. The second mistake is picking a side by scale claims you never measured; say what your volume actually was.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not put everything on one transport for simplicity?

Because simplicity in the operational sense and simplicity in the fit sense pull in opposite directions, and I would rather be explicit about which one I am buying. One transport means one client library, one alerting setup, one upgrade path, and that is genuinely valuable for a small team. But forcing a workload with 450x variance in job cost onto a partitioned log buys you head-of-line blocking that you then paper over with retry topics, dead-letter queues and a scheduler — three moving parts to avoid adding one. My rule now: one transport by default, a second one when a workload's consumption shape is fundamentally different, and never a third without a written case.

At what scale does the log choice break down?

Two walls, at very different places. The first is per-key throughput: a single key's records all land on one partition, so one hot tenant cannot exceed what one partition and one consumer thread can do, no matter how large the cluster is — that is a key-design problem, not a capacity problem. The second is topic and partition count at the cluster level; KRaft removed the ZooKeeper metadata ceiling that used to bite around tens of thousands of partitions, so modern clusters handle far more, but every partition still costs file handles, replication streams and controller state, and rebalance and recovery times grow with it. I plan partition counts from target throughput and expected key cardinality, not from a round number.

What did you trade away by choosing the log for the analytics consumers?

Operational simplicity and per-message control. With a queue I would have got dead-lettering, per-message TTL and delayed retry for free; on Kafka I had to build the retry-topic ladder and a dead-letter topic myself, plus the tooling to inspect and replay from it, which is roughly two weeks of work that a broker would have given me on day one. I also took on schema governance, because a retained log means a message written today may be read by code written in two years — that is a benefit and an obligation at the same time. I judged replay and free fan-out worth those costs for that set of consumers, and I would make the same call again.

How did you get the team to agree, given the room was split?

I changed what we were arguing about. Instead of "which broker", I asked each consumer's owner to answer four workload questions in writing, and the answers made most of the assignment obvious without anyone having to lose. Where the answers genuinely conflicted — the document worker — I said so and proposed the split rather than pretending one option dominated. Then I wrote the ADR including the option we rejected and the conditions under which we would revisit, and I asked the loudest dissenter to review it. People accept a decision they disagree with far more readily when their objection is written down accurately in it.

Would share groups change your decision today?

For the document worker, quite possibly. Share groups went production-ready in Kafka 4.2 and give exactly what that workload needed — many consumers per partition, per-record acknowledgement, delivery counts that let you dead-letter a record after N attempts — so the head-of-line blocking that forced me onto a second broker is now addressable inside Kafka. I would still not call it a free win: share groups explicitly give up partition ordering, the operational maturity of RabbitMQ's quorum queues for this pattern is a decade deeper, and RabbitMQ 4.3 added strict priorities and delayed retries that a share group has no direct answer for. I would prototype it against the real job-cost distribution before switching, because the whole point of the original mistake was choosing by consistency instead of by measurement.

What would you do differently now?

I would measure the job-cost distribution before choosing, not after. One histogram of per-message processing time would have shown a long tail out to 90 seconds and made the head-of-line risk obvious in an afternoon; instead I reasoned from an average and lost six weeks. I would also write the ADR before implementation rather than during, because writing forces you to state the assumption that turns out to be wrong while it is still cheap to be wrong. The decision framework I used was sound — the failure was skipping the measurement that fed it.

03The poison message that stopped a partition

tier · staff production incident / outage

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

At 14:06 the order-fulfilment consumer stopped committing offsets on one partition out of 24, and by 14:20 that partition was 180,000 records behind while the other 23 were at zero. Nothing was down. Error rate was normal because the handler was catching, logging and retrying the same record forever, and the log line was identical every 200 ms, so the log-volume alert did not fire either. This is the signature failure mode of a partitioned log: a single record that cannot be processed does not fail the system, it silently converts one partition into a wall, and everything behind it waits. About 4% of orders — the ones whose key hashed to partition 11 — simply stopped moving, and they were spread across every region and customer tier, which made the support tickets look random.

The record itself was mundane. An upstream service had started emitting a decimal quantity (2.5) on a field our schema declared as an integer, because someone had relaxed a validation in a service two hops away. Deserialisation threw, our handler caught the exception, logged it, and did not commit — which is the correct behaviour for a transient failure and catastrophic for a permanent one. That distinction is the whole lesson: an at-least-once consumer must classify failures, because "retry forever" is right for a network blip and wrong for a message that will never parse. We had written the retry loop for the first case and never asked what the second case would do.

The incident call took eleven minutes to find it because the obvious tools lie here. Lag told us which partition, not which record. What actually located it was reading the consumer's current offset for partition 11 and dumping that exact record with a console consumer at that offset — the message was on screen thirty seconds later. I made the mitigation decision explicitly, and said it out loud on the call so the choice was on the record: we skipped the record by seeking past it, accepting that one order would be dropped and would need manual repair, rather than trying to hotfix the deserialiser under pressure with 180,000 records queued behind it. Restoring flow for thousands beat perfect handling for one; we captured the record first so nothing was actually lost.

// the fix that mattered: classify the failure, then route it
try
{
    await handler.HandleAsync(record, ct);
}
catch (Exception ex) when (IsPermanent(ex))       // schema, validation, business-rule violations
{
    await deadLetter.PublishAsync(record, reason: ex.Message, ct);
    consumer.Commit(record);                      // advance past it: the partition keeps moving
}
catch (Exception ex) when (IsTransient(ex))       // timeouts, 5xx, broker unavailable
{
    if (record.Attempt >= MaxAttempts)            // bounded, never infinite
    {
        await deadLetter.PublishAsync(record, reason: "retries exhausted", ct);
        consumer.Commit(record);
    }
    else
    {
        await retryTopic.PublishAsync(record with { Attempt = record.Attempt + 1 }, ct);
        consumer.Commit(record);                  // the retry ladder owns it now, not this partition
    }
}

The permanent fix had three parts, and I would defend the ordering. First, the classify-and-dead-letter logic above, so no single record can ever wall a partition again — that shipped the same afternoon. Second, a retry ladder of three topics with increasing delays (5 s, 1 min, 15 min) so transient failures still get patience without blocking live traffic; on a queue broker this is a built-in feature rather than something you build — RabbitMQ 4.3 added first-class delayed retries specifically to stop poison-message loops, and Azure Service Bus has had scheduled messages and a native dead-letter sub-queue for years. Third, schema enforcement at the producer: a schema registry with BACKWARD compatibility set on the topic, so the upstream change that caused this would have been rejected at publish time instead of discovered by a consumer three hops downstream.

What I would do differently: the alert. Lag per partition existed but was aggregated to a group-level max, and the real tell — "offset has not advanced on a partition that still receives records" — was not monitored at all. A stalled partition is a distinct signal from a slow one and deserves its own alarm. I would also have run this exact scenario as a game day; we had rehearsed broker failure and never rehearsed a single bad message, which is by far the more common incident.

What the interviewer is really probing

Incident-command judgment under a bad set of options, plus whether you understand that in an ordered log, one record is a single point of failure for everything behind it. The signal: you triaged before you diagnosed, you made an explicit, reversible mitigation choice with a stated cost, and your permanent fix addresses the class of failure rather than the one record.

The mistake candidates make on the outage archetype is telling it as a hero narrative — "I stayed up all night and fixed it". Interviewers want the decision, the trade-off, and the follow-up. The second mistake is stopping at the code fix and never mentioning the missing alert, which is usually the more important finding.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why skip the record rather than fix the deserialiser?

Because the cost of the two options was asymmetric and time-dependent. Fixing the deserialiser meant a code change, review, build and deploy — thirty to forty minutes on our pipeline — while 180,000 records aged and the backlog grew at about 400 records/second. Skipping cost one order, which we had captured to a file and could repair by hand. I would have made the opposite call if the bad record were part of a financial sequence where skipping breaks a downstream invariant, or if I could not capture it first. The general principle: prefer the reversible mitigation with the smaller, bounded, known cost, and never let "fix it properly" hold live traffic hostage.

What if it had been thousands of poison records, not one?

Then skipping is no longer a mitigation, it is data loss at scale, and the decision inverts: I would stop the consumer entirely rather than let it stream thousands of records into a dead-letter topic nobody has capacity to reprocess. A mass poisoning almost always means an upstream producer change, so the fastest real fix is to roll back the producer and then replay from the offset where the bad data started — which is exactly the situation the log's retained history is for. That is also the moment to be loud with stakeholders, because a paused consumer with a known restart point is a far better position than a drained backlog you cannot reconstruct.

At what scale does the retry ladder become the problem?

When the retry topics start carrying a meaningful fraction of live traffic, which for us was around 2%. At that point retries compete for the same consumer capacity as first-time deliveries, and if a downstream is broadly failing you get a retry storm that amplifies load exactly when the system is weakest. The fix is a retry budget rather than a per-message retry count — cap retries as a percentage of throughput, and shed the excess straight to the dead-letter topic — plus a circuit breaker so a failing dependency stops receiving traffic instead of receiving it three times over. Retries are a load multiplier, and every retry policy should have an answer for what happens when everything fails at once.

What did you trade away with the dead-letter approach?

Ordering, and I want to be precise about it. Once a record can be dead-lettered or pushed to a retry topic while its successors proceed, per-key ordering is no longer guaranteed for that key — record 5 can be applied before record 4 comes back from a 15-minute retry. For fulfilment that was acceptable because the operations are commutative at the entity level and the sink upserts by version, but I would not do it blindly for a workload with genuine sequential dependencies. Where order is sacred, the correct behaviour really is to stop that key's stream and page a human; the design choice is deciding which of your streams are which, and writing it down.

How did you stop this class of bug at the source?

Schema enforcement at publish time with an explicit compatibility mode, which turns a runtime failure three hops downstream into a build or publish failure in the producer's own pipeline. We set BACKWARD compatibility on the shared topics, meaning new schemas must be readable by consumers on the old schema, and wired the compatibility check into CI so an incompatible change fails the producer's build. That is the structural lesson from this incident: in an event-driven system the producer's tests cannot tell it whether a change is safe, because safety is a property of every consumer — only a registry or contract test knows.

Would a queue broker have avoided this entirely?

It would have avoided the blocking, not the bad message. On a quorum queue or Service Bus, a nacked message goes to the dead-letter queue after the delivery limit and the other consumers keep working, so the blast radius is one message rather than one partition — RabbitMQ 4.3 sharpened this further with delayed retries aimed squarely at poison-message loops. But the record is still broken, still needs a human, and the upstream schema drift still happened; the queue changes the failure from an outage into a ticket. That is a big improvement and it is exactly why I now treat "can a single message block others" as a first-class question when choosing a transport, rather than something to discover during an incident.

04ZooKeeper to KRaft, in flight

tier · staff migration / version upgrade

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were on Kafka 3.5 with a three-node ZooKeeper ensemble, and the deadline was not ours: Kafka 4.0 removed ZooKeeper mode entirely, so staying put meant no security patches on a cluster carrying every order in the business. The awkward part is that you cannot jump. The supported route is to reach 3.9 first — the bridge release that can run the KRaft migration — do the ZooKeeper-to-KRaft migration there, and only then move up the 4.x line. So a "version upgrade" was really three projects wearing one name: get to 3.9, change the cluster's metadata plane while it serves traffic, then walk 4.0 → 4.1 → 4.2 → 4.3. I sequenced it that way explicitly, with a go/no-go at each boundary, because the failure modes are completely different.

The concept worth understanding, and the reason interviewers like this story, is what KRaft actually changes. In ZooKeeper mode, cluster metadata — topics, partitions, ISR, configs — lived in an external consensus system, and one broker acted as controller by watching it; failover meant a new controller reading all of that state back, which is why large clusters took minutes to recover and why partition counts had a practical ceiling. KRaft moves metadata into an internal Raft-replicated log that the controllers own, so metadata is just another log with offsets, failover is a leader election over that log rather than a full state reload, and controller recovery drops from minutes to under a second on realistic clusters. It is the same architectural move as the transactional outbox in miniature: stop coordinating two systems, make one of them the log.

The migration itself is the interesting operational sequence. You provision a KRaft controller quorum, put the brokers into migration mode so they dual-write metadata to both ZooKeeper and the KRaft log, wait for the controllers to report the migration complete, then roll the brokers out of migration mode and finally decommission ZooKeeper. The dual-write window is the risky part and it is not reversible after you exit it, so I insisted on two things: a full rehearsal in a staging cluster restored from a production metadata snapshot, and a written rollback plan with a hard cut-off — if we had not reached "migration complete" within our 90-minute window, we reverted to ZooKeeper mode and went home. We used the rehearsal to time each step, and the real run took 64 minutes with zero consumer-visible impact, which I attribute mostly to having done it once already rather than to anything clever.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not skip the bridge release and rebuild the cluster fresh?

We costed it and it lost, though not by as much as people assume. A fresh KRaft cluster plus MirrorMaker 2 replication avoids the in-place migration entirely, but it introduces a harder problem: moving every consumer group's offsets across clusters. MirrorMaker translates offsets — improved again in 4.3 with batch offset translation for multiple consumer groups (KIP-1239) — yet the cutover still means every consumer stops on one cluster and starts on another at a translated position, with duplicate or gap risk at the seam for 40-odd groups owned by nine teams. In-place migration kept offsets untouched, which meant no consumer team had to do anything. I would choose the fresh-cluster route if I also wanted to change something structural — cloud region, partition layout, security model — because then you are paying the coordination cost for more than one benefit.

What would you have done if the migration stalled halfway?

Reverted, because that was the plan we wrote before we started and the whole point of writing it was to make the decision when we were calm. While brokers are in migration mode the metadata is dual-written, so ZooKeeper still holds a complete, authoritative copy and going back is a broker roll rather than a restore. The line we could not cross was exiting migration mode — after that ZooKeeper stops receiving updates and reverting means restoring from a snapshot and losing whatever metadata changed since, so I gated that step behind an explicit go/no-go with the controller's own "migration complete" signal, not a human eyeball on a dashboard.

At what scale does this migration get genuinely dangerous?

It scales with metadata volume rather than with data volume, which surprises people. The dual-write window is proportional to topic and partition count, so a cluster with a few hundred topics migrates in an hour while one with tens of thousands of partitions can run long enough to threaten any sane maintenance window. That is also where the payoff is biggest, since KRaft's recovery advantage grows with metadata size. Above that scale I would migrate in stages if the topology allows, keep an extra controller quorum node for headroom, and rehearse against a restored copy of the real metadata rather than a synthetic one — a synthetic cluster with a tenth of the partitions tells you nothing about the timing.

What did you trade away by going one minor version at a time?

Calendar time — about five weeks of baking that a single jump would have compressed into a weekend — and some team patience, because "we are still upgrading Kafka" got old. What I bought was attribution: when the rebalance-duration alerts went noisy, I knew it was KIP-848 arriving in 4.0 and not one of four other changes, and the investigation took an hour instead of a day. On a system every service depends on, I will pay weeks for the ability to attribute a regression to one change. I would batch minors only on a cluster with a genuinely small blast radius and a fast rollback.

What is actually better now, concretely?

Controller failover went from tens of seconds to sub-second in our tests, which matters because that window is when produce requests to affected partitions stall. We deleted an entire ZooKeeper ensemble — three nodes, its own monitoring, its own upgrade cycle, its own on-call knowledge that two people had. And we can now take features that only exist on the 4.x line: share groups for queue-shaped workloads (4.2), and in 4.3 things like follower fetch from the tiered offset (KIP-1023) and OAuth client assertion support (KIP-1258) that directly reduce cross-AZ traffic cost and credential handling respectively. Being on a supported line is itself the feature; everything else is a bonus.

How did you communicate the risk to non-engineering stakeholders?

In terms of exposure and choice, not in terms of Kafka. The framing was: the version we run stopped receiving security fixes, the only supported path takes about six weeks, and here is the one 90-minute window where a mistake could delay orders — with the abort plan that caps that risk. I gave them a decision they could actually make (which window, and whether we accept a possible second attempt) rather than a technical briefing they could only nod at. Afterwards I sent a three-line note saying it was done and nothing broke, because the follow-up is what buys you trust for the next risky change.

05Exactly-once vs. idempotent consumers

tier · senior technical disagreement

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A senior colleague wanted Kafka transactions and exactly-once semantics for a payments-adjacent pipeline; I wanted at-least-once delivery with idempotent consumers and a dedup table. We disagreed for two weeks, and the disagreement was worth having because both positions were defensible — which is exactly why it turned into a good story. What broke the deadlock was refusing to argue about "exactly once" as a slogan and instead writing down, for our specific pipeline, where each duplicate could actually originate.

The technical crux, and the thing most candidates get wrong in interviews: Kafka's exactly-once semantics is a guarantee about a read-process-write cycle inside Kafka. A transactional producer with an idempotent write path plus read_committed consumers gives you atomic "consume from topic A, produce to topic B, commit the offset" — all three succeed or none do. It says nothing about a side effect outside Kafka. The moment your handler charges a card, writes to Postgres, or calls a partner API, the transaction cannot cover it, and you are back to at-least-once with an idempotency requirement. Our pipeline's terminal step wrote to a relational database and called an external ledger, so EOS would have protected the two internal hops and left the expensive hop unguarded.

So I drew the pipeline and marked every boundary. Two hops were topic-to-topic and genuinely benefited from transactions; one hop crossed into a database, where the correct tool is an idempotency key with a unique constraint. We ended up doing both, which is the outcome I would defend: transactions where they are cheap and exact, idempotency where transactions cannot reach. My colleague was right that EOS was worth using; I was right that it was not sufficient. The framing that got us there was "which boundary does each mechanism cover", not "who is correct".

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not use exactly-once semantics everywhere and skip the dedup table?

Because EOS is scoped to Kafka's own read-process-write cycle and our terminal step wrote to a database and an external ledger, which no Kafka transaction can enrol. Using it everywhere would also have bought unnecessary cost on hops where a duplicate is harmless — transactions add commit-interval latency, reduce throughput, and introduce operational concerns like hanging transactions blocking read_committed consumers and producer fencing on rebalance. My rule is to apply the strongest guarantee at the boundary where a duplicate is actually expensive and the cheapest sufficient one everywhere else, because uniform maximum strictness is a real tax paid for imaginary safety.

What if the message id is not stable across retries?

Then the whole idempotency scheme is decorative, and that is the failure I look for first in any review. A dedup key must be assigned by the producer at the moment the business fact occurs and travel with the message — not generated by the consumer, not derived from a timestamp, not the broker offset, because a replay from a different offset or a re-publish would produce a new key for the same fact. Where a producer genuinely cannot assign one, the fallback is a deterministic hash of the business-identifying fields plus a version, which works as long as the payload is canonicalised. If neither is possible, I would say plainly that the system cannot be made idempotent and the duplicate has to be caught by a downstream reconciliation instead.

At what scale does the dedup table break?

It breaks on write amplification and on retention, in that order. Every message now costs an extra indexed insert, so at very high throughput the dedup table becomes the hottest table in the database and the primary-key index the hottest index — at that point I would partition by day and drop partitions rather than delete rows, because a bulk delete on a hot table is its own outage. Beyond roughly the point where the table stops fitting comfortably in memory, I would move dedup to a dedicated store with native TTL, such as Redis with a persistence guarantee that matches the risk. The retention window is the other constraint: it must exceed the longest possible redelivery, so if you add a 30-day manual replay capability, a 7-day dedup window silently becomes a correctness bug.

What did you trade away?

Latency and a permanent piece of infrastructure. Transactions cost about 18 ms at p99 on the internal hops, which we could afford but which would be disqualifying for a sub-10 ms path. The dedup table is a schema, a growth curve, a retention policy and a thing every new consumer author has to be taught about — I paid for that with a shared handler wrapper so nobody has to remember, but it is still cognitive load. And I gave up the simplicity of one story: instead of "we do exactly-once" the team now has to say "we do transactions here and idempotency there", which is more accurate and less memorable.

How did you keep the disagreement from damaging the relationship?

By making the argument about the pipeline rather than about the two of us, and by conceding early and specifically where he was right. I wrote the design doc with his position stated first and in its strongest form — if you cannot articulate the other view better than its author, you are not ready to disagree with it — and I asked him to review my prototype rather than presenting it as finished. When we landed on the combination, I said in the team channel that transactions on the internal hops were his call and they were the right one. That costs nothing and it is the difference between a colleague who will argue with you again next time, which you want, and one who quietly stops.

What would you do differently now?

I would prototype in week one instead of week two. Two days of measurement — actual added latency, actual dedup table growth — collapsed an argument that a week of assertion had not moved, and I should have reached for that immediately. I would also write the boundary diagram before the first conversation, because most transaction-semantics disagreements are really about people holding different implicit diagrams in their heads and never comparing them. Drawing the picture is often the entire intervention.

06The duplicates that were not duplicates

tier · mid nasty debugging session

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Support reported customers receiving the same notification two or three times, but only occasionally, and only for some customers. The producer team swore they published once, and they were right — I checked, and the topic held exactly one record per notification. So the duplication was happening on the consumption side, and the intermittency plus the clustering (bursts of duplicates, then hours of nothing) is the tell for a rebalance-related redelivery rather than a logic bug. Logic bugs are boring and constant; distributed bugs cluster around events.

The mechanism took me most of a day to pin down, and it is the single most useful thing I have learned about consumers. We had enable.auto.commit=true with the default 5-second interval, and our handler did its work asynchronously — it kicked off the notification send and returned immediately so the poll loop stayed fast. That means the consumer could commit offset 1,050 while the sends for records 1,020 to 1,050 were still in flight. Under normal running nobody notices. But every time a pod was rescheduled, the group rebalanced, and the new owner of that partition resumed from the last committed offset — which, depending on where the 5-second timer had landed, was sometimes behind the work already done and sometimes ahead of it. Behind means duplicates. Ahead would have meant silent loss, which is worse and which we had almost certainly also been doing without noticing.

The correlation that proved it was embarrassingly simple once I looked: I overlaid the duplicate timestamps from the notification service's own logs with the consumer group's rebalance events, and every burst sat inside a rebalance window. That is the general technique worth taking away — when a distributed bug is intermittent, stop reading code and start correlating timelines. Deploys, scale events, rebalances, leader elections, network partitions and GC pauses all have timestamps, and the bug almost always lives inside one of those windows.

// the bug: auto-commit advances the offset on a timer, regardless of whether the work finished
// (enable.auto.commit = true, auto.commit.interval.ms = 5000)
var record = consumer.Consume(ct);
_ = notifier.SendAsync(record.Message.Value, ct);   // fire and forget: offset moves anyway

// the fix: manual commit, after the work is durable, at batch boundaries
consumer = new ConsumerBuilder<string, Notification>(new ConsumerConfig
{
    EnableAutoCommit = false,                       // we own the offset now
    MaxPollIntervalMs = 300_000,                    // room for a slow batch before we are evicted
    IsolationLevel = IsolationLevel.ReadCommitted
}).Build();

foreach (var batch in Batches(consumer, ct))
{
    await notifier.SendAllAsync(batch, ct);         // awaited: the work is done
    consumer.Commit(batch.Last());                  // then, and only then, the offset moves
}

Two changes fixed it and one made it impossible to regress. Manual commit after awaited work removed the class of bug. Adding an idempotency key on the notification send meant that the duplicates that at-least-once delivery legitimately still produces — a crash between the send and the commit — became invisible to the customer, because the notification service now rejects a repeated key within its window. And I added a test that starts two consumers against a Testcontainers Kafka, kills one mid-batch, and asserts that every message is delivered at least once and that the sink shows each exactly once. That test has caught two regressions since.

What I would do differently: I would have questioned auto-commit on day one of writing the consumer, not after an incident. Auto-commit is a fine default for a consumer whose processing is synchronous, cheap and idempotent, and a trap for everything else — and the moment someone introduces async work inside the loop, the default silently becomes wrong. I now treat "where does this consumer commit, and what has definitely happened by then" as a mandatory question in code review for any handler.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can debug something that does not reproduce on demand, and whether you understand at-least-once delivery well enough to know that the fix is not "stop the duplicates" but "make duplicates harmless and stop creating extra ones". The signal: you used the intermittency pattern as evidence, you correlated with system events instead of reading code, and you distinguished the bug from the inherent guarantee.

The mistake on the debugging archetype is a story with no method — "I dug through the logs and eventually found it". Name the hypothesis, name what would have falsified it, name the evidence. The second mistake is blaming the producer team before checking; say what you verified first.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the duplicates cluster instead of appearing steadily?

Because the trigger was rebalances, not throughput. Auto-commit only produces a wrong offset when the consumer stops between the timer firing and the in-flight work completing, and the thing that stops a consumer is a group membership change — a pod rescheduled, a deploy, a session timeout after a long GC pause. Between those events the same code is perfectly correct, which is why it survived months in production and never reproduced locally. That clustering is diagnostic in itself: a defect that appears in bursts correlated with infrastructure events is almost never in the business logic.

Were you also losing messages?

Almost certainly yes, and that is the part I made sure the write-up said. If the auto-commit timer fired after the poll but the process died before the in-flight sends completed, the offset was ahead of the work and those notifications were never sent and never retried — invisible, because nobody complains about a message they did not know was coming. Duplicates get reported; gaps do not. We could not prove the historical loss because we had no independent record to reconcile against, which became its own action item: the notification service now records an intent row when the consumer accepts a record, so a gap is a query rather than a guess.

At what scale does manual commit become a problem?

When the batch takes long enough to threaten max.poll.interval.ms, which defaults to five minutes — exceed it and the broker assumes the consumer is dead, evicts it, rebalances the partition to someone else, and your commit is then rejected because you have been fenced. The result is a loop of slow batch, eviction, redelivery, slower batch. The fixes are to bound the batch by expected duration rather than by record count, to raise the interval deliberately when the work is genuinely long, or to hand long work to a queue and keep the poll loop fast. Committing more often is not the answer; it just shrinks the duplicate window while leaving the eviction risk untouched.

What did you trade away by moving to manual commit?

Simplicity, and I have watched that cost land on other people. Auto-commit is one config line and it is right for a whole class of consumers; manual commit means every handler author now has to think about where the commit goes, what has definitely happened by then, and what a mid-batch crash replays. I mitigated it by putting the pattern in a shared base handler so the default path is correct and you have to opt out deliberately, and by writing the reasoning into the team's consumer checklist. But it is genuinely more rope, and on a team with a lot of turnover I would weight that cost higher.

How would you detect this class of bug earlier next time?

Two ways, one cheap and one structural. The cheap one is a chaos test in CI: run two consumers against a real broker in Testcontainers, kill one mid-batch, and assert the sink's invariants — that catches commit-semantics bugs in seconds and it is now part of our template. The structural one is end-to-end reconciliation: count what the producer emitted and what the sink applied, per hour, and alert on divergence. That would have caught both the duplicates and the silent losses without a single customer report, and it is the thing I would build first in any pipeline where correctness matters more than latency.

Onboarding a large customer took us from 240 balanced partitions to 239 balanced partitions and one that was on fire. Partition 84 carried about 31% of total volume while the median partition carried 0.3%, its consumer sat at 90 seconds of lag while everyone else was at zero, and the broker hosting its leader showed materially higher disk write throughput than its peers. The cluster had plenty of headroom in aggregate, which is exactly what makes key skew such a good interview topic: the system is nowhere near capacity and one customer still cannot be served.

The cause was our partition key. We keyed events by tenantId because tenant-level ordering was a requirement, and the default partitioner hashes the key with murmur2 modulo the partition count — a perfectly uniform function fed a badly skewed input. Uniform hashing distributes keys evenly, not volume, and when one key carries a third of the traffic no hash function can save you. This is the same phenomenon as a hot shard in a database or a celebrity user in a social graph; the lesson transfers, and saying so signals that you understand the class rather than the instance.

The fix had to preserve the ordering guarantee we had actually promised, so the first job was finding out what that was. "Tenant ordering" turned out to be nobody's real requirement on inspection: the consumers needed ordering per entity — per order, per shipment — because those are the things whose state transitions must not be applied out of sequence. Cross-entity ordering within a tenant was never used. That let me change the key from tenantId to tenantId + ":" + entityId, which took the big customer's traffic from one partition to roughly 200 of them and preserved every guarantee anyone depended on. The whole scaling problem dissolved into a key-design problem, which is usually where these end up.

Why not just give the big tenant its own topic?

Because it solves this customer and institutionalises the problem. A dedicated topic means routing logic in every producer, a second set of consumers to deploy and monitor, and a standing question of "how big does a tenant have to be" that someone will have to answer again for the next one. Narrowing the key fixed the whole class in one change and made the system indifferent to tenant size. What if the hot key genuinely cannot be split — one entity really does carry 30%?

Then you have to break ordering deliberately or move the work out of the ordered path. The usual technique is salting: append a bucket number to the key so the entity spreads across N partitions, and accept that ordering now holds only within a bucket — which is only safe if the consumer can reconcile, for example by applying versioned upserts where a stale version is discarded. The alternative I prefer when it fits is to keep the ordered stream tiny (just the state transitions) and move the expensive work to an unordered queue keyed by nothing. What I would not do is pretend the ordering still holds; that is how you get a corruption incident six months later.

When entity-level cardinality stops growing with volume — for example if one entity becomes extremely chatty, an order with thousands of events, we are back to a single hot key with a smaller radius. I sized for roughly 5x current peak and wrote down the number at which we should revisit, because an undocumented headroom assumption is one nobody checks.

What did you trade away?

Tenant-level total ordering, which we had promised implicitly by keying on tenant even though no consumer used it. That is a real narrowing and it constrains future consumers: anyone who later wants "all events for this tenant in order" now cannot have it without a re-key or a merge step, and I documented that in the topic contract so it is a known constraint rather than an ambush. I also spent a full retention period running dual publishes, which doubled that topic's storage and produce cost for a week — cheap for the safety, but a real cost I had to get approved rather than assume.

How did you validate the migration was safe before cutting over?

By running the new consumers in shadow mode against a separate sink and comparing outcomes. For one retention period both topics carried the same events; the shadow consumers wrote to a parallel schema, and a reconciliation job compared final entity states between the two paths every hour. When they matched for 72 consecutive hours including a peak day, we cut over. The comparison also caught something the design review had missed — one consumer relied on receiving two different entities' events in a fixed relative order — and finding that in shadow rather than in production is exactly what the extra week bought.

How did you get the consumer teams to agree to change topics?

By making it nearly free for them and by leading with their pain, not mine. Four teams consumed that topic, and the ask was a config change plus a re-consume from the start of the new topic — I wrote the migration steps per team, ran the shadow comparison myself, and offered to pair on the cutover. Framing mattered too: this was not "help me fix a partition", it was "your slowest tenant's events currently arrive 90 seconds late and this removes that". When the change is small and the benefit is theirs, coordination stops being negotiation and becomes scheduling.

I found it while writing a runbook, which is where most of these are found. I wanted to document which services consumed the customer-events topic, so I listed the ACLs — and there were none. The cluster had been stood up years earlier with a single shared credential and allow.everyone.if.no.acl.found effectively in play, so any workload with the cluster credential could read any topic. Customer events carried full names, email addresses, postal addresses and, on one event type, partial payment metadata. Nineteen services had the credential; three had any business seeing that data. This was not an attack, and I want to be precise about that: it was a standing exposure with no evidence of misuse, which changes the urgency but not the obligation.

I raised it as a security finding the same afternoon rather than quietly fixing it, and that was the right call for a reason worth explaining in an interview: a data-access exposure has regulatory and disclosure implications that are not an engineer's to decide alone. I wrote it up with the blast radius (which topics, which fields, which credentials, what the retention was — so, how far back a reader could have gone), what I could and could not prove from the available logs, and a proposed remediation with a timeline. The remediation had three layers, and the ordering was deliberate — reduce the blast radius before hardening the perimeter, because the perimeter change is slower. First, per-service identities and least-privilege ACLs: each service got its own principal with explicit read or write grants per topic and per consumer group. Second, we split the topic so that sensitive fields moved to a separate, narrowly-granted topic and the general topic carried only an identifier plus non-sensitive attributes, which meant the other sixteen services kept working with no code change. Third, we tightened authentication — mTLS between services, and OAuth for the human and CI paths; Kafka 4.3 added OAuth client assertion support (KIP-1258), which lets a client authenticate with a signed assertion instead of a shared client secret, and that is the direction I would push any new cluster today.

09Killing the dual write

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Every service in the estate did the same unsafe thing: save to the database, then publish an event. Nine services, roughly forty call sites. It works almost all of the time, which is precisely what makes it durable tech debt — the failure rate was around 0.02% of writes, invisible in aggregate and infuriating when it hit a specific order. The reconciliation job we had built to paper over it was flagging about 60 divergences a week, each one a human ticket, and someone had quietly started calling it "the Monday list".

The reason the dual write cannot be made safe by trying harder is the part I would want an interviewer to hear. Two independent systems, no shared transaction: if the database commit succeeds and the publish fails, you have state with no event, and downstream never learns; if you publish first and the commit fails, you have an event for something that never happened, which is worse because consumers act on it. Wrapping both in a try/catch and retrying the publish narrows the window but cannot close it — the process can die between them, and the retry itself may succeed after a rollback. There is no ordering of two non-transactional operations that is safe. You need one atomic write, which means the event has to be written into the same transaction as the state.

That is the transactional outbox: the handler writes the business row and an outbox row in one transaction, and a separate relay reads the outbox and publishes to the broker, marking rows sent. The atomicity problem is now solved because the two writes are in one database transaction, and the remaining risk moves to the relay — which can publish a row twice if it crashes after publishing and before marking. That residual duplicate is fine, because it is at-least-once with a stable message id, and consumers already had to be idempotent. Trading "possible loss" for "possible duplicate" is the whole point, and being able to say that sentence is what separates someone who has implemented it from someone who has read about it.

// one transaction, two writes: state and intent are now atomic
using var tx = await db.Database.BeginTransactionAsync(ct);

order.MarkShipped(shipmentId);
db.Outbox.Add(new OutboxMessage
{
    Id          = Guid.CreateVersion7(),      // time-ordered id: also the dedup key downstream
    Topic       = "orders.shipped.v1",
    PartitionKey= $"{order.TenantId}:{order.Id}",
    Payload     = JsonSerializer.SerializeToUtf8Bytes(new OrderShipped(order.Id, shipmentId)),
    OccurredAt  = timeProvider.GetUtcNow()
});

await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);
await tx.CommitAsync(ct);                     // if this fails, neither the state nor the event exists

We had a real choice about the relay, and it is worth naming both options because interviewers probe it. A polling relay queries the outbox table on an interval, publishes, and marks rows sent: simple, no extra infrastructure, and it costs you latency (our interval was 200 ms) plus a steady query load on a hot table. Log-based change data capture — Debezium reading the write-ahead log — has near-zero added latency and no polling load, but it is another distributed system to run, needs replication slots managed carefully (an abandoned slot will fill the disk and take the database down), and couples you to the database's log format. We chose polling for seven services because the latency was acceptable and the operational surface mattered more, and CDC for the two highest-volume ones where the polling query was measurably expensive.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why can the dual write not be fixed with retries?

Because the failure you are trying to survive includes the process dying between the two operations, and a retry loop cannot run after the process is gone. Retrying the publish narrows the window to the length of the retry, but if the pod is killed, the node fails, or the deployment rolls, the committed state has no event and nothing will ever notice. Reversing the order does not help either: publishing first means a rollback leaves an event describing something that never happened, and consumers will already have acted on it. Only a single atomic write covers all interleavings, and since the broker cannot join the database transaction, the event has to be written as data first and forwarded afterwards.

At what scale does the polling relay stop working?

When the outbox query becomes a meaningful load on the primary, which for us started around a few thousand messages/second on the busiest service. The symptoms show up as index bloat and vacuum pressure on Postgres, because you are inserting and updating the same narrow table constantly; the mitigations are to delete rather than mark sent, to partition the table by time and drop partitions, and to keep the query strictly index-covered. Past that, CDC is the right answer because it reads the write-ahead log the database is producing anyway instead of adding a workload. The other, sneakier limit is ordering: a polling relay that publishes rows in id order across multiple workers can reorder within a key unless you shard the relay by partition key, which we had to add for the high-volume services.

What did you trade away?

Latency and a permanent new failure mode. Events now arrive up to a poll interval later than the database write, which is fine for our consumers but would not be for a sub-100 ms path. More importantly the relay is a new thing that can stop: if it dies, the application keeps working perfectly and writes keep committing, so nothing looks wrong while the outbox silently grows and downstream drifts further behind. That is a nastier failure than the one I removed in terms of detectability, which is exactly why the "age of the oldest unsent row" alert is not optional — the outbox pattern without that alert is a trap.

How did you handle the nine services having nine different owners?

I did the first one myself, end to end, including the tests and the dashboard, and then presented it as a package rather than a proposal — a NuGet package, a migration script, a 15-line integration snippet, and a graph showing that service's divergences going to zero. Teams adopt a working thing far faster than they adopt an initiative. For the two teams that pushed back, the objection was capacity rather than disagreement, so I offered to do the change and let them review it, which cost me two days each and removed the argument entirely. The one thing I would not do again is set a deadline for other teams; a visible scoreboard of who had adopted it worked better than any date I could have imposed.

Would you use CDC everywhere if you started again?

No, and the reason is operational rather than technical. Debezium is excellent and the latency is genuinely better, but a replication slot is a loaded weapon: if a connector stops and the slot is not advanced, the database retains write-ahead log segments until the disk fills and the primary goes down — a messaging problem turning into a database outage. Running it well means monitoring slot lag, having a runbook for a stuck connector, and having someone who understands both systems on call. For seven low-volume services, 100 ms of extra latency was a far cheaper price than that. I would default to CDC only where the team already runs Kafka Connect competently.

How did you prove it actually worked?

By keeping the reconciliation job running for a full quarter after the change and watching its output go to zero, rather than deleting it the moment I believed I was done. That is the discipline I would want a team to copy: a compensating control is also your best verification instrument, so switch it from "fixes divergences" to "reports divergences" and only remove it when it has reported nothing for long enough to cover your seasonal peaks. We also injected failures deliberately in staging — killing the relay mid-publish, killing the app between commit and publish — and asserted that the outbox row survived and was eventually delivered exactly once at the sink.

12The retention setting that ate three days

tier · senior data-loss / corruption scare

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A downstream analytics consumer had been paused for five days during a rewrite. When it was switched back on, it started reading and immediately hit OFFSET_OUT_OF_RANGE: its committed offset pointed at a segment that no longer existed, because the topic had a three-day retention and the broker had cheerfully deleted everything older. The client's auto.offset.reset was latest, so it silently jumped to the end of the log and carried on, and for about ninety minutes nobody noticed that three days of events had been skipped rather than consumed. The scare was not the deletion — that was configured behaviour — it was that the default recovery from a missing offset is to lose data quietly.

Understanding this properly means separating two things that look similar. Retention is time- or size-based deletion of whole segments, and it applies to the topic regardless of whether anyone has read it: the broker has no idea your consumer is behind. Compaction is different — it keeps the latest record per key indefinitely, so a compacted topic is a snapshot of current state rather than a history of changes, and a record only disappears when a newer record for that key supersedes it or a tombstone is written and the delete retention passes. We had chosen a three-day time retention for a topic that was, in practice, being used as a source of record by a consumer that could be down for a week. That mismatch between retention and the maximum tolerable consumer downtime is the actual bug.

Recovering was possible only because the data existed elsewhere. The source service still had the underlying rows, so we backfilled by re-publishing the three days from the database into a temporary topic and pointing the analytics consumer at it, which took about six hours including validation. If the topic had been the only copy, the answer would have been "that data is gone", and I want to be blunt about that in an interview because the honest answer to "how did you recover" is sometimes "we got lucky that there was another copy". What I did next was make sure luck was not part of the design.

# the settings that decide whether history survives, per topic
kafka-configs.sh --bootstrap-server $BROKERS --alter --topic orders.events.v1 \
  --add-config retention.ms=1209600000        # 14 days: longer than any tolerable consumer outage

# a state topic wants compaction, not time deletion
kafka-configs.sh --bootstrap-server $BROKERS --alter --topic orders.state.v1 \
  --add-config 'cleanup.policy=compact,min.compaction.lag.ms=3600000,delete.retention.ms=86400000'

# and the client-side setting that turns a gap into a loud failure instead of silent loss
# auto.offset.reset = none   -> the consumer throws instead of skipping to latest

Three changes came out of it. Retention on every topic is now set from an explicit statement of "how long may a consumer be down and still catch up", defaulting to fourteen days rather than three; the storage cost of that change was real but modest, and tiered storage makes long retention much cheaper than it used to be. Consumers set auto.offset.reset=none so an out-of-range offset raises an error and pages someone, rather than silently skipping — latest loses data quietly and earliest can reprocess everything and cause a duplicate storm, and both are worse than stopping. And we added an alert on consumer offset lag measured in time to retention expiry: if a group's oldest unread record is within 48 hours of being deleted, someone gets paged while there is still time to act.

What I would do differently: I would have treated "this consumer is paused for a rewrite" as a change requiring a retention review. It was announced in a stand-up and nobody connected a five-day pause to a three-day retention, because those two facts lived in different people's heads. I now ask one question whenever a consumer is going to be down for an extended period: is it going to be down longer than the topic's retention? It takes ten seconds and it would have prevented this entirely.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you understand that message brokers are not databases and retention is a deletion policy that does not care about your consumers, plus how you behave when data may have been lost. The signal: you established the blast radius before promising anything, you were honest about the role luck played, and your fixes turned silent loss into loud failure.

The mistake on the data-loss archetype is minimising — "we recovered everything, no impact". Interviewers want to know what you would have done if you could not recover. The second mistake is blaming the consumer team; the config was a shared design decision.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just set retention to infinite?

Cost and obligation, in that order. Infinite retention on a high-volume topic is a storage bill that grows forever and a restore time that grows with it, though tiered storage changes that calculus a lot by pushing old segments to object storage at a fraction of broker-disk cost — Kafka 4.3 even added follower fetch from the tiered offset (KIP-1023) to cut the replication cost of that further. The obligation is the sharper problem: if the topic carries personal data, infinite retention means you cannot honour a deletion request without compaction plus tombstones, and "we keep everything forever" is a compliance position rather than a technical default. My rule is retention set from a stated requirement — the longest tolerable consumer outage, or a regulatory period — never from a habit.

What would you have done if there had been no other copy?

Told people immediately and accurately, then worked the problem from partial sources. I would have scoped exactly which events were lost by comparing the deleted offset range against what downstream had processed, established what could be reconstructed from adjacent systems — audit logs, the partner's own records, a derived store that had already consumed the events — and reported the irrecoverable remainder as a number rather than a vague apology. The uncomfortable part of this archetype is being willing to say "three days of analytics events are gone and here is the impact"; every attempt I have seen to soften that has made it worse when the true extent emerged later.

At what point does compaction become the right answer instead of longer retention?

When the consumer needs current state rather than history. A compacted topic keeps the latest record per key indefinitely, so a consumer that was down for a month can rebuild a complete picture of the world by reading from the beginning, at a storage cost proportional to key cardinality rather than to event volume — that is the right tool for reference data, configuration, and entity snapshots. It is the wrong tool where the sequence itself matters, because intermediate states are deliberately discarded, so an audit trail or anything computing deltas needs a retained history. Plenty of systems want both, which is why I often publish a compacted state topic alongside an event topic with a bounded retention.

What did the change to auto.offset.reset=none cost you?

Operational noise, and a real design obligation. A brand-new consumer group has no committed offset, so with none it fails to start until someone explicitly seeks it to the beginning or the end — which caught two teams by surprise in the first month and needed a documented bootstrap step. That is the cost, and I think it is the right one: an explicit action at consumer creation is far cheaper than a silent skip in production. I would not apply it blindly to a consumer group that is genuinely ephemeral and stateless, such as a debug tool, where latest is exactly what you want.

How does the alert know it is nearly too late?

It compares the group's committed offset against the log-start offset rather than the log-end offset, and expresses the gap in time. The usual lag metric tells you how far behind the head you are, which says nothing about deletion risk; what matters is the distance to the tail, because that is where the broker is eating segments. We compute the timestamp of the oldest unread record and alert when it is within 48 hours of the retention boundary, so someone is paged while extending retention is still a viable fix. It is a small piece of tooling and it converts an unrecoverable incident into a boring config change, which is the best ratio of effort to value in this whole story.

Why not one canonical event with all the fields?

Because a canonical schema across bounded contexts is a permanent coupling disguised as a simplification. Every field belongs to someone, so every change needs the other two teams' agreement, and within a year the schema accretes optional fields that only one consumer understands while the others carry them around. It also forces a shared release cadence, which was the specific thing we were trying to escape by going event-driven. Three narrow contracts joined by an identifier cost consumers a join, and buy each team the ability to evolve alone — which, measured over the following year, was nine independent changes that would otherwise have been nine cross-team negotiations.

What if a consumer genuinely needs all three events together?

Then someone owns that composition explicitly, and it is a consumer-side concern rather than a producer-side one. The usual answer is a projection or a stream join that subscribes to the three topics and materialises a combined read model, co-partitioned on the correlation id so the join is local. That composition has its own owner, its own schema, and — importantly — its own choice about how to handle the fact that the three events arrive at different times, which is a real problem the canonical schema would have hidden rather than solved. Making the composition explicit means someone has to decide what "the order is complete" means, which is exactly the decision that was being avoided.

At what point does the correlation-id join break down?

When the events arrive far enough apart that the join needs unbounded state. A stream join keeps a window of one side waiting for the other, and if fulfilment can happen three weeks after the sale, an in-memory join window is not viable — you need a durable state store or a database-backed projection, which is fine but is a different engineering exercise. The other break is cardinality: one sales order producing hundreds of fulfilment events makes the join fan out and the read model expensive. I would size the window from the observed distribution of the time gap, not from an assumption, and I would set an explicit policy for what happens to an unmatched event past the window rather than letting it disappear.

What did you trade away?

Simplicity for consumers, honestly. Anyone who wants a whole-order view now has to subscribe to three topics and join them, where the canonical schema would have given them one message — and two downstream teams told me so, with justification. I judged that a cost worth paying because it falls on the consumer who needs the composition rather than on all three producing teams forever, and because it is a cost that can be paid once by building a shared projection. I also gave up the ability to enforce a global invariant across the three contexts at publish time; those now have to be checked by a reconciliation, which is weaker.

How did you get three teams to agree to this when they could not agree before?

By changing what they had to agree on. The six weeks had stalled because agreement required each team to accept someone else's model of their own domain, which is a very hard thing to ask; the split required them to agree only on an identifier and a compatibility rule, which is nearly free. I also gave each team something they wanted — full ownership of their own event, and the ability to change it without a meeting — so the proposal read as a gain rather than a compromise. The lesson I took is that when a group is stuck, the problem is often the shape of the question rather than the willingness of the people.

That is the realistic situation for this archetype: you cannot change the other side, so the question becomes what guarantee you can construct from your side alone. The concept worth teaching here is that when the receiver is not idempotent, the sender has to become exactly-once-effective by adding a layer that turns retries into queries — you cannot make their API safe, but you can stop yourself from calling it twice for the same fact.

What we built was a delivery record plus a reconciliation. Before calling the partner, we wrote a row keyed by our order id with status pending and a generated client reference that we embedded in a field the partner did echo back on their shipment object. If a call timed out, we did not retry blindly: we first queried the partner's list endpoint for a shipment carrying our client reference, and only sent the request if it was genuinely absent. That turns an ambiguous timeout into a determinable state, which is the general technique for talking to a non-idempotent API — the search-before-retry pattern. Duplicate shipments went from roughly one in four thousand to two in the following six months, both from a genuine partner-side edge case they later fixed.

// talking to a non-idempotent API: never blind-retry, always resolve the ambiguity first
var reference = $"ord-{order.Id:N}";              // our key, echoed back by their shipment object

try
{

    await deliveries.MarkSentAsync(order.Id, shipment.Id, ct);
}
catch (Exception ex) when (ex is TimeoutException or HttpRequestException)
{
    // ambiguous: it may or may not have been created. Ask, do not guess.
    var existing = await partner.FindShipmentByReferenceAsync(reference, ct);
    if (existing is not null)
        await deliveries.MarkSentAsync(order.Id, existing.Id, ct);   // it worked; do not resend
    else
        throw;                                                       // genuinely absent: safe to retry
}

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just stop retrying?

Because the failure mode swaps from duplicate shipments to missing ones, and a parcel that never ships is worse for the customer than one that ships twice. Timeouts happen for transient reasons constantly — a slow partner, a network blip, a deploy on their side — and without retries every one of those becomes an order that silently never reaches logistics. Given a choice between a defect I can detect and reverse (a duplicate shipment shows up in their list endpoint) and one that is invisible until a customer complains, I will take the detectable one every time. The search-before-retry approach gets both: we retry, and we do not duplicate.

What if their list endpoint were also unreliable, or eventually consistent?

Then the check becomes probabilistic and I would have to design for a wrong answer. If the endpoint lags behind writes, a search immediately after a timeout can report "absent" for something that exists, and we would duplicate anyway — so the mitigation is to wait past their consistency window before searching, and to make the ambiguous case a human queue rather than an automatic retry once the search has been inconclusive twice. That is slower and it is the honest design: when you genuinely cannot determine the state of a remote system, the correct behaviour is to stop and ask a person, not to pick an outcome and hope. I would also push much harder for the idempotency key, because at that point there is no clean engineering answer on our side alone.

At what volume does search-before-retry stop being viable?

It scales with the failure rate rather than with total traffic, which is what makes it affordable — we only searched on the ambiguous path, so at a 0.1% timeout rate the extra calls were negligible. It breaks down when the partner is broadly degraded, because then every call times out, every one triggers a search against the same struggling service, and you have built an amplifier. The guard is a circuit breaker: once the failure rate crosses a threshold, stop calling entirely, queue the work, and drain when they recover. That was a genuine gap in our first version and we added it after a partner outage where our retries and searches roughly tripled the load we were putting on them.

What did you trade away?

Latency on the failure path and a piece of code coupled to their API shape. The search adds a round trip exactly when things are already slow, which pushed our p99 on the failure path to several seconds — acceptable for an asynchronous shipment creation, unacceptable if a user were waiting. And the client-reference trick depends on a specific field of theirs being echoed back and searchable, so it is fragile to their API evolving; I documented that dependency prominently and added a contract test that runs against their sandbox nightly, so a change breaks a build rather than production.

How did you keep the partner relationship healthy while reporting their defects monthly?

By making the report useful to them rather than accusatory. It was a short note with counts, specific request ids they could look up, and no adjectives — no "this is unacceptable", no escalation to their management, and an explicit acknowledgement that we had built a workaround so it was not currently hurting customers. Their engineers could take that straight into their own prioritisation conversation, which is what eventually happened. I have found that giving another team the evidence to win an internal argument is far more effective than pressure, because their constraint is usually prioritisation rather than unwillingness.

I spent two days breaking the bill into components before proposing anything, and that was the single highest-value part of the work: about 45% was cross-availability-zone network transfer, 30% broker disk, 15% compute, and the rest managed-service overhead. Almost everyone's instinct, including mine initially, was that storage was the problem; it was not, and I would have optimised the wrong thing.

Cross-AZ traffic dominated because of how replication and consumption work. A produce to a partition goes to the leader, which then replicates to followers in other zones — so every message crosses zone boundaries twice at a replication factor of three — and consumers historically fetched only from the leader, adding another crossing whenever the consumer happened to be in a different zone from the leader. Understanding that the bill is driven by the replication topology rather than by message count is the concept here, and it is why "send fewer messages" is usually the weakest lever available.

Four changes, in order of what they returned. Enabling zstd compression at the producer cut bytes on the wire and on disk by about 68% for our JSON-heavy payloads, and because Kafka stores the compressed batch as-is, that saving applies to replication traffic, disk and consumer fetches simultaneously — one config change, roughly a third off the total bill. Enabling follower fetching so consumers read from a replica in their own availability zone removed most of the remaining consumer-side cross-zone traffic. Raising linger.ms from 0 to 20 improved batching substantially, which compounds with compression because compression works on batches. And finally we moved cold data to tiered storage, which pushes older segments to object storage at a fraction of broker disk cost, and on Kafka 4.3 the new follower fetch from the tiered offset (KIP-1023) means a rebuilding follower can pull historical data from the tier rather than streaming it across zones from the leader — which is exactly the expensive path we had been paying for during every broker replacement.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not reduce the replication factor to save money?

Because replication factor is a durability decision, not a cost lever, and trading it for money is exactly the kind of saving that returns as an outage. At RF=3 with min.insync.replicas=2 you survive one broker or one zone loss with acknowledged writes intact; at RF=2 a single failure during a rolling restart can lose acknowledged data, and rolling restarts happen monthly. If cost pressure were extreme I would rather reduce retention, move cold data to a tier, or compress harder — all of which degrade convenience rather than durability. The only version of this I would entertain is a lower replication factor on genuinely reproducible topics, such as a derived stream that can be rebuilt from its source, and I would label the topic so nobody later treats it as authoritative.

What did compression cost you?

Producer CPU, about 8% on average, and a small latency increase per batch — zstd is a good default because its ratio is strong at modest CPU, but the right choice depends on payload shape and I measured rather than assumed. We tested lz4 and gzip on real payloads: lz4 was faster but gave roughly half the ratio, gzip compressed similarly to zstd at noticeably more CPU. The subtle cost worth knowing is that if a broker has to recompress — which happens when the topic's compression type differs from the producer's — you pay CPU on the broker instead, so I set the topic to producer so batches are stored exactly as sent and passed through to consumers untouched.

At what scale do these savings stop mattering?

They matter more as you grow, not less, because every lever here is proportional to bytes. What changes at larger scale is which lever dominates: at very high volume, cross-zone replication traffic becomes so large that people start considering single-zone clusters with cross-zone disaster recovery instead of stretched clusters, which is a fundamentally different availability posture and a much bigger decision than a config change. The place these savings genuinely stop mattering is a small cluster where the managed-service base cost dominates the marginal byte cost — there, the only real lever is fewer clusters, and consolidating environments is a political project rather than a technical one.

How did you avoid breaking anything with follower fetching?

By understanding what it actually changes and rolling it out per consumer group. Fetching from a follower means reading from a replica that may be slightly behind the leader, and the broker only serves up to the high watermark, so you never read unreplicated data — but a consumer that assumed leader-level freshness could see a few extra milliseconds of staleness. I checked with each consumer team whether that mattered, found one — a near-real-time fraud check — for which we left leader fetching in place, and enabled it everywhere else. Rolling it out group by group with rack awareness configured correctly took a week, and the one thing I would warn about is that a misconfigured rack id silently gives you no saving at all, so verify the traffic actually moved rather than trusting the config.

How did you get teams to care about cost without mandating anything?

I gave them a number attached to their own name and then said nothing. The monthly per-topic estimate went to each owning team with no target and no ask, and within two months three teams had cut retention on topics they realised were storing debug output for thirty days. Mandates produce compliance theatre and resentment; visibility produces judgment, because engineers generally do the reasonable thing once they can see the consequence of the unreasonable one. The one thing I did add was a cost estimate field in the topic-creation template, so the decision is priced at the moment it is made rather than discovered a year later.

The mechanism is simpler than people expect and worth being able to explain precisely. A trace context is a small piece of data — a trace id, a span id and flags, standardised as W3C traceparent — that has to travel with the work. For HTTP it rides in a header; for messaging it rides in message headers, which every modern broker supports (Kafka record headers, AMQP 1.0 application properties, Service Bus message properties). The producer injects the current context into the message; the consumer extracts it and starts its span as a child, linked to the producer's. The subtlety, and the thing that makes messaging tracing different, is that a message can sit in a queue for hours and can be consumed by many groups, so the consumer span is usually modelled as a link from the producing span rather than a straightforward parent-child nesting — otherwise a single producer span appears to last four hours and fan out into hundreds of children.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why links instead of parent-child spans for the consumer?

Because messaging breaks the assumptions parent-child nesting is built on. A parent span is expected to enclose its children in time, but a message can sit in a queue for hours, so nesting makes the producer span appear to last as long as the slowest consumer and destroys any useful latency reading. Fan-out makes it worse: one message consumed by six groups would give one parent six children in unrelated services and traces. Links express the real relationship — "this work was caused by that message" — while letting the consumer span be its own trace with its own timing. I do use parent-child where the messaging hop is genuinely a synchronous request-reply, because there the enclosure is real.

At what volume does tracing become too expensive?

Much sooner than people expect on a busy topic, because the cost is per span and a message crossing five services generates ten or more. At 100% sampling on a 20,000 messages/second topic you are producing hundreds of thousands of spans a second, and the backend bill dwarfs the messaging bill it was meant to explain. The answer is tail-based sampling in the collector: buffer the spans of a trace, and keep it if it contains an error or exceeds a latency threshold, plus a small baseline percentage of healthy traces for aggregate statistics. That keeps every interesting trace and discards the boring bulk, which is the opposite of head-based sampling where you decide before you know whether anything went wrong.

What did you trade away?

Message size and a small latency cost, plus an ongoing dependency on a convention that is still moving. Trace headers add on the order of a hundred bytes per message, which is negligible for a 4 KB payload and distinctly not negligible for a 200-byte one at high volume — worth checking rather than assuming. The convention instability is the more annoying trade: because the OpenTelemetry messaging semantic conventions are still in development, a library upgrade can rename the attributes my dashboards query, so I pinned with the stability opt-in and accepted that we will have a migration to do when they stabilise. That is a known, scheduled cost rather than a surprise, which is the best you can do with a moving spec.

I did not push, because a partially-instrumented estate still gives value for the flows that are instrumented, and forcing it produces box-ticking rather than useful spans. The same thing: the single flow that generates the most support questions, not the most traffic. The page said consumer lag on four groups, and the graph was the strangest shape I have seen at 02:40: lag climbing in a staircase, with brief flat sections. Consumers were alive, brokers were healthy, no deploy had happened. What was actually happening was that the groups were rebalancing continuously — each rebalance revoked partitions, paused all consumption, reassigned, resumed for maybe twenty seconds, and then rebalanced again. Throughput was not slow; it was repeatedly being reset to zero. A rebalance storm looks like a performance problem and is actually an availability problem.

The mechanism is worth knowing cold, because it is one of the most common Kafka production issues. A consumer stays in its group by sending heartbeats within session.timeout.ms and by calling poll again within max.poll.interval.ms. Miss either and the coordinator declares it dead and rebalances the group. Under the classic eager protocol, a rebalance stops the world for every member, not just the failed one — so one flapping consumer can pause an entire group repeatedly. Our trigger was a node-pool scale-down: the cluster autoscaler was evicting and rescheduling pods every couple of minutes during a low-traffic window, and each eviction triggered a rebalance that took long enough to push the next consumer past its timeout, which triggered another. It was self-sustaining, which is why it did not resolve on its own.

Mitigation at 02:47 was blunt and correct for the hour: I disabled the autoscaler on that node pool, which stopped the evictions and let the groups settle within one rebalance cycle. Lag drained in about eleven minutes. I deliberately did not start tuning timeouts at three in the morning — changing session.timeout.ms under pressure, on four groups, with no ability to test, is how a bad night becomes a bad week. Stop the trigger, restore service, then think. I wrote what I had done in the incident channel as I did it, which mattered later because the autoscaler being off had to be remembered and reversed.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just raise the session timeout at 02:47?

Because it is a change with a delayed and asymmetric downside, applied under the worst possible conditions for judgment. A longer session timeout means a genuinely dead consumer is detected later, so the partitions it owned sit unconsumed for that much longer — I would be trading a loud problem I understood for a quiet one I might not notice for weeks. It also would not have stopped the evictions, which were the actual driver, so I would have been treating a symptom while the cause kept firing. Disabling the autoscaler was reversible in one command, addressed the trigger directly, and had a blast radius I could fully predict at 3am, which is the property I care about most in a mitigation.

At what point does static membership become a liability?

When a member dies for real rather than restarting. Static membership deliberately suppresses the rebalance on a restart, so if an instance is genuinely gone, its partitions stay unassigned until the session timeout expires — with a long timeout that is a real availability gap for those partitions. It also requires the group.instance.id to be stable and unique per replica, which means a StatefulSet or equivalent rather than a Deployment with random pod names, and a duplicate id causes members to fence each other in confusing ways. I use it where restarts are frequent and planned, keep the session timeout tuned so a true failure is still caught within an acceptable window, and alert on partitions with no owner.

What did the new consumer protocol actually change for you?

It removed the stop-the-world property, which was the thing that made the storm self-sustaining. Under the classic eager protocol every member revokes all its partitions and waits for the whole group to re-join, so the cost of one flapping member is paid by everybody, and a slow rebalance can push other members past their own deadlines — a positive feedback loop. KIP-848, which is the default from Kafka 4.0, moves assignment to the broker-side coordinator and makes it incremental, so members keep the partitions that are not being moved and the group does not pause. Our rebalance pause time dropped by about 95%, but I would be careful not to attribute all of that to the protocol; static membership and the disruption budget did much of the work by reducing how often rebalances happened at all.

What did you trade away with the mitigation?

Cost and elasticity, temporarily. With the autoscaler off, the node pool stayed at peak size for four days until the permanent fixes landed, which was real money and also meant we lost the automatic capacity response if traffic had spiked. I flagged that explicitly in the handover note rather than leaving it as an undocumented change — the failure mode I actively fear with night mitigations is that they become permanent by forgetting, and six months later someone finds an autoscaler disabled with no explanation. I set a calendar reminder and re-enabled it myself once static membership was deployed and verified.

How did you handle the team who had moved your consumers onto that pool?

Without any blame, because their change was reasonable and the incompatibility was not documented anywhere they could have found it. The general lesson I took is that in a distributed system the dangerous changes are often made by people who cannot see the consequence — so the fix is to make the constraint visible in their tooling, not to ask them to remember something about my service.

What was built, the latency it achieved, the effort compared with the original ask, and what the saved capacity went to instead.
Differently
Go to the users in round one; when a request feels disproportionate, investigate the requirement instead of debating the request.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

When did you talk someone out of what they asked for?

Hint: real-time, microservices, multi-region, a rewrite, a new database, machine learning. What was the underlying need, and how did you find it?

How do you express technical cost to someone non-technical?

Hint: in what you will not build instead, in ongoing operational burden, in the on-call rota, in time-to-first-value. Which framing has actually landed for you?

When did you say yes to something expensive because the case was genuinely good?

Hint: important balance for this archetype — you need a story where you did not push back, or you look like a blocker. What convinced you?

Have you been overruled, and what did you do next?

Hint: disagree-and-commit is the expected behaviour and it is worth having a real example. Did you write down your concern? Did the outcome vindicate you or not — and can you say so without gloating?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

What if the stakeholder had insisted on the full real-time version?

I would have built it, and I would have made sure the decision and its cost were written down — not as a defensive move but because the trade-off deserved to be a visible choice rather than a verbal one. My job at that point is to give the best possible information and then execute well; continuing to fight after the person who owns the priority has decided is how engineering loses influence rather than gains it. I would have asked for one thing in exchange: agreement on what we were not doing that quarter, because "yes and also everything else" is the version that actually fails.

Why is the alert better than a real-time dashboard, technically?

Because a dashboard requires a human to be looking at it, which makes detection latency a function of attention rather than of the system. A streaming alert on a rolling failure rate detects in under thirty seconds at three in the morning with nobody watching, and it delivers the signal to the person who can act. It is also enormously simpler: one small consumer with a windowed counter, no serving store, no reconciliation with the warehouse, and no need to solve the genuinely hard part of streaming aggregation — late and out-of-order events, which a dashboard showing a number to a human must handle correctly or it will quietly mislead them.

At what point would the full streaming version be justified?

When the value of the number depends on its freshness in a way a threshold cannot capture — live pricing, fraud scoring, capacity or bidding decisions where a human or a system acts on the current value continuously. It is also justified when there are many such numbers, because at that point you are building a platform once rather than a bespoke alert twenty times, and the fixed cost of the stream processor amortises. What does not justify it is a preference for seeing a fresher number on a screen, which is what most "real-time" requests turn out to be. I would want a named decision that the freshness changes before I would spend a quarter on it.

What did you trade away?

Generality. The alert solves exactly one question, and when a second and third question arrived over the following year we built two more bespoke consumers rather than a platform — by the third one the incremental cost was starting to argue for the general solution, and I said so. That is the honest downside of the cheap option: it is cheap per instance and it does not compose. I flagged it at the time as a decision to revisit after the third use case, which is roughly where we ended up, and revisiting from a position of three known requirements is a much better place to design a platform from than one hypothetical one.

How did you keep the relationship good while declining?

By never actually declining. I gave him a better outcome for his real problem, a real price for the thing he had asked for, and the decision itself — at no point was I the obstacle. I also did the cheap option fast, which matters more than any conversational technique: shipping something useful in four days buys more trust than a well-argued no. And I fed back what I had learned from the operations team, so he understood his own users' need better than before, which is the part that made him treat me as an ally in later planning rather than as a cost centre to be negotiated with.

21The big behavioural Q&A

22 prompts 5 themes cross-cutting

These are the prompts that are not tied to one incident — the ones that ask about your judgment, your values and how you operate. Each answer names which of the twenty stories to deploy and what the interviewer is listening for. Deploying a story you have already pressure-tested is far stronger than improvising, and knowing which one to reach for is most of the skill.

Failure & ownership

Tell me about a time you caused a production incident.

Deploy: story 06 (duplicates) or 12 (retention), both of which are your own design decision biting you. They want: unambiguous ownership without self-flagellation, a clear technical explanation of the mechanism, and a systemic fix rather than "I will be more careful". Say "I chose auto-commit because it was the default and I had not thought about async handlers" — the specificity is what makes it credible. Finish on what is different now: the alert, the review question, the test. Avoid passive voice; "a bug was introduced" is the phrase interviewers listen for.

What is the biggest mistake of your career?

Deploy: story 15 (the cancelled mesh) — four months of your own design, killed by you. They want: a mistake with real cost, owned without deflection, and evidence that your process changed. The trap is choosing something small and safe; at lead level a "mistake" that cost a day reads as an unwillingness to answer. The other trap is a mistake that was really someone else's. End with the concrete process change — in this case, refusing to start a resilience project without a written RTO and RPO.

Tell me about a time you lost or nearly lost data.

Deploy: story 12 (retention) and, if pushed for a second, 09 (dual write). They want: honesty about the extent, calm behaviour before the extent was known, and the distinction between what you fixed and what you were lucky about. Say the words "we got lucky that the source data still existed" if that is true — candidates who claim a clean recovery every time are not believed. Then say what makes luck unnecessary now: the alert on time-to-retention-expiry and auto.offset.reset=none.

When did you realise you were wrong about something important?

Deploy: story 02 (log vs. queue — the document worker you put on the wrong transport) or 05 (where your colleague was right about transactions). They want: the specific evidence that changed your mind and how fast you acted on it. The strongest version includes a time cost you paid for being slow to concede. Naming what the other person was right about, by name and in public, is a differentiator — most candidates describe changing their mind in a way that still centres their own correctness.

How do you handle being on call?

Deploy: story 18 (rebalance storm) and 03 (poison message). They want: a philosophy, not war stories — stabilise before optimising, prefer the reversible mitigation, narrate as you go, defer risky tuning to daylight, and treat every page as feedback about the alerting. Mention what you do about pages that should not have fired, because the strongest on-call answer is about reducing the volume of pages rather than about surviving them heroically.

Conflict & influence

Tell me about a technical disagreement with a colleague.

Deploy: story 05 (exactly-once vs. idempotency). They want: disagreement handled as a shared investigation rather than a contest, and an outcome better than either starting position. Include the mechanism that broke the deadlock — moving it out of chat into a diagram, prototyping to get a number. Do not resolve it by seniority, and do not tell a story where you simply won; both are read as collaboration risk.

How do you influence people you have no authority over?

Deploy: story 14 (the partner with no idempotency key) and 09 (rolling the outbox across nine teams). They want: data over pressure, making adoption cheap, and giving the other party what they need to win their own internal argument. The monthly duplicate report and the "I did the first one and packaged it" move are both concrete and repeatable. Avoid any story where influence meant escalating to a manager.

Tell me about a time you pushed back on your manager or a senior stakeholder.

Deploy: story 20 ("just make it real-time"). They want: pushback that includes a genuine path to yes and a translation of technical cost into prioritisation terms. Emphasise that you investigated the requirement rather than debating the request, and that you gave the decision to the person who owned it. Interviewers are frequently stakeholders themselves, so any hint that the requester was foolish is expensive.

How do you get alignment across teams that disagree?

Deploy: story 13 (three definitions of "order") and 07 (getting four consumer teams to change topics). They want: the insight that stuck groups usually need the question reshaped rather than more persuasion. Naming the real disagreement out loud, and reducing what people must agree on to the minimum, are both transferable techniques. Mention what you gave each team in return — ownership, or a benefit that was theirs.

Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news.

Deploy: story 15 (cancelling the project) or 08 (escalating the security exposure). They want: the sequencing — in person before writing, taking responsibility yourself, being factual about what you know and do not know, and following through. Include the human cost honestly; a cancellation where nobody was disappointed did not happen. For the security one, emphasise escalating rather than quietly fixing.

Describe a time you received difficult feedback.

Deploy: the over-correction in story 10, where your guidance about events versus commands became dogma and someone told you so. They want: that you can hear it, that you changed behaviour, and that the change was visible to the person who gave the feedback. Publicly walking back your own rule is a strong, specific example. Avoid the humble-brag shape ("I was told I care too much"), which interviewers spot immediately.

Leadership & mentoring

How do you level up the engineers around you?

Deploy: story 10 (teaching the events-versus-commands distinction). They want: mechanisms that work without you — a rotating design review, a checklist, a naming rule, handing the teaching to someone else. Name a specific person and a specific capability they now have. The closing evidence is what happened after you stopped pushing; anything else is an assertion.

Tell me about a time you raised the technical bar for a team.

Deploy: story 19 (making the tests trustworthy) or 09 (the outbox library). They want: the leverage move — making the right thing the easy thing, rather than reviewing harder. A shared library, a template, a default, a test that encodes a guarantee. Mention the resistance you met and how you handled it, because a bar-raising story with no friction is not believable.

How do you make architectural decisions with a team rather than for them?

Deploy: story 02 (log vs. queue). They want: a decision process — reducing the debate to workload questions people can answer with evidence, writing the ADR including the rejected option, and asking the loudest dissenter to review it. Also that you know when to just decide; consensus-seeking past the point of diminishing returns is its own failure, and saying so shows judgment.

What does technical leadership mean to you when you are not the manager?

Deploy: the pattern across stories 09, 10 and 15. They want: influence through artefacts and demonstration rather than authority — do the first one yourself, package it, make the constraint visible in someone else's tooling, and be willing to stop your own project. Keep it concrete; this question invites a philosophy answer and the strongest response is three examples with outcomes.

How do you handle an engineer who is producing designs you disagree with?

Deploy: story 10 (the engineer who had built the command-shaped flows). They want: critique of the pattern rather than the person, public acknowledgement that the original decision was reasonable, and a signal of confidence afterwards — giving them the next piece of the work. Interviewers are alert to any hint that you took over; describe what you delegated rather than what you corrected.

Ambiguity & prioritisation

Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without enough information.

Deploy: story 13 (events nobody could define) or 03 (skipping the poison record). They want: a decision made while it was still unclear, with a stated cost and a plan for being wrong. Say what would have changed your mind and what the reversal would have cost. The failure mode is a story where someone else eventually clarified the requirement and you executed.

How do you prioritise when everything is urgent?

Deploy: story 11 (cutting the event model) and 16 (decomposing the bill before optimising). They want: a principle you actually apply — cost-to-change, reversible versus one-way, measure before optimising. The strongest version has hindsight evidence: the irreversible decisions held up and the deferred ones were genuinely deferrable. Mention how you communicate the deprioritised work so it does not silently vanish.

Tell me about a time you had to hit a hard deadline.

Deploy: story 11. They want: scope cut rather than quality cut or heroics. Be explicit that you cut architecture, not rigour, and that the deferred items had owners and dates — and say whether they were actually paid. Any mention of weekends or long hours as the mechanism will be read as an inability to prioritise.

How do you decide what not to build?

Deploy: story 20 (real-time) and 15 (active-active). They want: the habit of finding the decision behind the request, and the discipline of demanding a number behind an adjective. "Highly available" and "real-time" are both places where a design can be four times more expensive than the requirement. Give the cheap alternative you built instead and what it achieved.

Values, judgment & motivation

Tell me about a time you did the right thing when it was inconvenient.

Deploy: story 08 (escalating the ACL exposure rather than quietly fixing it). They want: that you escalate access and data findings rather than deciding alone, and that you understand why that boundary exists. Be precise that it was an exposure and not a breach — overstating is as damaging as understating. The follow-through (turning the policy into a build failure) is what makes it more than a compliance anecdote.

What kind of problems do you want to work on, and why distributed systems?

Deploy: your own genuine answer, anchored to two stories that show the taste you are describing. They want: a motivation that predicts your behaviour rather than a slogan. A good honest shape: you like problems where the failure modes are emergent rather than local — where the bug is in the interaction and not in any one service — and where correctness has to be designed rather than tested in. Point at story 06 (the bug that only existed during rebalances) and story 09 (a guarantee you have to construct) as the two things you find genuinely interesting, and be ready for the follow-up about what you find tedious.

22Night-before cheat sheet

10 lines read this last

Ten speakable rules for delivering these stories well. Read them the night before and once more in the car; they are about delivery, and delivery is where prepared candidates most often lose points.

  1. Lead with one line of situation, then stop. "A consumer group fell four hours behind every weekday at peak." That is enough context for anyone to follow. Candidates who spend ninety seconds on company background have lost the room before the interesting part.
  2. Quantify the result, and quantify the problem. Two numbers and a duration: 2M lag to 40K, draining in four minutes, held for a quarter. A story without a number is an opinion, and in messaging the numbers are always available — lag, rate, p99, duplicate count, cost.
  3. Say "lag was X while throughput was Y", never lag alone. The single fastest way to sound like you have actually run a consumer group is to pair a level with a rate. The same applies to queue depth, backlog and any other accumulating number.
  4. Own the "what I would do differently" before they ask. Every story here ends with one. Volunteering it reads as confidence; being extracted from it reads as defensiveness. Make it specific and structural — an alert, a review question, a default — never "I would communicate more".
  5. Separate "I" from "we" deliberately and out loud. "The team built the retry ladder; I made the call to skip the record and I wrote the classification logic." Interviewers are trained to probe vague plurals, and clean attribution makes everything else you say more credible.
  6. Name the guarantee you traded. Ordering for throughput, latency for cost, durability for simplicity, generality for speed. Every distributed-systems decision gives something up, and a candidate who names it unprompted is instantly a level above one who describes a free win.
  7. Explain the mechanism, not the tool. "A single record that cannot be processed blocks everything behind it on that partition" beats "we had a Kafka issue". The mechanism is what proves you understand it; the tool name proves only that you were nearby.
  8. Say the version and the date when it matters. "Share groups went production-ready in Kafka 4.2", "the new consumer rebalance protocol has been the default since 4.0", "RabbitMQ 4.2 made Khepri the default metadata store". One or two of these, used where they are relevant, signal that you are current — a dozen of them sounds rehearsed.
  9. For an incident, give the decision before the diagnosis. "I chose to skip the record and repair it manually rather than hotfix under pressure" is the sentence they are waiting for. Diagnosis shows competence; the decision under a bad set of options shows judgment, and judgment is what is being levelled.
  10. End every story on the durable change. The alert that now exists, the test that now runs, the person who now makes that call without you. The last thing you say is the thing they write down, so make it the evidence that you leave systems better than you found them.
