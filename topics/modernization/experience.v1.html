How to read the tier chip. It is the altitude the answer is pitched at, not the seniority of whoever lived it. A Mid answer finds the bug and explains the mechanism correctly. A Senior answer fixes it and proves the fix held. A Staff answer changes the system so the whole class of problem cannot recur, and gets other teams to adopt the change. A Principal answer changes what the organisation spends the next year on, and defends that to people who will never read the code. Most stories below can be told at two adjacent tiers — the drill chain is where the altitude actually shows.

The one sentence that makes a modernization story credible. Interviewers hear "we migrated from .NET Framework to .NET" a hundred times a year and it tells them nothing. What separates the people who did it from the people who watched it: they can say how the old and the new ran at the same time. Shared session state, shared authentication cookies, a routing layer, a dual-write, a read-repair job, a kill switch. If your story has no coexistence mechanism in it, it was either tiny or it was a big-bang rewrite, and both of those are worth saying out loud rather than hiding.

01Strangling a WebForms monolith with a proxy

Staff Migration / version upgrade

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The system was a fourteen-year-old .NET Framework 4.8 application: about 180 WebForms pages, a later MVC 5 area bolted onto the same project, one 900-table SQL Server database, and roughly 40 developers who all had to keep shipping while we moved. The first plan on the table was the one everyone reaches for — a parallel rewrite, ship it in a year, switch DNS. I argued against it and the argument I used was not aesthetic, it was arithmetic: the team was landing about 25 user-visible changes a month into the old app, and a rewrite means either freezing those or building them twice. We went with a strangler fig instead: a new ASP.NET Core app on .NET 10 sitting in front of the old one, taking routes over one at a time, with the legacy app still serving everything it had not lost yet.

The mechanism is the part interviewers care about, so it is worth being exact. YARP ran inside the new .NET 10 app as a catch-all fallback: anything the new app had no endpoint for was proxied to the old IIS site. That gave us one hostname, one TLS certificate, one set of cookies, and — critically — the ability to move a single URL by adding a route rather than by coordinating a release. The hard part was never routing, it was shared state. WebForms leaned on Session for a shopping-basket-shaped object and on FormsAuthentication for identity, and neither survives a process boundary. We used System.Web adapters, which give the new app a System.Web.HttpContext-shaped surface plus a remote app channel: the ASP.NET Core side calls back into the framework app over a shared-secret HTTP endpoint to read and write session and to resolve the authenticated user. It is slower than in-process session — every session-touching request on the new side pays a round trip and a session lock — so we annotated new endpoints as read-only session wherever we could, and made "stop touching Session" the first refactor on every page we moved.

We moved routes in dependency order, cheapest first, and kept a public burn-down of "routes still proxied". Fourteen months in we were at 70% of traffic on .NET 10, and then the migration stalled for a quarter, because the remaining 30% was the reporting area that nobody understood and one page that generated Word documents through Office interop. That stall is the honest part of this story and the part I would change: I let the burn-down chart imply linearity when the work was obviously not linear, and I should have split the plan into "routes" and "the four things that are actually hard" from day one, sized separately, so the stall was a scheduled event rather than a surprise to the steering group. What I would keep: the fallback proxy meant we were never more than one deployment from serving a page the old way, and we used that escape hatch twice in production without a customer noticing.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you have actually run a long migration or only read about one. The signals they want: that you chose incremental for a reason you can quantify, that you can name the coexistence mechanism precisely (session, auth, routing, transactions), that you had a rollback at every step, and that you know where the plan stalled. The common failures: narrating two years of programme with no single decision in it; saying "strangler pattern" as a synonym for "we did it gradually" without a proxy or a seam; claiming the whole migration as yours when 40 people shipped it; and having no answer for "what did you do about session state?"

(c) Recall prompts

What is the oldest thing still running in a system you own, and what keeps it alive?

Hint: look for a Windows-only dependency (Office interop, a COM component, System.Drawing, a printer driver), a licensed third-party control suite, an SSRS or Crystal report server, a scheduled task on a named server, or a database feature nobody can reproduce elsewhere. Whatever answer comes to mind first is usually the thing that made your migration non-linear.

When did you last argue incremental against big-bang — and which side were you on?

Hint: the strong version of this memory includes the arithmetic you used, not the principle. Change rate times duration, cost of double implementation, the size of the feature freeze someone was quietly proposing. It is also fine to remember a time you argued for the rewrite and were right, because the app was small or the domain was being replaced.

Have you ever had two versions of the same system serving live traffic at once? How did they share state?

Hint: shared cookie with a common data-protection key ring, sticky sessions, a session store both sides can read (SQL Server or Redis), a remote-session channel, a shared identity provider, dual writes to one database. If the answer is "they didn't share anything", that is also a story — say what broke.

What did you migrate first, and would you pick the same thing again?

Hint: the two defensible strategies are "lowest risk, to prove the machinery" (a static or read-only page) and "highest pain, to prove the value" (the slowest or most-changed area). Naming which one you chose and why is a stronger answer than either choice on its own.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why a proxy in front rather than just multi-targeting the projects and flipping when everything compiles?

Because compiling is not the hard part in a web app — System.Web is. Multi-targeting works beautifully for class libraries and is exactly what I did for the domain and data layers, but a WebForms page has no target on the other side; there is no netstandard version of a page lifecycle. The proxy buys the one thing multi-targeting cannot: partial delivery. Each route that moves is independently deployable and independently revertable, so the risk is spread across 180 small changes rather than concentrated in one flip. The cost is that you run two stacks and two pipelines for a year or more, and you have to be honest that the coexistence code itself is throwaway work.

Remote session over HTTP on every request — at what scale does that break?

Sooner than people expect, and the limit is not bandwidth, it is the session lock. ASP.NET session is exclusive per session id, so a page that fires several concurrent XHRs serialises them, and now each acquisition is a network round trip instead of an in-process lock. On our traffic — around 300 requests a second at peak, of which maybe a third touched session — we saw the p95 on migrated endpoints go up by roughly 15 ms, which was acceptable. It would not have been at ten times that volume. The mitigations, in the order I would apply them: declare endpoints read-only session so they take a shared lock, stop putting mutable domain objects in session at all, and if you still need it, move both apps onto a shared distributed session store so neither is calling the other. The strategic answer is that the remote channel is a migration crutch and should be measured with a deletion date.

How did you keep 40 developers from stalling while the architecture changed underneath them?

Three things, and the first one matters most: a rule that new work lands in the new stack unless someone can say why not, so the migration was fed by feature work rather than competing with it. Second, I made the seam boring — one shared library with the auth, logging, configuration and data-access wiring already done, so moving a page was a mechanical task a mid-level engineer could finish in a day, not an architecture exercise. Third, a weekly clinic where anyone stuck on a migration could bring the file; that surfaced the systemic blockers early, which is how we found out that half the pages depended on one 4,000-line Utils class. Where I got it wrong: for the first two months I reviewed every migration PR myself, which made me the bottleneck and taught nobody. Handing that to three other people, with a written checklist, was the change that actually scaled it.

What did you trade away by keeping the old app alive for fourteen months?

Cost and cognitive load, mostly. We paid for two hosting environments, two CI pipelines, two dependency-scanning surfaces, and a security posture that was only as good as the older half. Every developer had to hold two mental models, and onboarding got measurably worse — new joiners took about a week longer to make their first change. There is also a subtler tax: the coexistence layer became a place where bugs could hide between the two systems, and those are the hardest to debug because neither side's logs tell the whole story. I would make the same trade again, but I would put an explicit end date on the fallback proxy and treat overshooting it as a real escalation, not as a fact of life.

The board asks why they should fund another year of this. What do you say?

I stop talking about frameworks. The three arguments that work are support risk with a date on it (.NET Framework 4.8 gets security fixes only, .NET 8 and .NET 9 both go end of support on 10 November 2026, and the estate has to land somewhere supported), throughput with evidence (deployments per week and lead time on the migrated routes versus the legacy ones — ours went from fortnightly to daily), and hiring (we could not recruit for WebForms and had stopped trying). Then I give them the option they are actually asking for: a smaller scope where we finish the customer-facing 80% and freeze the back-office remainder on a supported host, and I say what that costs in ongoing risk. Giving the funder a real cheaper option is what makes the expensive one credible.

Would you do it differently now that AI-assisted upgrade tooling exists?

Yes for the mechanical half, no for the strategy. GitHub Copilot app modernization for .NET — GA since September 2025 and folded into Visual Studio 2026 — will do project retargeting, package upgrades, API swaps and containerisation across a solution far faster than a human, and it now handles a good deal of the ASP.NET-to-ASP.NET Core mechanics. What it does not decide is which routes move first, what your coexistence seam is, or whether the reporting module should be migrated or retired. So my plan would look the same and my timeline for the easy 70% would be much shorter — which shifts the risk profile: you reach the four genuinely hard things sooner, with more review capacity spent on them. That is a better shape, but only if you do not let generated volume outrun your ability to review it (see story 06).

02The pod that died quietly after the upgrade

Senior Performance problem diagnosed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We moved a document-processing API from .NET 8 to .NET 10 as a routine LTS hop — three days of work, tests green, load test green, shipped. Four days later the on-call channel had a slow drip of complaints: every few hours a request would take eight or nine seconds instead of 200 ms, always on the batch endpoint, never reproducible on demand. The dashboards were unhelpful in the specific way that matters — average latency was better than before the upgrade, CPU was down about 12%, and memory was down by nearly half. On paper the upgrade was a success, which is exactly why nobody looked at it as the cause for two days.

What had changed was the garbage collector's operating point. DATAS — Dynamic Adaptation To Application Sizes — became the default for Server GC in .NET 9 and stayed the default in .NET 10. It continuously tunes the number of heaps and the budget to the workload, which is why memory halved; the trade is that it aims for a smaller footprint and will do more, smaller collections, and it adapts reactively. Our batch endpoint allocated a burst of large arrays for PDF rendering — most of them over 85 KB, so straight onto the large object heap — after long quiet periods. DATAS had by then scaled the heap count down to match the quiet workload, and the burst arrived faster than it could scale back up. The nine seconds was a gen 2 blocking collection compacting a fragmented LOH under a heap configuration sized for a much smaller app.

The diagnosis was ordinary once we stopped trusting averages. dotnet-counters against a live pod showed gen-2-gc-count and time-in-gc spiking exactly at the slow requests; a dotnet-trace capture with the GC provider confirmed blocking gen 2 collections with LOH compaction. The fix was two lines and a design change. Immediately, we turned DATAS off for that one service — it is the wrong default for a spiky, allocation-heavy workload with plenty of memory headroom — and gave the container a real memory limit so the GC could size itself against a known ceiling instead of the node. Properly, we stopped allocating the buffers at all: ArrayPool<byte>.Shared for the render buffers took steady-state allocation on that path down by about 80% and made the whole question moot. What I would do differently: our load test ran a constant rate for ten minutes, which is precisely the shape an adaptive GC optimises for. A load profile with idle periods and bursts would have caught this before production, and adding one to the pipeline was the durable outcome of the incident.

(c) Recall prompts

When did a system of yours behave differently in production than in your load test — and what was different about the traffic shape?

Hint: bursts after idle, a long tail of large payloads, one customer with 100x the data of everyone else, a nightly batch overlapping peak, or a cache that was warm in the test and cold in production. The shape mismatch is nearly always the answer, and naming it precisely is what makes this story senior rather than mid.

Have you ever had an upgrade improve the average and break the tail?

Hint: GC mode changes, a new default in a connection pool or HTTP client, TLS or compression defaults, a JIT change like dynamic PGO, or a library that started batching. Also check the reverse: a regression that only showed up as a support ticket because the dashboards were all means.

What is the last thing you profiled in production, and with what tool?

Hint: dotnet-counters, dotnet-trace, dotnet-dump, a continuous profiler, or the runtime metrics your APM already collects. If the honest answer is "I have only ever profiled locally", say so and describe how you would do it safely — the honest version is more impressive than a vague claim.

Do you know your services' GC configuration, or is it whatever the template gave you?

Hint: Server vs Workstation, concurrent, DATAS on or off, heap hard limit, and whether the container memory limit is set at all. In Kubernetes, a pod with no memory limit lets the GC size against the node — which is how one noisy pod evicts its neighbours.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did memory drop and latency get worse at the same time?

Because those are two sides of the same tuning decision. An adaptive collector trading footprint for throughput will hold a smaller heap, which means allocation budgets are exhausted sooner and collections happen more often. For a steady workload that is a good deal — you free memory you were not using and the extra collections are cheap and concurrent. For a bursty one it is a bad deal, because the collector has adapted to the quiet period and the burst arrives before it can scale heaps back up, so you take a blocking gen 2 at the worst moment. The general lesson is that memory and latency are a dial, not two independent metrics, and any runtime that moves the dial for you has to be evaluated against your real traffic shape.

Why not just leave the flag off everywhere?

Because for most of our services DATAS is straightforwardly better and turning it off would cost real money. Our typical API pod went from a 1.5 GB working set to under 800 MB on .NET 9, which let us halve the memory request and fit more pods per node. Blanket-disabling a good default because one service is unusual is exactly the reasoning that produces an estate where nobody knows why any setting is the way it is. So the rule I wrote down was: the platform default stays on, a service may opt out, and opting out requires a linked measurement in the PR description. That also gives you an audit trail when the default changes again in a future release.

The buffers were the real problem. Why did you ship the flag first?

Time-to-safety versus time-to-correctness. The flag was a config change with a known blast radius that we could ship in an hour and revert in a minute; the pooling change touched the rendering path and needed a careful review because ArrayPool hands you a buffer that is at least the size you asked for and is not zeroed, which is a beautiful source of subtle bugs if any code assumes buffer.Length is the payload length. So: mitigate on the clock, fix on the calendar, and write the follow-up ticket before you close the incident. I would defend that sequencing in any incident, but I would also admit the risk — mitigations that work tend not to get followed up, and the only reason ours did was that we put the ticket in the same sprint rather than a backlog.

At what point would you stop tuning the GC and change the architecture?

When the allocation is inherent to the workload rather than incidental. We were rendering documents in the request path on the same process that served interactive API traffic — that is the architectural smell, and no GC configuration fixes it. The next step, which we took six months later, was to move rendering onto a queue-backed worker with its own scaling and its own runtime configuration, so a heavy tenant could not affect interactive latency at all. As a rule of thumb: if a single request can allocate tens of megabytes, it does not belong in a shared request pipeline, and I would rather isolate the workload than keep discovering new GC knobs.

What would you have done if the regression had appeared during the upgrade rather than four days later?

Honestly, it would have been easier, and that is the argument for a longer bake. Our upgrade went to 100% after a two-hour canary because the metrics looked great, which is exactly the failure mode: a two-hour canary cannot see a problem that needs an idle period followed by a burst. I now ask for a bake that covers at least one full business cycle for any runtime upgrade — a weekend and a Monday morning — with the canary compared on percentiles and GC counters, not on averages and error rates. That costs a few days of calendar and it has caught two things since.

03Cutover night and the pool that ran dry

Senior Production incident / outage

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We had migrated the ordering service from ASP.NET MVC 5 on IIS to ASP.NET Core on .NET 10 in App Service, and cutover was a Tuesday 22:00 traffic switch with the old site kept warm behind a feature flag. For fifty minutes it was beautiful. At 22:51, under about a fifth of normal daytime load, the service fell over: requests queued, then failed in waves with Timeout expired. The timeout period elapsed prior to obtaining a connection from the pool. Restarting the instances bought us four minutes each time. We rolled back at 23:20, which was the right call and, I will say honestly, one I made about fifteen minutes later than I should have because I was convinced I was one log line from understanding it.

The mechanism was sync-over-async, and the migration is what exposed it. In the old app, the data layer was synchronous SqlCommand.ExecuteReader() on a classic thread-pool-per-request model, and it worked because IIS gave it a large thread pool and the calls were genuinely blocking. During the port we had converted the outer layers to async — controllers, handlers, EF Core — but three legacy helper classes still exposed synchronous methods, and their new implementations called the async ones with .Result. Under Kestrel, that blocks a thread-pool thread while an async operation waits for a thread to complete. The .NET thread pool responds to starvation by injecting new threads, but only at roughly one or two per second, so the queue grows far faster than it drains. Meanwhile every blocked call was holding an open SqlConnection, and the default Max Pool Size is 100 per distinct connection string; once 100 connections were parked behind blocked threads, every subsequent request waited 15 seconds and then threw. The pool exhaustion was the symptom everyone saw. The thread starvation was the disease.

Finding it took the memory dump. dotnet-dump plus clrstack -all showed dozens of threads sitting in GetAwaiter().GetResult() inside LegacyPricingGateway, and the ThreadPool queue length in the counters had been climbing for the entire fifty minutes — it was visible in the data from 22:05, we just were not looking at it. We deleted the three synchronous wrappers, made the callers async all the way, and cut over again nine days later without incident. Two durable changes came out of it: a Roslyn analyser that fails the build on .Result and .Wait() outside of Main and test setup, and a rollback rule I have used ever since — the person running the cutover does not get to decide when to roll back, because they are the most invested in it working. We now write the abort criteria and the clock into the runbook before the change starts, and someone else holds them.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the same code work under IIS and fail under Kestrel?

Because the two hosts have different concurrency economics. Classic ASP.NET on IIS ran a request on a thread and largely expected blocking work, with a big pool and integrated pipeline knobs sized for that; blocking a thread was the normal case, so the pool was provisioned for it. ASP.NET Core assumes non-blocking IO and runs with a much smaller effective concurrency, so each blocked thread is a much larger fraction of your capacity, and thread injection at one or two per second cannot keep up with an arrival rate in the hundreds. Add a connection held for the duration of each blocked call and you get a second, harder ceiling at Max Pool Size. The general point is that porting code between hosts changes its performance model even when the code is identical, which is why "it compiled and the tests passed" is not evidence of a safe migration.

Would raising Max Pool Size have fixed it?

No, and it is the trap answer. A larger pool moves the failure later and makes it worse: more concurrent connections means more server-side memory and worker threads on SQL Server, and you convert an application outage into a database-wide one that affects every other service on that instance. The pool limit was doing its job — it was a bulkhead. The right response to hitting a bulkhead is to ask why demand is unbounded, not to widen it. I did raise Min Pool Size afterwards, for a completely different reason: connection establishment against Azure SQL includes a TLS handshake and login round trips, so a warm floor of connections smooths cold-start latency after a scale-out.

What would you have done if there had been no rollback available — say the database schema had already migrated?

Then the plan has to be different from the start, and I would not have agreed to that cutover shape. Concretely: make schema changes expand-only and deploy them days before the code, so the old code still runs against the new schema and rollback stays open — add nullable columns and new tables, never rename or drop in the same release, and only contract weeks later once the old code is gone. If I had somehow ended up there anyway, the options collapse to shedding load to protect what still works (rate-limit or queue the write path, serve reads), scaling out to buy time while accepting the same failure at a higher volume, and getting a database restore point identified early so the worst case has a known cost. But the honest answer is that irreversible cutovers are a planning failure, not an incident-response problem.

Your analyser fails the build on .Result. What about the places that legitimately need it?

They exist and they need to be visible rather than banned. Application startup, a Main that is not async, some framework callbacks with synchronous signatures, and code inside tests are genuine cases. So the rule is an error with a documented suppression that requires a justification comment, which turns each instance into something a reviewer sees and a search can enumerate. There were eleven at the end, and reading that list was itself useful: two of them were in a legacy interface we could change, which we did. A blanket ban you cannot honour gets suppressed globally within a month, and then you have a rule that lies.

What did the second cutover do differently, and how did you know it would hold?

Four things. We ran a soak at 1.5x expected peak with the same traffic mix for two hours in a staging environment pointed at a copy of production data, watching thread-pool queue length as a first-class metric rather than CPU. We shifted traffic in stages — 5%, 25%, 100% over ninety minutes — instead of flipping a switch. We wrote abort criteria with numbers (queue length above 50 for two minutes, or any pool timeout at all) and gave them to the engineering manager, who had no emotional investment in the change. And we did it on a Wednesday morning rather than a Tuesday night, because the population of people who can help at 10 a.m. is about ten times larger. That last one gets underrated; night cutovers are frequently a cargo cult inherited from systems that had real overnight maintenance windows.

04Rewrite, replatform, or wrap the pricing engine

Principal Hard architectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The pricing engine was 240,000 lines of VB.NET on .NET Framework 4.8, written over eleven years, with about 1,900 business rules in it. It also had two properties that made it the crux of the whole modernization programme: it was correct, in the sense that the business trusted its output down to the cent and reconciled against it monthly, and it depended on a COM component from a vendor who no longer existed, used for a compounding calculation nobody could re-derive. Three options were live. Rewrite it in C# on .NET 10 with a proper rules model. Replatform it as-is by getting it onto .NET 10 with a compatibility shim. Or wrap it: leave it on Framework in a Windows container, put a modern HTTP contract in front of it, and treat it as a black box behind an anti-corruption layer.

I ran a two-week spike rather than a two-week debate, and the spike is what I would tell an interviewer about, because the decision fell out of it. We took the 40 highest-value rules and reimplemented them, then replayed eighteen months of real pricing decisions — about 4 million of them — through both engines and compared outputs. We matched on 96.2%. The 3.8% was not random: it was rounding-order differences, a rule that read the system clock, and about 900 cases where the old engine was arguably wrong and the business had been silently absorbing the result for years. That number decided everything. A rewrite was not a code project, it was a "renegotiate 3.8% of historical prices with customers" project, and no amount of engineering conviction was going to make finance sign that.

So we wrapped, but not passively. The old engine went into a Windows container on Azure Kubernetes Service with a thin ASP.NET Core facade in front of it defining a versioned contract in our language, not its own — an anti-corruption layer that meant no new code ever saw a VB type or a COM handle. Then we made the wrapper the migration vehicle: every rule change from that point had to be implemented in the new C# rules module, and the facade routed rule-by-rule, comparing outputs in shadow mode for two weeks before switching each one. Three years later, by the account of the engineer who took over, roughly 60% of pricing volume was on the new engine and the old one was still running for the long tail — which is exactly the outcome I predicted and, I would argue, the correct one: the remaining rules are rarely invoked and rarely changed, so the payback on porting them is genuinely negative. What I would do differently: I would have negotiated a written retirement decision for the COM dependency at the start. We deferred it as "a detail", and it quietly became the single reason the container has to stay on Windows, which costs real money in node pools every month.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

96.2% match. Why is that a stop signal rather than a good start?

Because the residual is not noise, it is money owed to or by named customers. Four million decisions with 3.8% divergence is about 150,000 prices that would change, and each one is a conversation someone in the business has to have. The engineering instinct is to say "we will chase the last four percent", but the shape of that work is unbounded — the divergences that remain are precisely the ones with no documented intent, so each one needs an archaeological investigation and a business ruling. I would have taken a different view at 99.99% with a bounded, characterised residual, because then you are negotiating a known list. The general principle: for a system whose output is binding, the acceptance criterion is not "works", it is "matches, or the differences are enumerated and accepted".

You wrapped a legacy component and it is still running three years later. Did you actually modernize anything?

Yes, and I would push back on the framing. What we modernized was the ability to change: pricing rule changes went from a six-week release cycle on Framework to same-week deployments on the new engine, and every new rule since has been written in a testable model. What we did not do is delete the old code, and I think the industry systematically overvalues deletion. The legacy engine now serves rarely-changing, rarely-invoked rules at a marginal cost of one node pool, and porting them would consume months to remove a cost we can quantify as small. The honest counter-argument is the one about the Windows dependency and the security surface of an unmaintained COM component, which is why I would insist on a written review of that specific risk every year rather than letting it disappear.

At what scale does the anti-corruption layer become the problem?

When it stops being a translation and starts being a system. The failure mode is a wrapper that accumulates business logic — special cases, retries with semantics, cached derived values — until you have three places where pricing behaviour lives and no one can say which is authoritative. The signals I watch for: the wrapper needing its own database, the wrapper's tests being about business rules rather than translation, and rule changes that require edits on both sides. Our guard was a rule that the facade may not contain a conditional on a business concept; if it needs one, the rule belongs on one side or the other. It also becomes a throughput problem eventually, because you have added a hop and a serialisation boundary to every price — we measured about 6 ms, which was fine for us and would not be fine in a latency-sensitive path.

How did you get finance and the business to accept a decision this technical?

By not making it technical. I presented three options with a cost, a duration, a risk, and — the part that mattered — what each one asked of them. The rewrite asked them to adjudicate 150,000 historical price differences; the wrap asked them for a node pool budget and an annual risk review. Once it was framed as their work rather than ours, the decision took twenty minutes. I also brought the replay evidence rather than an opinion, and I made a point of saying which option I had expected to recommend before the spike, which buys a lot of credibility. The technique generalises: senior stakeholders do not evaluate architectures, they evaluate obligations.

What would you do differently if you were making this call today, in 2026?

I would spend the first week on AI-assisted analysis rather than on a manual spike. The current generation of modernization agents will produce a dependency inventory, flag the unportable APIs and generate a first-pass port of a subset far faster than four engineers can — which does not change the decision, but it changes the price of the evidence, so I would replay more rules and get a tighter number. I would also seriously evaluate whether the rules could be expressed as data rather than code, because a rules table plus a small evaluator is a much better target than idiomatic C#, and it removes the "rewrite" framing entirely. And I would put the COM component on the table on day one as a named, dated retirement decision, because that is the mistake I actually made.

Suppose the vendor COM component had a critical CVE next month. What is your plan?

The plan has to exist before the CVE, and ours was thin, which I would fix. Practically: the component is unmaintained, so there is no patch, which leaves isolation and replacement. Isolation first — the wrapper already means only one service can talk to it, so I would confirm the container has no outbound network path, runs as a low-privilege user, and is reachable only from the facade's subnet, then confirm the exploit path is actually reachable from our inputs. Replacement second, and this is where the answer is uncomfortable: we would be reverse-engineering a compounding calculation under time pressure, which is the worst possible circumstance. That is the real argument I should have used three years earlier, and it is a better one than any of the ones I did use — I would frame the retirement of an unmaintainable dependency as an availability risk with a plan, not as tech debt.

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We moved a 900 GB SQL Server 2014 database off a physical box onto Azure SQL Managed Instance. It went well: the Managed Instance link kept a continuously replicating copy for eleven days while we tested, and the actual failover window was under fifteen minutes on a Sunday morning. Three weeks later a support ticket arrived saying a customer's name was displayed as Bj?rn. Then another. Then someone in the Prague office found that a whole month of address lines had lost their diacritics. The migration was long since declared a success and nobody was looking at it any more, which is the part of this story I think about most.

The mechanism is boring and vicious. The old instance was on SQL_Latin1_General_CP1_CI_AS; during the move someone standardised the new instance on Latin1_General_100_CI_AS_SC_UTF8, which is a defensible modern choice — UTF-8 collations have been supported since SQL Server 2019 and they let varchar hold the full Unicode range at roughly half the storage of nvarchar for Latin text. The problem was not the target, it was a legacy ETL that still connected with a client-side codepage assumption and wrote through a varchar staging table with an explicitly-set old collation, so characters outside its codepage were silently replaced with ?. SQL Server does not error on that. Conversion between collations is lossy by design and quiet by design: you get ?, not an exception. Truncation gives you an error, unmappable characters give you a question mark, and only one of those wakes anybody up.

Recovery was possible only because of a decision that had felt like paranoia at the time: we had kept the source instance running read-only for 30 days rather than the 7 the plan called for. So we had a clean source. I wrote a reconciliation job that hashed the affected text columns on both sides row by row, found 41,000 rows across nine tables where the target contained ? and the source did not, and patched them in batches with the business validating a sample of 200. Total data actually lost: none, in the end. Total credibility lost: quite a lot, and rightly, because we had validated the migration with row counts and checksums on numeric columns and had run exactly zero checks on text fidelity. The durable changes were a post-migration validation suite that samples text columns and compares them against the source, a database-level guard rail — ANSI_WARNINGS ON plus a build check that no table carries a column-level collation override — and a rule that the source stays available for one full business cycle plus a month, written into the plan with the cost attached so nobody quietly shortens it. What I would do differently: I would have changed the collation as a separate, later project. Doing it in the same change as the platform move meant that when something looked wrong, we had two candidate causes and no clean rollback for either.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did nothing fail? Surely a bad character conversion is an error.

No, and this is the specific piece of knowledge the story exists to teach. SQL Server treats collation conversion as a defined, lossy operation: characters that exist in the source but have no representation in the target codepage become a substitution character, and that is the documented, correct behaviour rather than an exception. Truncation is different — that raises String or binary data would be truncated, and since SQL Server 2019 the message even tells you which column. So the two damage modes have completely different observability, and any migration checklist that relies on "we would have seen errors" is only covering half of them. The practical consequence: if you change collation or codepage anywhere in the path, you must actively scan the data for the substitution signature, because nothing will volunteer it.

How did you bound the blast radius without a full comparison of 900 GB?

By using the signature rather than the whole dataset. A literal ? in a text column is rare in real data, so I started with a scan for that character across every varchar and nvarchar column in the schema — expensive but bounded, and it ran in about forty minutes against a read replica. That gave a candidate set of a few hundred thousand rows, most of them legitimate question marks in free-text notes. Then I joined only those candidates back to the retained source by primary key and compared hashes, which reduced it to the 41,000 genuinely damaged rows. The general technique is worth stating in an interview: find a cheap signature that over-selects, then do the expensive exact comparison only on the candidate set. Comparing everything is often not feasible and is rarely necessary.

At what scale does "keep the source running" stop being a viable safety net?

It fails on three axes. Volume — you can afford a read-only copy of 900 GB for a month; you probably cannot afford it for a 40 TB warehouse, and at that point you shift to targeted retention of the tables that carry irreplaceable data. Rate of change — the source is only useful for rows that have not changed since cutover, so a high-churn system gives you a shrinking window and you need change tracking to know which rows are still comparable. And regulation — sometimes you legally cannot keep a second copy of personal data, which flips the strategy entirely toward pre-migration validation, because there will be no post-migration recovery. When the safety net does not scale, the answer is to move the checks earlier: validate on a full-size rehearsal, not on production after the fact.

You changed collation and platform at once. What would the phased version have cost?

An extra outage window and roughly three weeks of calendar, which is exactly why it got merged in the first place — and I signed off on that. Phased, the sequence is: move to Managed Instance on the identical collation, run a business cycle, then do a collation change as its own project with its own validation, its own rehearsal on a restored copy, and its own rollback. The reason it is worth the three weeks is attribution: when something looked wrong, we spent the first day arguing about whether the platform or the collation was responsible, and we had no rollback that undid one without undoing the other. My rule now is that a migration changes exactly one dimension at a time — host, version, collation, schema — and I will trade calendar for that every time, because the alternative is trading it for debugging under pressure.

What do you tell the customer whose name was wrong for three weeks?

The truth, quickly, and with the fix already applied where possible. Concretely: what happened in one sentence without jargon, what data was affected and what was not, that it has been corrected and verified against the original, and what we changed so it cannot recur. I would not send that until I could say "corrected" rather than "investigating", unless there was a decision the customer needed to make. The decision that is not mine is whether this is a reportable personal-data integrity incident — that goes to whoever owns that obligation the same day, with the evidence, because an engineer quietly deciding a data-integrity issue is not reportable is how organisations get into real trouble.

Hint: characterization tests, a contract snapshot, a staging environment with real-shaped data, a rollback path, or an inventory of which repositories were even in scope. This pre-work is usually the part of the story with the most staff-level content in it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

You rejected changes that were genuine improvements. Isn't that wasteful?

It would be if I had rejected the changes; I rejected them in that pull request. Mixing a mechanical, low-risk, high-volume change with a discretionary design change destroys the reviewability of both: the reviewer cannot skim the mechanical part because a real change is hiding in it, and cannot properly evaluate the design change because it is buried in 3,000 lines of retargeting. We kept a list, and about half of the agent's suggestions were adopted later as their own small PRs where they got real review. The principle is older than AI tooling — one intent per commit — but AI makes it matter more, because generation is now cheap enough to produce diffs no human would have written by hand.

The serialisation change would have broken a partner. Why did review catch it and your tests not?

Because our tests asserted on objects and the partner consumed bytes. Round-tripping through the serialiser and comparing the deserialised object is invariant to a surprising number of wire-format changes — property casing, enum representation, null handling, number formatting — and that is exactly the class the change fell into. The fix was to add contract snapshot tests: serialise a fixed set of representative objects and assert on the resulting JSON text, checked into the repository so a change to the wire format shows up as a diff a human must approve. For HTTP APIs we did the equivalent with the generated OpenAPI document, which ASP.NET Core 10 emits as OpenAPI 3.1 and can serve as YAML, making the diff readable. Catching it by luck is not a control; the snapshot is.

At what scale does "one repo per PR" stop working?

At around the point where the number of repositories exceeds what one person can shepherd in a sprint, which for us was about twenty. Beyond that the rule survives but the process has to change shape: you need the migration to be a pipeline rather than a queue of PRs — a scheduled job that opens the PR, runs the evidence gathering automatically, and only escalates to a human when the evidence differs from the expected pattern. That is the same idea as an auto-merge policy for dependency updates, with the same precondition: it only works where your tests are genuinely load-bearing. For a 300-repository estate I would invest first in making the evidence automatic and only then in scaling the generation, because otherwise you have built a machine for producing unreviewed change.

How do you stop the team from resenting the extra process?

By making the process cheaper than the alternative and by not being the only person who believes in it. The PR template with the evidence checklist took about ten minutes per PR, and I made sure the two things it asked for — the characterization run and the contract diff — were produced by CI automatically, so the "evidence" was a link rather than homework. Then I asked the engineer whose PR had contained the serialisation change to present it at the team session, which he did generously, and after that nobody argued about the rules because the rule had a face and a near-miss attached to it. Where I would be careful: that only works if the presentation is genuinely blameless, and I checked with him privately first.

What is your position on letting an agent open PRs unattended today?

Conditional yes, scoped by blast radius. For changes whose correctness is fully captured by tests you trust — patch-level dependency bumps, formatting, analyser fixes — unattended generation with auto-merge on green is a straightforward win and we do it. For anything that can change observable behaviour, I want a human accountable for the merge, and I want the evidence to be produced by something other than the agent that wrote the change, because a system that both writes the code and asserts it is correct has a conflict of interest. The uncomfortable part of this answer is that the boundary keeps moving, so I treat it as a policy to revisit every quarter rather than a principle — and I would rather be six months behind the frontier on a payments system than six months ahead.

07"We should split it into microservices"

Senior Technical disagreement with a colleague

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Two months into planning the modernization of a logistics platform, a senior colleague — genuinely good, and someone whose opinion carried weight — proposed that the target architecture be about fourteen microservices. His reasoning was not naive: the team of 22 was tripping over itself in one deployment unit, releases had become a weekly coordination meeting, and one slow module was scaling the entire application. Those are all real problems and he was right that the status quo was unacceptable. My disagreement was about the solution, and specifically about one fact: all fourteen proposed services would have needed to write to the same shipment aggregate inside what was currently a single database transaction.

I did not argue in the abstract, because architectural arguments in the abstract are unwinnable and unpleasant. Instead I asked for a week to write up the three highest-traffic workflows as they would look after the split, with the transaction boundaries explicit. The order-to-dispatch flow turned out to touch five of the proposed services and needed either a distributed transaction or a saga with five compensating actions, one of which — un-reserving warehouse capacity after a carrier had already been booked — has no clean compensation in the real world; you have to phone someone. That document changed the conversation completely, because it moved us from "monolith bad, services good" to a concrete question: is this boundary real? Some were. Carrier integration, notifications and the tracking read model were genuinely separable, with asynchronous coupling and their own scaling profiles.

What we agreed was a modular monolith first: one deployment unit, but hard module boundaries enforced mechanically rather than by good intentions — separate assemblies, no project reference across a boundary except to a contracts project, and architecture tests in CI that fail the build on a violation. Then we extracted exactly the three services where the boundary had proved itself under real change, over about nine months. The tracking read model went first and paid for itself immediately, because it could scale independently and was read-only. Where my colleague was right and I was wrong: I had underweighted the organisational problem. Module boundaries in one repository do not give teams independent release cadence, and eighteen months later that was still the loudest complaint. If I ran the argument again I would concede that point explicitly at the start and treat deployment independence as a first-class requirement to be solved, rather than as a thing microservices happen to provide.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Isn't a modular monolith just a monolith with extra rules?

It is, and that is the point — the rules are the product. What distinguishes it is that the boundaries are mechanically enforced and the coupling is explicit, which gives you two things a conventional monolith does not: you can reason about the blast radius of a change, and you have a real option to extract a module later, because the seam already exists and has been maintained under pressure. What it does not give you is independent deployment, independent scaling, independent runtimes or fault isolation, and pretending otherwise is how teams end up disappointed. So I present it as a deliberate deferral: we take the boundary discipline now, when it is cheap, and buy the option on distribution for when we can name the specific benefit we need.

At what point would you have agreed to the full split?

Three conditions, and I would want at least two. If the workflows had been genuinely separable — asynchronous coupling, no shared transactional aggregate, no cross-service invariant that has to hold instantly. If the teams had been separable in the org chart, with real ownership and their own on-call, because a service without an owning team becomes everyone's problem and nobody's. And if the operational maturity had been there: distributed tracing, contract testing, a deployment pipeline that does not need a human per service, and people who have run distributed systems before. We had none of the third in that team at that time, and adding fourteen network boundaries to a group that had never debugged a partial failure is how you get a system that is slower, less reliable and harder to change than what you started with.

What did you trade away with the modular monolith?

Independent deployability, which cost us more than I expected — that was still the top complaint in retros eighteen months later, and it is a real productivity tax when 22 people share a release train. We also kept a single runtime and a single scaling unit, so the one heavy module still forced us to scale everything, and we kept a single technology choice, which mattered when a team wanted a different stack for a genuinely different workload. What we bought with it was a system where a change could be reasoned about locally and a boundary could be revised in an afternoon rather than negotiated between two services with deployed contracts. I would make the trade again, but I would have paired it with real investment in trunk-based development and independent feature flagging, which is the actual answer to release coupling and does not require distribution.

Your colleague still disagreed. How do you work with someone after you have won an argument?

Deliberately, and by giving them the decision that follows. He owned the extraction of the tracking service — the piece where he was right — and that was not a consolation prize, it was the highest-value part of the plan and he was the best person for it. I also wrote the decision record so that it stated his position accurately, in his words where possible, including the part we did not solve; people forgive losing an argument far more easily than being misrepresented in the write-up everyone reads later. And I said out loud in the team session that the release-cadence problem was real and unaddressed, which cost me nothing and meant he did not have to keep re-raising it to be heard. The failure mode I have seen elsewhere is the winner tidying the disagreement out of the record, and it poisons the next three arguments.

Nine months later, was the modular monolith still modular?

Mostly, and the erosion is instructive. The architecture tests held wherever they were specific, and they were bypassed twice — both times through the contracts assembly, which slowly accumulated types that were not contracts at all but shared domain objects, because that was the one legal path across a boundary and water finds a route. The lesson is that a single permitted escape hatch becomes the coupling point, so I would now test the contracts assembly itself: no behaviour, no dependencies on module internals, and a size budget that triggers a review. The other half of the answer is that boundaries survive in proportion to how often they are exercised — the three modules that had real, frequent change stayed clean, and the one that nobody touched drifted, because nothing pushed back on it.

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I inherited the modernization of a claims-handling application whose original team had left the company four years earlier. There was no specification, the wiki was a graveyard of half-finished pages from 2019, and when I asked the three business stakeholders what the system did, I got three different answers that disagreed about whether a particular approval step existed at all. The instinct in that situation is to run a requirements-gathering exercise. I have done that before and it produces a document describing the system people believe they have, which is not the one you have to migrate.

So we went to the evidence instead, in three passes. First, what is actually called: twelve months of IIS logs aggregated by route showed 214 endpoints, of which 61 had received zero requests in a year and another 40 had fewer than ten. Second, what actually runs: SQL Server Query Store gave us every query executed against the database in the retention window, which surfaced two scheduled jobs and one Access front end nobody had mentioned — the Access database turned out to be how the fraud team did their entire workflow. Third, what actually happens: we put the new service behind the old one in shadow mode using YARP's request mirroring, so production traffic hit both, only the legacy response was returned, and we diffed the two. That third pass is the one I would recommend to anyone, because it converts "does the new implementation behave the same" from an opinion into a daily number. We ran it for six weeks and the mismatch rate went from 11% to 0.4%, with the residual being timestamp fields and a genuine legacy bug in date rounding that the business chose to keep.

The characterization tests came out of the shadow run: every mismatch we resolved became a test that pinned the behaviour, including the bug we deliberately preserved, with a comment saying it was deliberate and who decided. That comment matters more than it sounds — an undocumented preserved bug gets "fixed" by a well-meaning engineer within a year. On the dead endpoints, I did not delete them. We instrumented them to log and alert on any call, announced a 90-day retirement window, and removed them at the end; three of the 61 fired, all from an internal reporting tool, which is exactly why you do not trust a year of logs as proof of nothing. What I would do differently: I spent the first three weeks trying to get a definitive answer from stakeholders before I started measuring. I should have run the log analysis in week one and used it to drive the stakeholder conversations, because people are far better at reacting to a concrete list than at recalling a system from memory.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just ask the users what they need and build that?

Because that is a different project, and conflating the two is how modernization programmes fail. Replacing a system you do not understand while simultaneously changing what it does gives you no way to tell a migration defect from an intended change, and no way to answer "is the new one correct" — correct against what? So I insist on behaviour parity as phase one, with a very short list of explicitly agreed exceptions, and I put the genuine improvements immediately after, when they can be evaluated on their own merits with a working baseline. The counter-argument is real: sometimes a system is so bad that parity is not worth having, and then you should say so and run it as a replacement project with proper discovery, not as a migration.

You kept 61 unused endpoints alive for 90 days. What is that costing you?

Directly, very little — they were already there. Indirectly, three things: they are attack surface that has not been reviewed in years, they hold the schema hostage because you cannot drop a column something might still read, and they distort every metric about how much work remains. The 90 days was a deliberate purchase of certainty, and I would shorten it for anything with a plausible caller and lengthen it for anything with an annual cycle — a year-end report endpoint could sit unused for eleven months and still be essential. What I would not do is delete on the strength of logs alone, because the cost asymmetry is brutal: an unnecessary 90-day wait costs a little attention, and deleting the thing the auditors need in March costs you the programme's credibility.

Shadow mode doubles your write load. How did you handle that?

We did not mirror writes to the real database — the shadow instance ran against a synchronised copy, and mutations on the new side went nowhere durable. That preserves the comparison for the vast majority of behaviour, at the cost of a class of bug it will never catch: anything that depends on the effect of a previous write, and anything about concurrency between the two systems. We handled those with a separate, smaller replay harness over recorded sequences. The other cost is money and latency — mirroring is fire-and-forget, so it does not block the response, but it does double compute on the mirrored routes, which is why we mirrored a sampled subset for the high-volume ones. Anyone who claims shadow traffic is free has not paid for it.

At what scale does the shadow-diff approach break down?

It struggles the moment responses contain legitimate nondeterminism — timestamps, generated ids, ordering that is unspecified, prices that depend on a rate fetched at call time — because the diff drowns in false positives and people stop reading it. The fix is a normalisation layer with an explicit allow-list of fields to ignore, and the discipline to treat each addition to that list as a decision rather than a convenience; ours grew to about fifteen entries and two of them were wrong, in the sense that they hid a real difference for a fortnight. It also breaks down where the response is enormous or binary — generated PDFs, for instance, where we compared extracted text rather than bytes. And it cannot help you at all with stateful, long-running workflows, which need the replay approach instead.

How did you present "we do not know what it does" to leadership without sounding incompetent?

By presenting it as a finding with a number attached rather than as a confession. "214 endpoints, 101 with negligible traffic, three undocumented consumers found so far, and here is the six-week plan to establish behaviour empirically" is a position of control; "nobody knows how this works" is a complaint. I also made the point that this is the normal state of a fourteen-year-old system and that the alternative — trusting the documentation — is the actually dangerous option, which reframes the discovery work as risk reduction rather than as delay. The one thing I would avoid is promising a completion date for discovery before you have done a fortnight of it; I gave a date early on and had to move it, and that cost more trust than the ambiguity did.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

You ended up more expensive than the datacentre. Was the migration a failure?

Not on the terms it was justified on, but I would not let anyone pretend the cost line was a win. What we bought for the difference was elasticity we actually used at month-end, a disaster-recovery posture that previously did not exist, patching and hardware refresh we no longer do, and — the one that mattered to the business — deployment frequency that went from fortnightly to daily. The right way to present it is as total cost of ownership with the operational labour included, and to be explicit that the raw hosting line went up. Where I would push back on my own answer: TCO arguments are easy to inflate, so I only counted the datacentre costs we genuinely stopped paying, and I said which staff time was redeployed rather than saved.

Log ingestion was your biggest line. Why not just log less everywhere?

Because logs are how you diagnose the next incident, and a team that has been burned on cost tends to over-correct into blindness, which is a much more expensive failure. The discipline I prefer is to separate the three telemetry jobs: metrics for alerting, which are cheap and should be comprehensive; traces for diagnosis, which can be sampled aggressively with tail-based sampling so you keep the errors and the slow ones; and logs for forensics, which should be structured, at Information by default, and routed to a cheap tier when they are high-volume and rarely queried. On top of that, a per-service ingestion budget with an alert, so a chatty new dependency is caught in a day. What I would not do is set the production log level by hand during an incident without a ticket to put it back — that is exactly how ours got left on.

At what scale do these levers stop mattering?

They invert. At our size the estate was small enough that human-scale clean-up was the whole game and the biggest lever was configuration hygiene. Past a certain point — dozens of teams, thousands of resources — no one can hand-audit anything and the levers become governance ones: policy that denies untagged or oversized resources at deployment, chargeback so teams see their own number, platform templates that default to sensible sizes, and automated rightsizing recommendations that create tickets. In the other direction, at very large scale the interesting money moves to architecture rather than configuration — data gravity, egress topology, storage tiering, and whether you are paying for compute to move bytes that should not be moving. The generalisable point is that cost work follows the same shape as performance work: profile first, and expect the answer to be somewhere you did not look.

Non-production auto-shutdown annoys developers. How did you make it stick?

By making the resume path faster than the complaint. Auto-pause on a serverless database has a resume delay measured in tens of seconds, which is genuinely irritating if it happens when you are mid-flow, so we scheduled the wake for 07:30 on weekdays rather than relying purely on first-touch resume, and we gave every developer a one-command way to pin an environment awake for the day. The shutdown itself was never negotiable and never required anyone to remember anything, which is the actual design rule — a cost control that depends on discipline decays within a month. I also showed the team the number: £2,800 a month is a contractor, and framing it as headcount rather than as pennies changed the tone of the conversation immediately.

The CFO asks you to cut another 30%. What do you say?

I would ask what we should stop doing, and mean it as a real question rather than a rebuke. After the easy 45% is gone, the remaining levers all have a cost: reducing redundancy or failover capacity, dropping retention below what audit wants, moving to a smaller region, denser packing with worse blast radius, or turning off an environment. I would come back with three named options, each with a risk and an owner for accepting that risk, plus a fourth that is architectural and takes a quarter — usually the data path, since that is where the money hides at that stage. What I would refuse to do is take the cut out of telemetry or non-production and call it free, because both of those bill you later in incident duration and defect escape, which are just costs on someone else's line.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is the in-process model going away at all — what is actually better about isolated?

The isolated worker runs your code in its own process rather than inside the Functions host, which decouples your runtime and dependency versions from the host's. In-process, your assembly loads alongside the host's, so you inherit its .NET version and can hit assembly conflicts with anything it happens to reference — which is precisely why the model is pinned to a host runtime and has to retire with it. Isolated gives you a normal .NET generic host: your own DI container, your own configuration, middleware you can write, and the freedom to target a new .NET version the day it ships rather than when the Functions host catches up. The cost is a serialisation boundary between host and worker, which shows up as slightly higher cold-start and as binding types you no longer get handed directly — that is the source of nearly all the migration friction.

What would you have done if the product director had simply said no to all three options?

Escalate, but with a change of subject: at that point it is not a scheduling disagreement, it is an unallocated risk, and my job is to make sure it is accepted by someone with the authority to accept it. I would write it down in one paragraph — what happens on 11 November if nothing changes, what the recovery costs then, who is accepting the risk — and put it in front of his manager and mine together, without drama and without a recommendation the first time. In practice that conversation almost never needs to happen, because writing the paragraph is usually enough to change the answer. What I would not do is quietly do the migration in slack time and let the feature slip, because covering for a bad decision means the decision never gets corrected and I have taken on accountability for something I did not agree to.

Seven hard functions, one of them Durable. What made those different?

Durable Functions works on the isolated model but the orchestration context is a different type with a different surface, and orchestrator code is replayed — so any refactor risks changing the determinism guarantees that make replay safe. Concretely you cannot introduce a non-deterministic call, a DateTime.UtcNow, or a new Guid into an orchestrator during the port without breaking in-flight instances, and there were in-flight instances with a lifetime of days. So the plan for those two was different in kind: drain to zero in-flight orchestrations during a quiet window, deploy, then resume — which required coordination with the business about a pause, not just an engineering change. That is the general shape of the "hard bucket" in any migration: the difficulty is rarely the code, it is that the code has state in flight.

Eleven functions could have stayed on the old model past the deadline. What does "retired" actually mean operationally?

It is worth being precise, because the answer differs by product and people get it badly wrong in both directions. For the Functions in-process model, existing apps do not stop dead on the date — what ends is support: no security patches, no fixes, and no help when something breaks. That is different again from the Functions v1 runtime, which retired on 14 September 2026, and from v3 on Linux Consumption on 30 September 2026, where the guidance was blunter about apps being stopped. So "accept the risk into January" was a defensible option for eleven internal, low-traffic functions and would not have been for a public payments endpoint. The discipline is to read the actual retirement notice rather than repeating a summary — I have seen a team panic-migrate on a date that only affected a SKU they did not use, and another discover that "unsupported" meant "stopped".

How do you stop platform deadlines from being a surprise next time?

A single register with three columns — the date, the thing it kills, and a named owner — reviewed quarterly with the same seriousness as the roadmap, and populated from the vendors' own lifecycle feeds rather than from memory. The rule I care about is that everything on it must be a date, not a version: ".NET 8 and 9 end of support, 10 November 2026" is actionable, "we should upgrade .NET" is not. The second half is capacity: I now hold roughly 15% of the team's quarter for this class of work as a standing allocation rather than fighting for it each time, which converts an annual negotiation into a routine. It is not glamorous and it is the single highest-leverage process change I have made on a legacy estate.

Our pull-request pipeline took 22 minutes on a good day and failed for unrelated reasons about one run in seven. The consequence was cultural rather than technical: people batched changes to avoid the wait, batched changes are harder to review, and a flaky pipeline teaches everyone that a red build means "click retry". On a team doing a long migration that is corrosive, because the test suite is the only thing standing between you and a behaviour change nobody intended. I took two weeks on it and treated it as a reliability project rather than a speed one, which turned out to be the right framing.

The breakdown was: 8 minutes of TypeScript type-checking and bundling for the front end, 9 minutes of .NET build and test, 3 minutes of container build, 2 minutes of everything else. The front-end half was solved by a version bump we had been putting off — TypeScript 7.0, which went generally available on 8 July 2026 and replaces the JavaScript compiler with a native Go implementation; on our roughly 4,000-file codebase a full type-check went from 96 seconds to about 9. The catch is the one that matters in an interview: TypeScript 7.0 shipped without a stable programmatic API, so the tools that host the compiler — typescript-eslint, ts-jest, and the template type-checkers behind Vue, Svelte and Astro — could not run on it at release. We run typescript-eslint, so the answer was a two-compiler arrangement: tsgo for the build and the type-check gate, TypeScript 6 retained purely for the lint step, pinned and documented with a deletion condition rather than left as sediment.

The .NET half was less exciting and mattered more. About four of the nine minutes were 140 integration tests that each spun up a Testcontainers SQL Server; sharing one container per test class with a transaction-per-test rollback took that to 90 seconds. The flakiness was concentrated in eleven tests, all of which depended on real time or on test execution order — so we quarantined them into a separate non-blocking job the same afternoon, which took the failure rate from 14% to under 2% immediately, and then fixed them over the next fortnight using TimeProvider so time could be controlled rather than waited for. The result was 22 minutes to 6, and merges per day roughly doubled. What I would do differently: I quarantined the flaky tests without a hard deadline, and two of the eleven were still sitting in that quarantine job four months later. A quarantine without an expiry date is just a slower deletion, and I should have written the date in the job's own configuration.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Node can run TypeScript directly now. Why do you still have a compiler in CI at all?

Because those are different jobs and conflating them is a real trap. Node's native TypeScript support — on by default for .ts files since Node 24, the current LTS — is type stripping: it removes the annotations and runs the JavaScript. It never checks anything, so a file with a genuine type error runs happily. That is excellent for scripts and for local iteration, and it is not a substitute for a type-check gate; if you drop tsc or tsgo from CI on the strength of "Node runs TypeScript now", you have silently turned TypeScript into JSDoc. There is a second gotcha worth knowing: stripping cannot handle constructs that need code generation, so enums and parameter properties either need the erasable subset or a real compiler. Our rule is stripping for developer ergonomics, an explicit type-check job for correctness.

Two compilers in one repository sounds like exactly the debt you spend your life removing.

It is debt, and the difference between acceptable and unacceptable debt is whether it has a written exit condition and an owner. Ours was one line in the README and a linked issue: when typescript-eslint supports the TypeScript 7 API — expected with the 7.1 programmatic API — delete the second dependency and the alias. The alternative was to sit on the old compiler for another six months and keep paying 96 seconds on every commit, or to drop linting, which is a much worse trade. What I would refuse is the version of this where the interim state is undocumented and the reason lives only in the head of whoever set it up; that is how a repository ends up with four package managers and nobody able to explain any of them.

You quarantined flaky tests. Isn't that just hiding failures?

Yes, temporarily, and that is better than the status quo it replaced — which was a pipeline where every failure was assumed to be flake, including the real ones. A quarantine converts an unreliable signal into a reliable one plus a known debt, and the known debt is manageable in a way that ambient distrust is not. The conditions I would insist on: the quarantined tests still run and still report, the list is visible and short, each entry has an owner and a date, and the pipeline fails if the list grows beyond a threshold or an entry passes its date. We had all of those except the date, and predictably that is exactly where it decayed. If a team cannot meet those conditions, deleting the tests honestly is better than a quarantine that pretends.

At what scale does "make the whole pipeline fast" stop being achievable?

When the suite grows past what you can afford to run on every commit, which for most teams is somewhere north of twenty minutes of pure test time regardless of parallelism, because the money and the machine pool become the constraint. At that point the strategy changes from "run everything faster" to "run the right subset": test impact analysis driven by the dependency graph, a fast gate on the PR and a full suite on merge, and contract tests replacing broad integration tests at service boundaries. That trade is real — you accept that some failures are found post-merge instead of pre-merge, which is only tolerable if reverting is cheap and safe. I would not reach for it before exhausting the boring wins, because a subset strategy on top of a slow, flaky suite gives you the worst of both.

How did you justify two weeks on CI instead of migration work?

With the arithmetic, which was embarrassingly favourable. Twenty-two minutes times roughly 30 pipeline runs a day is eleven hours of wall-clock latency a day across the team, and the retry rate added about a fifth on top; even valuing that conservatively, the two weeks paid back inside a month. But the argument that actually landed with my manager was the risk one: we were in the middle of a migration whose entire safety model rested on the test suite, and a suite people did not trust was not providing that safety. Framing CI reliability as migration risk rather than as developer convenience is what got it prioritised, and I think that framing is genuinely true rather than a rhetorical trick.

How confident can you actually be that the WSDL is identical?

Confident about the bytes, less confident about the semantics, and the gap is where the risk lives. None of that is spin as long as it is accurate, and I would refuse to do it if it were not.

It was, of course, timezones, but the specific mechanism took a while and taught me more about DateTime than any amount of reading had.

There were three bugs stacked on top of each other. The first was the obvious one: the code used DateTime.Now to decide the billing day, and on the old Windows server the machine timezone was Europe/London while the container's system timezone was UTC — so between midnight and 01:00 British Summer Time, the container thought it was already the next day. That explained the date shift but not why only some customers were affected. The second bug was that usage records were stored as DateTime with Kind = Unspecified, which means the value carries no information about what it is; the old code got away with it because everything ran in one timezone, and the moment two components disagreed about the implicit zone, the arithmetic silently changed meaning. The third was the one that actually took the two days: the service crashed on start for one deployment variant with TimeZoneNotFoundException, because the slim base image did not include the tzdata package, so TimeZoneInfo.FindSystemTimeZoneById had nothing to read. Someone had "fixed" that weeks earlier by catching the exception and falling back to UTC, which is why the failure was silent and intermittent rather than loud.

What found it was not cleverness, it was writing down the invariant. I made a table of every timestamp in the flow and asked, for each one, what instant it represents and in whose calendar — and two columns of that table could not be filled in, which is exactly where the bugs were. The fix was systematic rather than local: DateTimeOffset for anything that is an instant, an explicit named IANA timezone stored against the customer for anything that is a calendar decision, DateOnly for billing periods because a billing day is not an instant at all, and TimeProvider injected so tests could pin the clock instead of hoping. We also deleted the swallowed exception and added tzdata to the base image, and added a startup check that resolves the timezones the application needs and fails fast if it cannot. What I would do differently: the swallowed exception was in a pull request I approved. It looked like defensive coding. Three independent conditions had to line up, which is why it took two days. Immediately, and it is the expensive half. Analysers and schema, in that order. On the storage side, a convention that instants are stored as datetimeoffset and calendar dates as date, with a schema check in CI. Then the operational half: a startup validation that resolves every timezone id the service needs, so a missing tzdata fails the deployment rather than a customer's invoice. None of that is clever, and it is the difference between fixing a bug and removing a bug class — which is the distinction I would want a mid-level engineer to be starting to make, and a senior one to reach for by default.

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

During the coexistence phase of a migration — the old ASP.NET Framework app and the new ASP.NET Core app both serving the same site behind one hostname — we started getting a specific complaint: users were being logged out, apparently at random, several times a day. It always followed a deployment or a scale event, which pointed straight at the ASP.NET Core Data Protection key ring. That subsystem encrypts the authentication cookie, and by default it persists its keys to the local filesystem — which in a container means they are generated fresh on every start and shared with nobody. Three instances behind a load balancer means three key rings, so a cookie issued by one instance cannot be read by the other two.

The genuinely interesting part is what had already been done about it before I looked. An engineer under pressure had found a Stack Overflow answer, and there were now two changes in production: the key ring was being persisted to an Azure Blob container, which is correct, and the keys were being written unencrypted because ProtectKeysWithAzureKeyVault had needed a permission nobody wanted to request that afternoon — and the storage account had a shared access signature in a configuration file with a two-year expiry. So the fix for the logout problem had turned the authentication cookie signing keys into a file readable by anyone who could read that connection string, which included every developer with access to the app configuration. Nobody had done anything wrong maliciously; it was three reasonable decisions under time pressure that added up to a forgeable session cookie.

Untangling it had a technical half and a process half. Technically: keys persisted to blob storage, encrypted at rest with a Key Vault key, access via managed identity so there is no secret in configuration at all, the shared access signature revoked, and — because we were in coexistence — the two applications configured with the same application name and a cookie format both could read, which for the Framework side meant the ASP.NET Core Data Protection interop package rather than a hand-rolled shared machine key. Then we rotated: new key ring, old keys retained for decryption only for the cookie lifetime, and a forced re-authentication for administrative accounts. The process half mattered more. The root cause was that requesting a Key Vault access policy took four days through a ticket queue, so the path of least resistance was the insecure one; we changed that by pre-provisioning managed identities and Key Vault access for every service in the platform template, so the secure option is the default and requires no ticket at all. What I would do differently: I found this by chance while reviewing something else. A key ring is exactly the kind of configuration that should be asserted in a startup health check — key ring persisted, key ring encrypted, protector configured — and building that check was the change that would have caught it in an hour instead of five weeks.

// The three properties that make a shared key ring safe: shared location,
// encrypted at rest, no secret in configuration.
builder.Services.AddDataProtection()
    .SetApplicationName("acme-portal")                 // must match the legacy app during coexistence
    .PersistKeysToAzureBlobStorage(new Uri(cfg["Dp:BlobUri"]!), new DefaultAzureCredential())
    .ProtectKeysWithAzureKeyVault(new Uri(cfg["Dp:KeyUri"]!), new DefaultAzureCredential())
    .SetDefaultKeyLifetime(TimeSpan.FromDays(90));

// And the assertion, because configuration that is only correct on the day it is written
// is not correct. Fails the deployment, not the user.
builder.Services.AddHealthChecks().AddCheck<DataProtectionRingHealthCheck>("dp-key-ring");

(d) Follow-up drill chain

What could an attacker actually have done with those keys?

Forged an authentication cookie for any user, including administrators, and been indistinguishable from a legitimate login — no password, no second factor, nothing in the sign-in logs, because from the application's point of view the cookie is simply valid. That is why this was a serious finding rather than a hygiene one: the impact is complete authentication bypass, and it is silent. It also affects anything else protected by that key ring, which in ASP.NET Core includes anti-forgery tokens and anything the application passes through a data protector — for us that was a couple of signed URLs. The reachability question is what bounded it: the storage account was not public, so the exposure was to identities inside our tenant with access to that configuration, which was about 30 people and no external party.

How did you determine it had not been exploited?

Partially, and I said so in the write-up rather than claiming more. We had storage-account access logs for the retention window showing who had read the blob — only the application's identity and one engineer during the original change. We checked sign-in and application audit logs for sessions whose identity had no corresponding authentication event, which is the signature a forged cookie would leave, and found none. What we could not do was cover the full exposure window, because the storage logs only went back 30 days and the exposure was five weeks. So the honest conclusion was "no evidence of misuse, with a gap of about a week where we cannot say", and the rotation plus forced re-authentication was what closed the risk regardless of what the logs could prove. Overstating that certainty is a fast way to lose a security team's trust permanently.

The coexistence requirement made this harder. What exactly is the constraint?

Both applications have to be able to read a cookie the other one issued, which means the same protection keys and a compatible cookie format. The mistake people make is to reach for a shared legacy machine key or to disable encryption to make the formats line up — both of which trade a compatibility problem for a security one. The supported route is the interop package that lets the Framework application use the ASP.NET Core Data Protection stack, with a matching application name so the key derivation purposes align. The forward-looking half of the answer: the constraint disappears the moment both sides delegate authentication to an identity provider and hold a token rather than a locally-protected cookie, which is where I would rather end up — and in ASP.NET Core 10, Identity ships passkey support built on WebAuthn and FIDO2, so the migration target can be genuinely better than the thing it replaces rather than merely equivalent.

At what scale does a blob-backed key ring stop being the right answer?

It holds well past most people's needs — it is a small file read at startup and on key refresh, so the load is trivial regardless of instance count. Where it strains is multi-region: a single blob in one region becomes a startup dependency for every region, and a regional outage now prevents cold starts everywhere, so you want geo-replication with an understanding of the consistency model, or a per-region ring with a shared root of trust. The other limit is organisational rather than technical — once dozens of services each have a key ring, the interesting question stops being "is this one configured correctly" and becomes "how do I know all of them are", which is a policy-and-inventory problem. That is the point where I would push the check into the platform: deny deployment of a service whose key-ring configuration does not match the template.

Would you name the engineer in the postmortem?

No, and I would push back on anyone who wanted to. The mechanism that produced the outcome was a four-day ticket queue standing between a person under deployment pressure and the secure option; naming him teaches the organisation that the lesson is "be more careful", which is not a lesson, and it guarantees the next person hides the same decision instead of flagging it. What I did do was ask him to walk the team through the change, which he offered to do himself, and that carried far more weight than any policy note. The one place I would name individuals is decisions and approvals — who accepted a risk, who signed off — because accountability for a decision is different from blame for a mistake, and conflating them is how you get a culture with neither.

The measure of success is not that they write code I would have written; it is that they catch things in each other's code that I would have caught.

At what scale does this approach stop working?

It works for a team; it does not work for an organisation, and the shape has to change at somewhere around three or four teams. Beyond that you cannot pair with everyone and the artefacts have to carry the whole load: a genuinely good template repository, analysers and platform defaults that make the wrong thing hard, written architecture decisions that explain the why, and a small number of people per team who have been through it and can teach locally. That last part is the one people skip — scaling teaching means growing teachers, so I would deliberately have each of the four run the kata for the next team rather than running it myself, which also does more for them than another round of my review comments would.

What if the team simply does not want to modernize?

Then I would want to know why before deciding it is resistance, because two of the most common reasons are legitimate. Sometimes people have watched a previous modernization fail and are protecting themselves from another death march; sometimes they can see that the new stack will not solve the problem they actually have. Both deserve a real answer rather than a change-management technique. If the reason turns out to be that the change threatens their expertise — which is what it was for us, in part — then the honest response is to make the new stack somewhere they can be expert again, quickly, which is exactly what the kata and the early review responsibility were for. And where someone genuinely does not want to come, saying so early is a kindness; a team of people who did not choose this will produce the compliance-without-understanding I got in my first two weeks, permanently.

The obvious suspects — VPN bandwidth, CPU, database load — were all fine, and the database reported that every query was completing in single-digit milliseconds. That combination is the fingerprint of a chattiness problem, and it is the single most common way a lift-and-shift goes wrong.

The arithmetic is the whole story and it is worth being able to do it out loud. Application Insights showed 1,240 dependency calls for that one page. On the LAN, round-trip time between the app server and the database was about 0.25 ms, so 1,240 round trips cost roughly 0.3 seconds and nobody had ever noticed. The cause was ordinary EF Core lazy loading in a loop — a stock-take of 400 line items, each touching a product, a location and a supplier — plus a repository method that reloaded a lookup table per item. This is the law that generalises: latency is a multiplier on chattiness, and moving a component changes latency by two orders of magnitude. Anything that worked only because a hop was nearly free is now a bug.

We fixed it in three passes. First, projection: the screen needed eleven fields, so it got a single query projecting straight into a DTO rather than materialising entity graphs — 1,240 calls to 3, and 38 seconds to 1.4. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is round-trip count the right metric rather than query duration?

Because duration is a property of the database and count is a property of your code, and only one of them survives a topology change. A page making 1,240 fast queries and a page making 3 fast queries look identical on any dashboard that reports average query time — both look excellent — and they behave completely differently the moment latency rises. Count also correlates with things duration does not: connection pool pressure, transaction duration, and the probability that a request is interrupted mid-flight. So my rule is that count is a design property to be budgeted per endpoint, and duration is an operational property to be monitored. If I could only have one alert on a data access layer, it would be on count per request, not on milliseconds.

You reached for AsSplitQuery in a similar case once. Why not here?

Because it solves the opposite problem and would have made this worse. Split query exists for cartesian explosion — when a single query with multiple collection Includes multiplies rows and moves far too much data — and it fixes that by issuing more round trips, one per collection. In a high-latency topology that trade goes the wrong way: you are trading bandwidth you have for round trips you cannot afford. So the decision rule is topology-dependent, which is exactly the kind of "it depends" worth being able to defend: on a low-latency link with fat result sets, split query; on a high-latency link, single query with projection so there is nothing to explode. The general form is that every ORM performance knob is a trade between round trips and payload size, and which one is scarce depends on where the components are.

At what scale does the memory cache for lookup tables become the problem?

Three ways. Staleness first: an in-process cache with a five-minute expiry means up to five minutes of inconsistency, and across ten instances it means ten different versions of the truth at once — fine for a supplier name, unacceptable for a price or a permission. Memory second: it is per-instance, so it multiplies by instance count, and a lookup table that grows with tenants will eventually be the reason your pods restart. And the stampede: when a popular entry expires under load, every instance reloads it simultaneously, so you want a coalescing or lock-per-key pattern rather than a plain expiry. The point at which I would move to a distributed cache is when consistency across instances starts to matter — and I would want to be honest that this adds a network hop back, which is the very thing we were trying to remove.

The real cause was splitting the app from its database. Would you ever do that deliberately?

Only with a latency budget written down and the hot paths verified against it, and even then only for a short, dated period. There are legitimate reasons to phase — a database migration window you cannot get, a regulatory constraint on data location, or a licence — but the mistake is treating the split as a neutral sequencing choice rather than as an architectural change with a 100x cost on one dimension. If I had to do it again I would either co-locate compute with the database until the move (running the app on-premises against the on-premises database, and migrating both together), or accept the split only after profiling the top twenty endpoints for round-trip count. The general rule I would give a team: never let a migration plan separate two components that talk to each other synchronously, dozens of times per request, unless you have measured what happens when the wire between them gets a hundred times longer.

What if the code had been someone else's and you could not change it?

Then you are down to topology and interception, in that order. Topology: put the compute next to the data, which for a lift-and-shift usually means moving the database first or hosting the app where the database is until both can move — unglamorous and almost always the correct answer. Interception: a read replica local to the app for read-heavy paths, or a caching layer in front of the data access if the consistency model tolerates it. What I would not do is try to fix chattiness with bandwidth or with a faster database, because neither touches the variable that changed. And I would resist a connection-level fix like multiple active result sets or aggressive pooling; those help with concurrency, not with a serial chain of 1,240 dependent round trips, and reaching for them is a sign the diagnosis has not landed.

What the interviewer is really probing. Incident command under real stakes, and whether you can size damage before you escalate. They want the sequence — contain, quantify, communicate, remediate — and the recognition that this failure was created by the migration's coexistence period, not by a bug in either system. The common failures: a story about the clever diagnosis with no mention of customers; escalating without a number; fixing only the flag; and no acknowledgement that leaving a disabled duplicate deployed was a decision someone made.

(b) STAR/CARL scaffold

Situation
An out-of-hours incident with customer money involved, caused by two versions of the same job running during a migration. One line: what fired, when, what was at stake.
Task
Yours: contain first, quantify second, communicate third — and own the coexistence decision that made it possible.
Action
Stopping both rather than the suspect one; the join against the provider's log that produced a number; who you woke and what you gave them; the reversal and the proactive customer contact; the three-layer remediation.
Result
Numbers: duplicates, customers, value, time to containment, time to reversal, complaints received. And the structural fix, not just the flag.
Differently
Undeploy the old component when the rollback window closes. Disabled-but-deployed is armed, and a flag is one configuration mistake away from firing it.

(c) Recall prompts

What is the worst thing you have been paged for, and what did you do in the first ten minutes?

Hint: the first ten minutes are the whole answer. Interviewers listen for containment before diagnosis, and for whether you escalated with information or with alarm.

Have you ever had two versions of something running at once?

Hint: a scheduled job, a queue consumer, a cron on an old VM, a cache warmer, a webhook handler, a sync process. This is the signature hazard of every coexistence period and almost everyone who has done a migration has one.

When have you had to tell customers something had gone wrong before they noticed?

Hint: a duplicate charge, a wrong email, a data exposure, an incorrect statement. Recall the decision to go proactive, who made it, and how long it took — the delay is usually where the story is.

What is still deployed in your estate that should have been removed?

Hint: an old service disabled by a flag, a decommissioned endpoint still routed, a VM kept "just in case", an obsolete pipeline that can still deploy, a database still accepting writes. Every one of these is a live incident waiting for a configuration mistake.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why stop both jobs rather than the one you suspected?

Because at 03:12 my confidence was maybe 80%, and the cost matrix was wildly asymmetric. Stopping both meant payment retries paused for an hour, which delays some customers' successful payments — annoying, fully recoverable, and invisible to nearly everyone at that time of night. Stopping the wrong one meant the damage continued while I believed it had stopped, which is the worst state to be in because it also destroys your ability to reason about what you are seeing. That is a general incident heuristic I would defend: when the failure is ambiguous and the reversible cost of over-containment is low, over-contain. Where the heuristic inverts is when stopping is itself dangerous — you do not stop both replicas of a database because one is misbehaving.

Idempotency keys would have prevented this. Why did the old code not have them?

Because it was written in 2014 against a provider API that did not offer them, and nobody revisited it when the provider added support. That is the ordinary way this happens — not negligence, but a component that stopped being read. The deeper point for a migration is that the coexistence period puts old code into a situation its authors never contemplated: it was written on the assumption it was the only thing consuming that queue, and that assumption was true for eleven years. So the durable lesson is to audit the assumptions of the old component when you introduce a second one, not just to test the new one. We ended up writing down, for each dual-run component, what it assumed about exclusivity, ordering and retries — and two others had similar gaps we fixed before they fired.

You added a distributed lock. What happens when the lock service is unavailable?

Nothing runs, and that is the deliberate choice for this workload. A blob lease acquisition that fails — for any reason, including the storage account being unreachable — results in the job logging and exiting, so the failure mode is delayed retries rather than duplicate charges. That is only correct because payment retries are latency-tolerant; for a job where not running is the dangerous outcome, I would choose the opposite bias and accept the risk of concurrent execution, provided the downstream work is idempotent. This is the honest form of the answer: mutual exclusion and availability are in tension, you pick which way to fail per workload, and you write down why. What I would not accept is a lock whose failure mode is undefined because nobody thought about it — that is the worst of both.

At what scale does a blob-lease lock stop being adequate?

It is fine for singleton-role election at low frequency, which is what a retry job is, and it starts to strain in three ways. Contention: leases are acquired at a coarse granularity, so a system needing thousands of fine-grained locks per second wants something built for that. Clock and renewal: a lease has a duration, so a process that stalls — a long GC pause, a paused container — can believe it holds a lease that has expired, which reintroduces the exact split brain you were preventing; the mitigation is fencing tokens so downstream work rejects a stale holder, and idempotency underneath as the real safety net. And multi-region: a single storage account is a regional dependency, so global exclusivity needs a consensus system rather than a lease. My general position is that a lock is a performance optimisation on top of idempotency, never a substitute for it — if correctness depends only on the lock, the design is wrong.

Who decided to leave the old service deployed, and how do you write that in a postmortem?

I did, and I would write exactly that. The decision was defensible when it was made — we kept it as a fast rollback for the first week of the new job — and it became wrong when the rollback window closed and nobody had a trigger to remove it. So the postmortem finding is not "a config change reset a flag", it is "we had no mechanism to retire a rollback option, so it aged into a hazard", and the action is a dated removal task created at the same moment the fallback is created. Naming myself matters here for a specific reason: it is the only way the finding reads as a system problem rather than as someone else's carelessness, and in a payments incident the postmortem is read by people looking for whether the team is honest. The three-week-from-deletion argument for not fixing the legacy idempotency is the one I am least proud of and I put it in writing too.

21The big behavioural Q&A

24 cross-cutting prompts not tied to one story

These are the prompts that do not map to a single incident — the ones where the interviewer is sampling your values, your judgment and your self-knowledge. Each answer below names the prepared story to reach for and what the question is actually measuring. Do not memorise the wording; memorise which story you are deploying and what it has to demonstrate. Modernization interviews lean unusually hard on the last two themes, because a company hiring for this work has usually been burned by a migration before and is checking whether you will do it to them again.

Failure & ownership

Tell me about a time a migration you led went wrong.

Deploy: story 03 (the cutover and the connection pool) for an operational failure, or story 05 (the collation) for a data one. They want: a real failure with your name on it, a clear containment decision, and a systemic change afterwards. The disqualifier is a failure that is secretly a strength ("we were too ambitious"). Say the cost in concrete terms — minutes of degradation, rows damaged, money — then what you changed about how you decide, not about how hard you try.

What is the biggest mistake you have made in a technical decision?

Deploy: story 04's deferred COM retirement, or story 20's "it is going away in three weeks so we will not fix it". They want: that you can distinguish a bad decision from a bad outcome. Explain what you knew at the time, why the decision was reasonable on that information, and the specific signal you would now go looking for earlier. The strongest version admits the reasoning was lazy rather than merely unlucky.

Tell me about something you shipped that you had to roll back.

Deploy: story 03. They want: the abort decision and who owned it. Include the fifteen minutes you spent debugging when you should have been rolling back — that admission is the most credible sentence available to you, and it sets up the mechanism you adopted afterwards, which is the point of the story.

When did you realise you were the bottleneck?

Deploy: story 01 (reviewing every migration PR yourself) or story 16 (correcting in review instead of teaching). They want: self-awareness about the lead-engineer failure mode of doing the work rather than multiplying it. Name the moment, the metric that showed it (queue time, PRs waiting), and what you handed over — with the checklist or artefact that made handing over possible.

Conflict & influence

Tell me about a technical disagreement you lost.

Deploy: the release-cadence half of story 07, where the colleague was right and the modular monolith did not solve it. They want: calibration and grace. Describe what they saw that you did not, what changed in your view, and — the part most candidates omit — how you supported the decision afterwards. Losing an argument and then quietly undermining the outcome is the thing they are screening for.

How do you get another team to do work that only benefits you?

Deploy: story 12 (partners with their own change windows) or story 10 (a platform deadline nobody else feels). They want: influence without authority — reframing the benefit in their currency, doing the first migration yourself, automating away their effort, and attaching a credible external date. "I escalated to their manager" as the primary tool is a weak answer; as a last resort with a written risk, it is a strong one.

Tell me about pushing back on someone more senior.

Deploy: story 19 (fourteen months) or story 10 (the November deadline). They want: that you can say no without becoming an obstacle, by changing what is being estimated rather than arguing the number, and by bringing what the stakeholder actually needs. Have the boundary ready too: the point at which you stop persuading, write the risk down, and name who is accepting it.

How do you handle an engineer who keeps working the old way?

Deploy: story 16, including the person who moved to the legacy platform. They want: the instinct to ask why before applying pressure, and evidence you can have a fit conversation kindly and early. Admit you waited three months; everyone does, and being able to say so is what separates someone who has actually managed this from someone describing a policy.

Leadership & mentoring

How do you bring a team through a change they did not choose?

Deploy: story 13 (the cancelled rewrite) for the hardest version, or story 16 for the everyday one. They want: that you lead with the reasoning rather than the verdict, give people a role in what comes next, and handle the individuals before the group. The salvage number — "a third of the work carries straight over" — is the transferable technique.

Tell me about someone you levelled up.

Deploy: story 16, specifically the domain expert who became the strongest reviewer. They want: a named mechanism and evidence it stuck — they started catching things in others' code, or taught it onward. Avoid making yourself the hero of someone else's growth; describe what they brought that you did not have.

How do you decide what the team works on when everything is urgent?

Deploy: story 10's bucketing, plus the standing 15% allocation for lifecycle work. They want: a repeatable method — dated external constraints first, then risk, then value — and the discipline to hold capacity for work that has no sponsor. Naming the standing allocation is the detail that reads as experience rather than theory.

What does good technical leadership look like on a legacy system?

Deploy: a synthesis — story 01's seam, story 08's evidence, story 18's deletion protocol. They want: a philosophy with mechanisms attached. Mine: make the safe path the easy one, make the invisible visible, prefer reversible steps, and never let "temporary" exist without a date. Say it in three sentences and then give one example each, or it sounds like a poster.

Ambiguity & prioritisation

Tell me about a project with no clear requirements.

Deploy: story 08. They want: that you generate evidence rather than seek consensus, and that you can decide what does not need building. The strongest line: "I stopped asking people what the system did and measured what it actually served" — followed by the three passes and what each one found that the others missed.

How do you decide what to migrate first?

Deploy: story 01 (order and the plateau) and story 17 (the sequencing mistake). They want: a defensible principle plus awareness of its cost. Either "lowest risk first, to prove the machinery" or "highest pain first, to prove the value" is fine; what is not fine is having no principle, or not knowing that the hard remainder needs sizing separately from the long tail.

How do you handle a requirement that changes mid-migration?

Deploy: story 13, where the product kept evolving and invalidated the plan. They want: that you check the assumption your plan rests on rather than absorbing change silently. The answer they are hoping for names the assumption explicitly and describes the trigger you put in place to re-examine it.

Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without enough information.

Deploy: story 03's rollback at 80% confidence, or story 20's stopping both jobs. They want: comfort with asymmetric cost reasoning — over-contain when the reversible cost is low. Say the confidence level out loud; "I was about 80% sure" is a phrase very few candidates use and it signals that you actually think in those terms.

Legacy, risk & judgment

When is it right not to modernize something?

Deploy: story 04 (the pricing engine still running) and story 12 (SOAP preserved for partners). They want: that you are not an enthusiast. The strong answer names the conditions — rarely changed, rarely invoked, contained blast radius, acceptable support position — and pairs them with the ongoing cost you make visible so the decision is revisited rather than forgotten.

How do you decide between rewrite, replatform and wrap?

Deploy: story 04's table, with the replay experiment. They want: that you make it empirical. The reusable structure is: what is the acceptance criterion for "the same", what experiment would tell us, and which dependency constrains the whole decision. Naming the dependency is the seniority signal.

What do you do about a critical system nobody understands?

Deploy: story 08 plus story 18's probe protocol. They want: evidence-gathering rather than either heroics or paralysis. Include the near-miss (the annual auditor export), because it proves you actually did this rather than describing an approach.

How do you keep a modernization from becoming a permanent second system?

Deploy: story 01's coexistence tax and story 20's disabled-but-deployed service. They want: awareness that coexistence is a cost with a half-life, and specific mechanisms — a dated deletion for every temporary component, a burn-down that separates the easy tail from the hard remainder, and a visible price tag on the legacy that survives. The failure mode you are proving you can avoid is a company that has been "migrating" for four years.

Values & motivation

Why do you want to work on legacy modernization? Most people want greenfield.

Deploy: no story — this is a genuine question and it deserves a genuine answer. They want: to know you will still be here in eighteen months. The honest reasons that land: the constraints are real, so the engineering is harder than greenfield; the systems matter, because nobody funds a migration for something nobody uses; and the work is unusually legible — you can measure what you improved. Do not claim to love it unconditionally; say what you find tedious too.

What is your relationship with new technology?

Deploy: story 06 (the AI modernization tooling) and story 11 (TypeScript 7 with an ecosystem constraint). They want: adoption with judgment. Show that you moved quickly on something recent and that you named what you would not let it do. A candidate who is either dismissive or uncritical about AI-assisted modernization in 2026 is a risk in different directions.

How do you keep current?

Deploy: no story — have three specifics ready. They want: evidence rather than a claim. Name a lifecycle date you are tracking and why (.NET 8 and 9 end of support on 10 November 2026), one thing you changed your mind about recently, and one thing you have actually built with something new. Vague answers about newsletters are worse than saying "mostly by upgrading things and reading the breaking-changes list", which at least is true and specific.

What would you do in your first ninety days on our legacy system?

Deploy: a synthesis of stories 08, 09, 10 and 18. They want: a plan that starts with evidence and produces something visible early. Mine: weeks 1–3, inventory and dates — what runs, what calls it, what lifecycle deadlines are coming; weeks 4–8, one visible improvement that proves the machinery, plus the safety net (tests, telemetry, cost and CI); weeks 9–12, a sequenced plan with a named coexistence mechanism and a decommission date. Say explicitly that you would not propose an architecture in week one, because the strongest signal here is restraint.

22Night-before cheat sheet

10 speakable rules read this last

Ten sentences to read the night before, and to say out loud once each so they are in your mouth rather than on the page.

  1. Open with the situation in one line, then stop. "A fourteen-year-old WebForms application, 180 pages, 40 developers, still shipping weekly" buys you thirty seconds of full attention; three minutes of programme background spends it. Modernization stories are long by nature, so the discipline to compress the setup matters more here than in any other topic.
  2. Anchor on one decision, one week, or one night. Nobody can follow a two-year narrative. Give the programme as context in a sentence, then tell the story of the cutover, the argument, the page at 03:12, or the moment you cancelled it.
  3. Name the coexistence mechanism. Shared session, a shared key ring, a routing proxy, a dual-write, an idempotency key, a distributed lock. This one detail separates people who have run a migration from people who have watched one, and interviewers know it.
  4. Quantify both sides of every result. Deployments per week and the coexistence cost; latency and memory; money saved and what you gave up. A modernization answer with only upside in it reads as a sales pitch.
  5. Separate "I" from "we" deliberately and out loud. Say what you personally decided, wrote or argued, and hand the rest to the team by name. These are inherently team-scale programmes and interviewers are calibrated for inflation on exactly this.
  6. Own a failure in the same breath as the fix. The fifteen minutes you should have spent rolling back, the collation change you merged into the same release, the review where you approved the swallowed exception, the three months you avoided a conversation. A migration story with no mistake in it sounds rehearsed, because real migrations are made of them.
  7. Have a date in your pocket, and use it once. .NET 8 and .NET 9 both end support on 10 November 2026 — the same day the Azure Functions in-process model retires; Windows Server 2016 extended support ends 12 January 2027; TypeScript 7.0 went GA on 8 July 2026 and its programmatic API is still landing. One date, used once, correctly — not a recital.
  8. Say what you deliberately did not modernize, and what it costs. The pricing engine still on Framework, the SOAP endpoint kept for three partners, the eleven functions accepted into January. Restraint with a visible price tag is the single strongest signal available in this topic.
  9. Give the trade-off in one sentence, every time. "That bought us X and cost us Y" — reversibility for calendar time, memory for tail latency, round trips for payload size, exclusivity for availability. Decisions without costs signal someone who has read about this rather than shipped it.
  10. Finish with what you would do differently, and make it a change of method. Not "I would be more careful" but "I now write the abort criteria before the change starts and give them to someone else", "I now change one dimension per migration", "I now put a deletion date on every temporary component".

The last check before you walk in. Pick five stories from the coverage table that are genuinely yours, and for each one be able to say in ten seconds: the situation, the number, the trade-off, and the thing you would do differently. Make sure those five span at least three archetypes and include one failure and one thing you chose not to do. Five stories you can tell cold beat twenty you half-remember, and the drill chains are what let you improvise the sixth.
