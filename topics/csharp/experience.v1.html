01The 40 ms p99 that was all garbage

Senior performance problem diagnosed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We had a pricing endpoint that everyone described as "fine on average". The mean was 6 ms; the p99 was 40 ms and it moved around during the day for no reason anyone could name. The first useful thing I did was stop looking at the endpoint and start looking at the process: dotnet-counters monitor --counters System.Runtime on one pod showed gen-0 collections at about 220 per second, an allocation rate near 1.4 GB per minute, and % Time in GC sitting around 18%. That is the shape of a latency problem that is not in your code at all — it is in the pauses between your code.

The mechanism matters here, and it is the thing I would want a candidate to explain to me. The .NET GC is generational: gen 0 is a small ephemeral segment, a gen-0 collection is genuinely cheap, and people conclude from that that allocation is free. It is not. Every gen-0 collection has to suspend the managed threads, walk the roots, and — the expensive part at high rates — promote whatever is still alive. At 220 collections a second, objects that live for the duration of one request are alive at collection time, get promoted to gen 1, then to gen 2, and now you are paying for a gen-2 collection you never budgeted for. The p99 was not slow work; it was fast work that happened to be running when the world stopped.

I took a 60-second trace with dotnet-trace collect --profile gc-verbose and looked at allocations by type. The top three were not domain objects. They were compiler-generated: <>c__DisplayClass instances (closures), WhereSelectListIterator instances (LINQ), and string. All three came from one 40-line method that ran per request, per line item — roughly 30 times per call.

// BEFORE: three allocations per item, times 30 items, times 900 rps
public decimal Total(IReadOnlyList<LineItem> items, Customer customer)
{
    var applicable = items
        .Where(i => i.RegionCode == customer.RegionCode)   // closure captures 'customer' -> display class
        .Select(i => i.Net * RateFor(i.TaxClass));         // second closure, second iterator
    var total = applicable.Sum();
    _log.LogDebug($"total for {customer.Id} = {total}");   // interpolation runs even when Debug is off
    return total;
}

The fixes were unglamorous and each one is a teachable rule. The Where/Select pair became a single for loop over the IReadOnlyList<T>, which removes both the iterator objects and the closures — the loop reads customer from the parameter instead of capturing it. Where I kept LINQ off the hot path, I made the lambdas static (C# 9) so the compiler physically cannot let me capture state by accident, and used the state-passing overloads where the BCL offers them. The interpolated log line became _log.LogDebug("total for {CustomerId} = {Total}", customer.Id, total), which defers formatting to the logger and skips it entirely when Debug is disabled — that one line was 11% of the allocation volume, because interpolation boxed the decimal and built a string that was then thrown away. Finally, RateFor was doing _rates[$"{taxClass}:{region}"]; a Dictionary<(TaxClass, string), decimal> keyed on a value tuple removed the per-call string.

Result: allocation rate fell to about 90 MB/min, gen 0 to roughly 18 collections/second, % Time in GC to under 3%, and the p99 landed at 11 ms. The mean barely moved — which is the point, and the sentence I would lead with in an interview.

What I would do differently: I spent two days reading code before I took a trace. I now treat "measure the process, then the method, then the line" as non-negotiable order. I would also mention that on .NET 10 part of this class of problem fixes itself — the JIT's expanded escape analysis stack-allocates objects it can prove do not escape the method, including some of exactly these short-lived closure and iterator objects — but I would never lead with that in a review, because it is an optimisation you cannot rely on for a specific line of code, only observe in aggregate.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can separate throughput from latency, and whether you measure before you change. The signal they want: a named tool, a number before, a number after, and a mechanism that explains why the change worked. The classic failure is the candidate who lists micro-optimisations as folklore ("LINQ is slow, structs are fast") with no trace, no baseline, and no idea which of the changes actually paid. The second failure is over-claiming: if six people worked on it, say which part was yours.

(c) Recall prompts

When did you last look at a .NET process's GC counters rather than its request logs?

Sketch: any incident where the symptom was jitter rather than slowness. If you have never run dotnet-counters, you probably have the equivalent from an APM dashboard — Application Insights, Datadog and Dynatrace all surface gen-0/1/2 counts and % time in GC. That counts; name the tool you actually used.

What is the largest single allocation source you have ever removed, and how did you find it?

Sketch: logging, serialisation, and string building are the three that come up most often in real systems. A gcdump comparison ("two snapshots 10 minutes apart, this type grew by 400 MB") is a very strong, very concrete answer.

Have you ever optimised something that turned out not to matter?

Sketch: this is the humility question in disguise, and interviewers love a specific answer. "I rewrote a hot loop with spans, measured 2% and reverted it, because the actual cost was a synchronous DNS lookup in the same method."

Where in your codebase would you expect the LOH to bite, and have you seen it?

Sketch: anything over 85,000 bytes — big byte[] for file or image handling, large string results, oversized List<T> growth. The tell is gen-2 collections that track upload size rather than request rate.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just give the pods more memory and move on?

Because the problem was not the heap size, it was the collection rate, and a bigger heap does not reduce how often gen 0 fills. In practice a larger budget delays gen-2 collections and makes each one longer, so you trade a frequent 3 ms pause for a rarer 60 ms pause — which is worse for a tail-latency SLO. More memory was also the change we could not un-make cheaply: the cluster was already over-provisioned, and adding headroom would have hidden the regression from the next person. I would give it more memory if the profile said the working set was genuinely large and long-lived, but that is a different diagnosis with a different counter behind it.

What if the allocations had been unavoidable — a genuinely large working set?

Then I would stop trying to allocate less and start controlling when the cost is paid. Options in rough order of intrusiveness: pool the buffers with ArrayPool<T> so the same memory is reused; move the hot data into pre-sized arrays or FrozenDictionary so it is allocated once at startup and promoted to gen 2 deliberately; and, on Server GC, check whether DATAS — on by default for Server GC since .NET 9 — is helping or hurting, because it deliberately trades a smaller heap for more frequent collections and can be turned off per workload. If the data really is per-request and really is big, the honest answer is that the service needs a streaming design, not a GC tweak.

At what scale does the fix you made stop working?

It scales with request rate but not with item count. The for-loop rewrite removes a constant number of allocations per call, so at 900 rps it was worth roughly a gigabyte a minute; at 90 rps it would have been noise and I would not have bothered. It breaks down when the per-request working set itself grows — if a customer sends 30,000 line items instead of 30, the loop is still allocation-free but the intermediate list is not, and I would have to stream through the items and aggregate rather than materialise them. That is the version of this I would build first if I were designing it today with the volumes we ended up at.

What did you trade away?

Readability, honestly. A Where/Select/Sum chain says what it means; the hand-rolled loop does not, and it is easier to get wrong when someone adds a rule to it. I bounded that trade: the loop is in one method, that method has a comment saying why it is written that way with the measured number attached, and there is a BenchmarkDotNet test in the repo that fails if someone reintroduces the allocation. Everywhere else in that service LINQ is still the default, and I pushed back on the follow-up ticket that proposed "de-LINQ-ing" the whole codebase.

How did you get the team to agree that this was the right fix?

I did not argue it, I showed it. I put the before/after counter graph and the BenchmarkDotNet table in the pull request, and I wrote down the rule we were adopting — hot path means measured hot path, not suspected hot path — so the change was a precedent rather than a one-off. The disagreement I actually had was about scope: one engineer wanted the same treatment applied to the whole service, and I said no on the grounds that we had a measurement for exactly one method and none for the rest. We agreed to add the counters to the dashboard and revisit with data.

Would you do it the same way on .NET 10 today?

The diagnosis, yes, exactly the same. The fixes, mostly — but I would check what the runtime now does for me before hand-writing anything. .NET 10's JIT does more escape analysis and will stack-allocate some short-lived objects that used to hit gen 0, and it can devirtualise and inline array interface methods, so a chunk of the classic "LINQ over an array is slow" folklore is measurably less true than it was on .NET 6. That is an argument for re-measuring on the target runtime rather than for skipping the work: escape analysis is an optimisation, not a guarantee, and it disappears the moment a reference leaks into a field or a lambda.

02Immutable domain, mutable ORM

Staff hard architectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were building the second version of a policy-administration domain — the kind where a single object accumulates twenty state transitions over its life and every transition has to be auditable. The team wanted the domain model immutable: record types, init-only properties, transitions expressed as with expressions returning a new instance. I agreed with the goal and spent three weeks discovering that the goal and the persistence layer wanted different things, which is the actual story.

The clean version worked beautifully in the domain project. A record gives you value equality, a synthesised ToString, a Deconstruct, and a non-destructive with — and value equality alone removed a whole category of test noise, because asserting on a whole aggregate became one line. The problem is that value equality on a record is shallow: the compiler-generated Equals compares each field with EqualityComparer<T>.Default, so a record holding a List<Endorsement> compares that list by reference. Two policies with identical endorsements were "not equal", and the bug that surfaced it was a caching layer that never got a hit. That is not a records problem, it is a "you did not read what the compiler generated" problem, and it is the first thing I now check in review.

// The trap: value equality that isn't
public record Policy(PolicyId Id, List<Endorsement> Endorsements);   // List compares by reference

// What we settled on: a collection type with the equality we actually meant
public sealed record Policy(PolicyId Id, ImmutableArray<Endorsement> Endorsements)
{
    public bool Equals(Policy? other) =>
        other is not null && Id == other.Id && Endorsements.SequenceEqual(other.Endorsements);
    public override int GetHashCode() => Id.GetHashCode();   // stable: identity only
}

The second collision was the ORM. EF Core can map a type with init-only properties and can bind through a constructor, but change tracking fundamentally wants to mutate an instance it is holding, and the identity map wants reference identity for an entity — while a record's whole point is that identity is its values. Mapping the aggregate root as a record meant every load produced an entity whose Equals the tracker could not rely on, and every with produced an object the tracker had never seen. We could have fought it with explicit Attach/Entry().State calls, and I prototyped that for a week. It worked and it was miserable, because every future developer would have to know the trick.

The decision I made — and this is the trade-off I would defend in an interview — was to stop asking one type to do both jobs. Persistence entities are ordinary sealed classes with private setters and a private parameterless constructor: mutable, reference-identity, boring, exactly what EF wants. The domain model is records, built from the entity at the edge of the repository and mapped back on save. It costs a mapping layer, roughly 400 lines of hand-written mapping in that service, and it means a change to a field touches two files. What it buys is that neither model has to lie about what it is.

What I would do differently: I would draw that table on the first day instead of the twentieth. The three weeks were not wasted — the prototype is what convinced the team — but I let the discussion run as "records versus classes", which is a religious argument, when the real question was "which type owns identity". I would also push harder on strongly-typed ids from the start; readonly record struct PolicyId(Guid Value) with an EF ValueConverter cost almost nothing and removed an entire class of "passed the wrong Guid" bugs.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can hold two competing forces in your head and choose, rather than reciting that immutability is good. They want to hear the cost of your choice stated out loud and owned. The failure mode is the candidate who describes a trade-off with no downside — if your design had no cost, you did not make a decision, you had a preference. The second failure is inventing team consensus; say who disagreed and how it resolved.

Where in your code does a type serve two masters — domain and persistence, or domain and wire format?

Sketch: EF entities returned straight from a controller, a DTO reused as a domain object, a protobuf-generated class used as a business type. Every one of those is a story about a trade-off you either made deliberately or inherited.

Sketch: caches that never hit, Distinct() that removed nothing, unit tests that passed because two references happened to be the same object. Any of these is a memorable 30-second story that also proves you know what the compiler generates.

Sketch: strongly-typed ids, money, coordinates, small keys. The good answer names a size threshold and a measurement, not a rule of thumb.

Sketch: hand-written mapping, a mapping library, or a source-generated mapper. The interesting part is the maintenance story — what happens when someone adds a field and forgets one side.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not use records for the entities and just teach the team the Attach tricks?

Because the trick has to be known by everyone who touches the repository, forever, and it fails silently — you do not get an exception, you get an update that did not happen. I have a strong bias against designs whose correctness depends on tribal knowledge that the compiler cannot check. If I could have expressed the rule as an analyzer or a base class that made the wrong thing impossible, I would have reconsidered; I could not, so I moved the complexity into a mapping layer where a missing field is at least a visible line of code.

What if the team had been five people who all knew EF deeply?

Then the calculus genuinely changes and I would probably have gone the other way for the small aggregates, because the mapping layer is a real cost paid on every change. My decision was as much about the team's composition and turnover as about the types: we were onboarding two people a quarter. That is a legitimate input to an architecture decision and I would say so explicitly — the same codebase with a stable senior team is a different design problem.

At what scale does the mapping layer break down?

It breaks on breadth, not depth. Four hundred lines across nine aggregates was maintainable; the same pattern across ninety aggregates would not be, and the failure mode is drift — a field added to the entity and not to the domain model, which no test catches because the test fixtures were written against one side. If I were at that scale I would generate the mapping rather than write it, and I would add a test that reflects over both types and fails on an unmapped property. I would also seriously consider EF's complex types and owned entities to shrink how many aggregates need mapping at all.

What did you trade away by making the domain model immutable at all?

Allocations, mainly, and a little cognitive load. Every with is a fresh heap object, so a hot loop that walks twenty transitions allocates twenty aggregates; that was irrelevant at our volumes and would not be in a tight processing loop. The second cost is that people new to the codebase write policy.With(...) and drop the result, which the compiler will not stop for a method call — we caught two of those in review before adding an analyzer for discarded pure returns. I would keep the trade: the audit requirement made "the old value still exists" worth real money.

How would you do this on C# 14, and what is coming that changes it?

On C# 14 the shape is the same but the value objects get cheaper to write: field-backed properties let a validating property keep a simple auto-property syntax without a hand-written backing field, and extension members let me hang domain operations off primitives without wrapper types. The thing I am genuinely watching is C# 15's union types, in preview in the .NET 11 SDK today with GA expected in November 2026 — a closed set of transition cases is exactly what my sealed-record hierarchy is emulating by hand, and I would revisit that model once it is stable. I would not build on it now, and I would say the same to a team that asked.

03Nullable was on. The nulls came anyway

Senior production incident / outage

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

At 09:40 on a Tuesday an order-intake service started throwing NullReferenceException from a method whose parameter was, according to the compiler, not nullable. Over eleven minutes it went from a trickle to 100% of one partner's traffic; the retry policy turned that into a poison-message loop that filled the dead-letter queue. I was incident lead. The bit worth telling is not the fix, it is that the codebase had <Nullable>enable</Nullable> everywhere and zero warnings, and people had concluded from that that nulls were impossible.

They are not. Nullable reference types are a compile-time flow analysis and nothing else. There is no runtime check, the annotations survive into metadata only as attributes, and any value that enters the process without passing through the compiler's analysis — deserialization, reflection, a dynamic proxy, interop, or a library compiled in a nullable-oblivious context — can put a null into a non-nullable reference and nothing will complain. Our path was the most common one: JsonSerializer.Deserialize<OrderRequest>. The partner had shipped a release that stopped emitting shippingAddress; the property was declared public Address ShippingAddress { get; init; }, non-nullable, and the serializer happily left it null because it had no instruction to do otherwise.

// The lie: the type says non-null, the serializer disagrees
public sealed class OrderRequest
{
    public string OrderRef { get; init; }        // CS8618 suppressed years ago by "= null!;"
    public Address ShippingAddress { get; init; }
}

// The fix, in three layers
public sealed class OrderRequest
{
    public required string OrderRef { get; init; }        // C# 11: the compiler enforces initialisation
    public required Address ShippingAddress { get; init; }
}

var options = new JsonSerializerOptions
{
    RespectNullableAnnotations = true,        // .NET 9: a null for a non-nullable member now throws
    RespectRequiredConstructorParameters = true,
};
// .NET 10 bundles this family of settings behind JsonSerializerOptions.Strict.

Mitigation first: we shipped a validation guard at the endpoint within 25 minutes that rejected the payload with a 400 instead of dying at layer five, which turned a 500-and-retry loop into a clean rejection the partner could see. The real fix went out the same afternoon — required members on every inbound contract, and RespectNullableAnnotations on the serializer options so the model and the wire format could no longer disagree silently. Then the boring, valuable part: I grepped for = null!; and !. across the solution. There were 214 occurrences. Most were legitimate; about thirty were exactly this pattern, a nullable warning silenced at a trust boundary because the deadline was closer than the risk.

What I would do differently: the postmortem action I am proudest of is not technical. We changed the definition of "nullable is enabled" for the team from "the build is warning-free" to "every trust boundary validates", and I wrote the boundary list above into the service template. What I got wrong in the moment was communication — I spent the first six minutes debugging instead of declaring, and the partner found out from their own dashboard rather than from us.

What the interviewer is really probing. Incident-command judgment first, technical depth second. They want the order of operations — stabilise, communicate, then fix — and they want to hear you separate mitigation from remediation. The failures are: telling the debugging story as a detective thriller with no mention of the customer; blaming the partner; and, at senior level and above, having no systemic follow-up. "We fixed the null" is a mid answer. "We fixed the null and changed what 'nullable is enabled' means to this team" is the level they are hiring for.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the guard clause go out before the real fix?

Because the guard was reversible, testable in five minutes, and stopped customer harm; the model change touched fourteen contracts and needed a partner conversation. In an incident I optimise for the smallest change that stops the bleeding and can be rolled back without a migration — that is a different objective from the change that makes the system correct, and conflating them is how a ten-minute incident becomes an hour. I said that explicitly on the bridge so nobody thought the guard was the fix.

What if turning on RespectNullableAnnotations had broken other partners?

It could have, and that was the reason I did not just flip it globally at 09:50. Making it throw converts a silent null into a hard rejection, which is correct but is a behaviour change for every caller. We shipped it per-contract behind the options used by the intake endpoint, ran a week of shadow logging that recorded what would have been rejected, and only then widened it. That log turned up two other partners omitting optional fields, which we then modelled as genuinely nullable rather than pretending.

At what scale does "validate at the boundary" stop being enough?

When the number of boundaries stops being countable — many services, many contracts, many teams. At that point per-endpoint validation drifts, and you need it generated from the contract rather than written by hand: a schema (OpenAPI or protobuf) as the source of truth, with the C# types generated from it so that a field's optionality is one fact, not two. The other scaling limit is versioning: once two versions of a contract are live simultaneously, "required" is a per-version property and a single model class cannot express it honestly.

What did you trade away with required members everywhere?

Flexibility in object construction and a little friction in tests. required is enforced at every construction site, so every test fixture and every mapper has to set every required member, and adding a required member is a source-breaking change for consumers of a shared library — which is a feature at a trust boundary and an annoyance inside one assembly. I did not apply it to internal DTOs for that reason. The other cost is that required is a C# 11 feature, so a consumer stuck on an older language version cannot even initialise the object, which mattered for one internal package.

How would you prevent the whole class of problem on .NET 10?

By making the strict behaviour the default in the service template rather than a decision each team makes. .NET 10 folds this family of options — respecting nullable annotations, respecting required constructor parameters, rejecting duplicate properties and unmapped members — into a strict preset for JsonSerializerOptions, so a new service starts from "the wire format must match the model" and opts out deliberately. I would pair that with a source-generated serializer context, which turns the contract into code you can actually read at review time, and with contract tests running against the partner's published schema in CI so a change like theirs fails our pipeline before it reaches production.

Whose fault was it?

Ours. The partner changed their payload, which they are allowed to do, and we had no contract test and no validation that would have caught it — the annotation was a comment we had convinced ourselves was a check. I said that in the postmortem in those words. Blaming an upstream for a change we did not defend against is the fastest way to make sure the same class of incident happens again, and it is also the answer that tells an interviewer whether you run blameless postmortems or performative ones.

Around the point where you cannot land a change in every repository yourself. At eleven services and one owning team, I could drive it. 05Exceptions or results: the boundary argument

Senior technical disagreement with a colleague

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A senior engineer on my team opened a pull request that replaced every thrown exception in a new module with a hand-rolled Result<T, Error> type. He had good reasons — the module was a validation pipeline, the "failures" were expected business outcomes rather than bugs, and he had measured a throw at roughly 8 microseconds against a returned struct at nanoseconds. I disagreed with the scope, not the idea, and the argument ran for two days across a PR thread before I moved it to a whiteboard, which is the part of the story I would actually tell.

The technical content is the part people get wrong under drill-down, so it is worth being precise. A C# exception is expensive because of stack unwinding and, mostly, because capturing the stack trace walks frames and allocates; the throw itself is not the cost, the first-chance handling and the trace are. That cost is irrelevant at 40 exceptions a second and catastrophic at 40,000 — the crossover is not a principle, it is a rate. The other half is the language: C# has no checked exceptions, so a method's signature does not tell you what it can throw, while Result<T> puts the failure in the type and the compiler makes you deal with it. That is a genuine expressiveness win. The cost is that Result is viral — every caller up the stack either handles it or propagates it — and C# has no ? operator, no monadic bind sugar, and (until unions ship) no closed type to pattern-match exhaustively, so the propagation is hand-written and easy to drop.

// The shape we agreed on: results inside the module, exceptions at the seams
public readonly record struct Result<T>(T? Value, ValidationError? Error)
{
    public bool IsOk => Error is null;
    public static Result<T> Ok(T value) => new(value, null);
    public static Result<T> Fail(ValidationError e) => new(default, e);
}

// Expected outcome -> result. The caller must look at it.
public Result<Quote> Validate(QuoteRequest r) =>
    r.Amount <= 0 ? Result<Quote>.Fail(ValidationError.AmountNotPositive) : Result<Quote>.Ok(Build(r));

// Broken invariant -> exception. Nobody is expected to handle this.
private Quote Build(QuoteRequest r)
{
    if (_rates.Count == 0) throw new InvalidOperationException("Rate table not loaded; startup validation failed.");
    return new Quote(/* ... */);
}

The rule we landed on, and wrote into the module's README, was a single sentence: expected outcomes are results, broken invariants are exceptions, and the boundary of the module is where results become HTTP status codes. Under that rule, a rejected amount is a Result and a missing rate table is a throw, because no caller can sensibly recover from the second one. We did not convert the rest of the service, which was his original proposal and the actual disagreement.

Why not adopt Result everywhere and be consistent?

Because consistency is worth a lot but not infinite, and the seams are where a bespoke Result costs the most: every framework we integrate with — ASP.NET Core middleware, EF Core, HttpClient, the retry policy — speaks exceptions, so a codebase-wide result type means an adapter at every boundary and a real risk of losing the exception's type and its stack. At 40 failures per second that is microseconds of CPU per second — invisible. A rule that fits in one sentence, which is genuinely valuable. Two conventions means every new method involves a small decision, and small decisions get made inconsistently under deadline pressure. I bounded the cost by making the boundary a physical one — the result type is internal to that assembly and cannot leak — so the question "which convention applies here" is answered by which project the file is in, not by judgment. If I had not been able to enforce it structurally, I would have picked one convention and lived with the worse fit.

06The counter that would not increment

Senior nasty debugging session

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A nightly reconciliation job reported that 0 of 1.2 million records had been adjusted, on a run where the adjustment logic had definitely executed — the audit rows were there. The counter it used to decide whether to send the summary email said zero. There was no exception, no warning, no failing test. It took me most of a day, and the answer is one of my favourite pieces of C# trivia because it is not trivia at all: it is how value types work.

The code held a per-partner statistics struct in a dictionary and incremented it in a loop. Simplified, it looked like this:

public struct Stats { public int Adjusted; public decimal Delta; }

var stats = new Dictionary<string, Stats>();
foreach (var record in batch)
{
    var s = stats[record.Partner];   // COPY out of the dictionary
    s.Adjusted++;                    // mutates the copy
    s.Delta += record.Delta;         // mutates the copy
    // ...and the copy is discarded at the end of the iteration
}

Indexing a Dictionary<TKey, TValue> where TValue is a struct returns a copy. The mutation is real, it just happens to an object nobody keeps. The compiler is silent because nothing is wrong: you are allowed to mutate a local. This is the single most common way mutable structs bite, and the version people hit second is worse, because it is invisible in a way that even careful readers miss — a struct stored in a readonly field. Calling a non-readonly instance method on a readonly field makes the compiler emit a defensive copy first, so the method mutates a temporary. Same silence, same wrong answer, and additionally a hidden allocation-free copy in your hot path that shows up as unexplained CPU.

What actually cracked it was not reading the code — I had read that loop four times. It was a test I wrote to disprove my own assumption: I asserted that stats["x"].Adjusted was 1 immediately after one increment, and it was 0. Ten seconds later the shape of the bug was obvious. The habit I took from that day is to write the smallest possible assertion against the thing I am most confident about, because a day of debugging is usually a day of being confidently wrong about one line.

The fix was to make Stats a readonly record struct and accumulate with a with expression written back into the dictionary — or, on the hot path where that mattered, ref var slot = ref CollectionsMarshal.GetValueRefOrAddDefault(stats, partner, out _); and mutate through the ref, which touches the storage in place with no copy. I chose the second for the million-row loop and the first everywhere else, and I wrote a comment on the ref version explaining exactly why it exists, because it is the kind of code that looks clever and gets "simplified" six months later.

What I would do differently: this bug was preventable by a rule, not by cleverness. Mutable structs are a sharp tool with a narrow use case, and we now have an analyzer that flags a mutable struct in a collection. I would rather lose the 3% of legitimate uses than lose another day.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did you use a struct there in the first place?

I did not — I inherited it, and my honest read is that it was written for allocation reasons that were never measured. A per-partner statistics record allocated once per partner is a handful of objects, not a million, so the class version would have cost nothing. That is the real lesson: the struct was a micro-optimisation applied at the wrong granularity, and it bought zero performance while costing a day of debugging and a wrong nightly report. When I do reach for a struct it is for small, short-lived, high-count values — ids, coordinates, parse results — and I make them readonly without exception.

What if the same code had been running concurrently?

Then it would have been worse and differently wrong. Structs give you no atomicity: a multi-field struct written from two threads can tear, so a reader can observe Adjusted from one write and Delta from another — a state that never existed. The copy semantics would also have hidden it, because each thread would be mutating its own copy and the last write to the dictionary would win, silently losing counts. For concurrent accumulation I would use Interlocked on individual fields, or a ConcurrentDictionary with an immutable value and AddOrUpdate, or — usually simplest and fastest — per-thread accumulators merged at the end.

At what point is CollectionsMarshal.GetValueRefOrAddDefault the right call?

When you have measured that the copy-and-write-back is actually costing you, and the type is big enough for the copy to matter — a two-field struct is 12 or 16 bytes and copying it is nearly free, so at that size the API buys you correctness clarity rather than speed. I used it because the loop ran 1.2 million times per night against a struct we later grew, and I wanted the write to be visibly in-place. The cost is that it is an unsafe-adjacent API: the returned ref is invalidated if anyone adds to the dictionary while you hold it, which is exactly the kind of rule a future maintainer will not know. That is why it is one method with a comment, not a pattern we use broadly.

What did you trade away with the analyzer rule?

Some legitimate performance work, and a bit of autonomy. There are real cases for a mutable struct — a stack-allocated builder, an interop layout, a hot accumulator behind a ref — and the rule makes each of them a conversation plus a suppression comment. I decided that was the right price because the failure mode of a mutable struct is a silent wrong answer, and silent wrong answers in a financial reconciliation are the most expensive category of bug we have. If the rule started generating more suppressions than catches I would drop it; I check that once a year rather than assuming.

How would you have caught this in review?

Honestly, I might not have — I had reviewed that file. What I would catch now is the pattern rather than the line: a struct with public settable fields is a smell, and any read-modify-write against a collection of value types deserves the question "does that write go back?". The systemic answer is better than the human one, which is why the analyzer exists. If I wanted a human control I would put it in the test layer instead: a property-based test that runs the accumulator over a random batch and asserts the total matches an independently computed sum would have failed on the first run, and would not depend on anyone remembering copy semantics at 6 p.m.

Staff scaling challenge

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

An ingestion service parsed delimited settlement files. It was sized for about 40,000 lines a minute; a new client arrived with a volume that put us at 400,000, with a contractual window of 20 minutes for a file that now took 90. The instinct in the room was to shard across more workers, which would have worked and would have cost roughly ten times the compute. I asked for three days to see how much of the ten times was the parser rather than the problem.

The old parser was the code everyone writes first: line.Split(','), then decimal.Parse, DateTime.Parse and a couple of Substring calls per field. For a 14-column line that is one string[] plus 14 string objects per row, all dead within microseconds — at 400,000 rows a minute, roughly 6 million short-lived strings a minute, which is a gen-0 machine gun (see story 01 for the same mechanism from the other end). None of it is needed: parsing a number out of a range of characters does not require materialising those characters as their own object.

The rewrite used ReadOnlySpan<char> end to end. A span is a stack-only view over existing memory — no allocation, no copy, bounds-checked, and the JIT is good at it. Every BCL primitive we needed already had a span overload: MemoryExtensions.Split for enumerating ranges, decimal.TryParse(ReadOnlySpan<char>, ...), DateOnly.TryParseExact, and SearchValues<char> for the "is this one of these delimiters" test that used to be a string.IndexOfAny.

// BEFORE: 15 allocations per line
var parts = line.Split(',');
var amount = decimal.Parse(parts[3], CultureInfo.InvariantCulture);
var settled = DateOnly.ParseExact(parts[7], "yyyy-MM-dd", CultureInfo.InvariantCulture);

// AFTER: zero allocations per line; 'line' is a slice of a pooled buffer
public static bool TryParseRow(ReadOnlySpan<char> line, out Row row)
{
    row = default;
    Span<Range> fields = stackalloc Range[14];
    if (line.Split(fields, ',') != 14) return false;

    if (!decimal.TryParse(line[fields[3]], NumberStyles.Number, CultureInfo.InvariantCulture, out var amount)) return false;
    if (!DateOnly.TryParseExact(line[fields[7]], "yyyy-MM-dd", CultureInfo.InvariantCulture,
                                DateTimeStyles.None, out var settled)) return false;

    row = new Row(amount, settled /* ... */);
    return true;
}

Two things I want to be precise about, because they are where drill-downs go. First, spans are ref structs: they cannot be fields of a class, cannot be boxed, and — before C# 13 — could not be used across an await or inside an iterator at all. C# 13 allows ref struct types to implement interfaces and to be used as generic type arguments with the allows ref struct constraint, which removed a lot of the old contortions but not the fundamental rule: a span cannot outlive the stack frame that owns the memory. Second, the reading side had to change too, or the parser wins nothing — we read through System.IO.Pipelines with a pooled buffer so the span views memory we already had, rather than a string we just allocated to make a span over.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just run ten workers and keep the simple parser?

Because the simple parser was CPU-bound on garbage collection, so ten workers meant ten times the compute for a linear win on a problem that had a constant-factor fix — and because the downstream database could not take ten times the concurrent write load without its own project. I did time-box it: three days, and if I had not had a working prototype at 5x I would have recommended the sharding. Scale-out is the right answer surprisingly often; it was not right here because the inefficiency was mechanical and local, which is the cheapest kind to fix.

What if the input had been UTF-8 bytes off a socket rather than a string?

Better, actually — that is the shape I would design for now. Working in ReadOnlySpan<byte> with Utf8Parser or the UTF-8 TryParse overloads avoids the transcoding step entirely, and System.IO.Pipelines hands you the bytes without a copy. Our source was a text file we already had to decode, so the win would have been smaller, but for a network protocol I would never round-trip through string. The catch is that byte-level parsing gets harder the moment the data is genuinely non-ASCII, because a character can span bytes and you cannot slice naively.

At what scale does the span design break?

It breaks on line size, not line count. Everything assumes a row fits in the buffer and can be viewed as a contiguous span, so a row larger than the pipe's segment, or one that straddles two buffer segments, needs the slow path — copy into a rented array and parse that. We handled it and it is roughly 0.1% of rows. It also breaks the moment the parsing needs state across rows, because you cannot hold a span in a field, so any cross-row context has to be copied into ordinary values. And it breaks conceptually when the format stops being positional: for genuinely irregular input, a real tokenizer beats a fast field splitter no matter how zero-allocation the splitter is.

What did you trade away?

Accessibility of the code and quality of the error messages. decimal.Parse throws with the offending text in the message; TryParse returns false and I had to write the diagnostics myself — which I did badly at first, logging a row number with no content, and improved after the first support ticket. The maintenance cost is real: two of eight engineers were comfortable changing that file. I bounded it by keeping the parser behind a plain interface with a large golden-file test suite, so the fast implementation is swappable and provably equivalent to the readable one.

What would you build differently on .NET 10 today?

Two things. C# 14's first-class span conversions mean spans compose with the rest of the language more naturally — implicit conversions between arrays and spans, spans as extension-method receivers, better generic inference — so some of the explicit ceremony in that file would disappear; the flip side is that those same conversions changed overload resolution, which bit us during the upgrade (story 04), so I would re-run the golden files rather than trust it. I would also lean harder on SearchValues<T>, which vectorises multi-value searches and got broader in .NET 9 and 10, instead of the hand-rolled delimiter scan I originally wrote.

How did you convince the team to let you spend three days on it?

By making the alternative concrete and by putting a stop condition on my own work. The sharding project was estimated at six weeks and a named monthly compute number; three days against that is a rounding error, and I said explicitly what result would make me abandon the idea. Time-boxed investigation with a pre-committed failure criterion is the only way I have found to get permission for speculative work without spending political capital — and it means that when you come back with a result, nobody has to take your word for the counterfactual.

08The deserializer that would run anything

Staff security issue found and fixed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I found this one in a routine review of an old integration endpoint, not from a scanner and not from an incident, which is worth saying because "how do you find security problems" is the real question behind the archetype. The endpoint accepted a JSON envelope from a partner and deserialised it with Newtonsoft.Json configured with TypeNameHandling.Auto. That setting tells the deserializer to honour a $type property in the payload and instantiate whatever type it names.

The consequence is not "an attacker can send you the wrong shape". It is that an attacker chooses which types your process constructs, and .NET has a long, well-catalogued list of types whose construction or property setters do something — start a process, open a file, resolve a remote object. Those are called gadgets, they are publicly enumerated, and the exploit does not need a bug in your code at all. My proof of concept was a payload that made the service write a file to a path I chose. I stopped there, wrote it up, and did not go looking for command execution, because the point was already made and going further on a production system is a career decision as well as a technical one.

// The vulnerability: the payload chooses the type
var settings = new JsonSerializerSettings { TypeNameHandling = TypeNameHandling.Auto };
var envelope = JsonConvert.DeserializeObject<Envelope>(body, settings);

// The fix: a closed, compile-time allowlist. The payload chooses a *discriminator*, not a type.
[JsonPolymorphic(TypeDiscriminatorPropertyName = "kind",
                 UnknownDerivedTypeHandling = JsonUnknownDerivedTypeHandling.FailSerialization)]
[JsonDerivedType(typeof(PriceUpdate), "price")]
[JsonDerivedType(typeof(StockUpdate), "stock")]
public abstract record Update;

// Plus: source-generated context, so the set of constructible types is fixed at compile time.
[JsonSerializable(typeof(Update))]
internal sealed partial class WireContext : JsonSerializerContext;

The design principle I would want a candidate to articulate: never let untrusted input choose a type. A discriminator string mapped by your code to a closed set of types is safe because the mapping is yours; a type name in the payload is unsafe because the mapping is the whole CLR. The same principle is why BinaryFormatter was removed from .NET outright rather than merely deprecated — it is unfixable by design, since its entire job is "reconstruct whatever object graph the bytes describe". If you still have it, the migration is the work; there is no safe configuration.

Getting it fixed was the harder half. The endpoint was owned by another team, mid-quarter, with no incident to point at. It shipped in nine days, and the durable outcome was that we added a check for the three dangerous settings to the shared analyzer package (story 17) so no new service could reintroduce it.

I optimised for the relationship and under-optimised for the paper trail, and if it had not been fixed in nine days I would have had no escalation path that did not look like a surprise.

What the interviewer is really probing. Judgment as much as knowledge: how you handle a finding in someone else's code, how far you take a proof of concept, and whether you can get a fix prioritised without an incident to force it. The failure modes are vivid — the candidate who exploited it further than needed, the one who posted it publicly to make a point, and the one who found it, told nobody, and let it sit. They also listen for whether you understand the class of bug or just the CVE.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just add a SerializationBinder allowlist and keep the existing serializer?

That is a legitimate fix and it was my week-one fallback — a binder that resolves only the six types we expect closes the hole without touching the wire format. I pushed for the System.Text.Json polymorphic model because the safety then comes from the type declarations rather than from a class someone can edit, and because a source-generated context makes the whole constructible set visible at compile time and in review. The trade is a wire-format change and a coordinated deployment with the partner, which is why I brought both options and let the owning team choose; they chose the bigger one because they were already touching the contract.

How did you know it was exploitable rather than theoretical?

I proved one narrow thing — that a type I chose in the payload was constructed and that a property setter I chose ran — using a benign gadget that wrote a file to a temp path, in a pre-production environment, with the owning team's lead watching. That is the minimum that distinguishes a real finding from a "this setting is discouraged" comment, which teams routinely deprioritise. I deliberately did not chain it to command execution: the extra proof would have added nothing to the decision, and running an RCE chain on someone else's service without written authorisation is not a thing I am willing to do even when I am confident it would work.

At what scale does "fix it in this endpoint" stop being the answer?

Immediately, and that was the part I nearly got wrong. One misconfigured endpoint is a bug; the same setting in a shared helper class copied into four services is a class of bug, and the fix has to be at the level of the class. We grepped the estate for the three settings, found two more instances, and then went one level further and asked what made them easy to write — the answer was a sample in our internal wiki from 2019. Fixing the wiki page was the cheapest control we shipped that quarter, and I would look for the source of a repeated mistake before writing any per-site fix.

What did you trade away with the analyzer rule and the closed allowlist?

Extensibility, deliberately. A closed [JsonDerivedType] set means adding a message kind is a code change and a deployment, so a partner cannot introduce a new type by agreement alone — which slowed one integration by a sprint and generated real complaints. I think that is the correct trade for an internet-facing endpoint, because "the set of types is a deployment decision" is exactly the property we wanted. Where a team genuinely needs open extensibility, the answer is not to loosen deserialization but to move the variable part into data — a payload with a fixed schema and a free-form properties bag that never determines a type.

How do you decide who to tell first?

My default order is: the owning team's lead, privately and immediately; the security team through the formal channel the same day; a wider audience only after there is a fix or a timeline. The reasoning is that the owning team can act fastest and deserves not to be ambushed, and the formal channel exists so that if the fix stalls there is an escalation that is procedural rather than personal. The mistake I made was treating that as sequential rather than parallel — I told the team on day one and filed formally on day four, which left three days where the only record of a live vulnerability was a conversation.

09Killing the service locator

Staff tech-debt paydown / refactor

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The codebase had a static ServiceLocator.Current.Resolve<T>() used in 340 places. It predated the team, it worked, and every attempt to remove it had died at the "this will take a quarter and delivers no features" conversation. What made it finally happen was that I stopped selling it as cleanliness and started attaching it to bugs we were already paying for.

There were three, and they are the technical spine of the story. First, captive dependencies: a singleton resolving a scoped service through the locator captures the first scope's instance forever, so a singleton cache was holding a DbContext from whichever request happened to warm it. That produced the "stale data for one pod until restart" bug we had chased three times and closed as unreproducible. Second, lifetime invisibility: because resolution happened deep inside methods rather than at construction, the container's own ValidateScopes and ValidateOnBuild checks could not see the graph and had nothing to validate. Third, testability: 340 hidden dependencies meant tests either used the real container or mutated static state, and we had a class of flaky test that came purely from test ordering.

// The debt: dependency invisible in the signature, lifetime decided at call time
public sealed class PriceCache          // registered as a singleton
{
    public decimal Get(string sku)
    {
        var db = ServiceLocator.Current.Resolve<PricingDbContext>();   // scoped -> captured forever
        return db.Prices.Single(p => p.Sku == sku).Amount;
    }
}

// The fix: the singleton owns a factory, not an instance
public sealed class PriceCache(IServiceScopeFactory scopes)
{
    public decimal Get(string sku)
    {
        using var scope = scopes.CreateScope();
        var db = scope.ServiceProvider.GetRequiredService<PricingDbContext>();
        return db.Prices.Single(p => p.Sku == sku).Amount;
    }
}

The plan was strangulation, not a rewrite. I added ValidateScopes = true and ValidateOnBuild = true to the host in every environment including production — that alone turned two classes of latent bug into startup failures, which is the trade I want: fail loudly at boot rather than quietly at 3 a.m. Then I banned new usages with an analyzer error (existing ones suppressed via a baseline file with a count in it), and made the count visible on the team dashboard. It went from 340 to zero over about five months, mostly as a few lines attached to whatever ticket happened to touch the file, with two deliberate multi-day pushes for the hairy clusters.

The genuinely awkward part was the plugin-ish code that resolved a handler by name at runtime — a legitimate reason to talk to the container. .NET 8 added keyed services, which turned ServiceLocator.Current.Resolve<IHandler>(name) into a first-class registration and a [FromKeyedServices("name")] constructor parameter, so those thirty-odd sites became ordinary injection. Before that existed, my plan had been a hand-written factory interface, which is fine and is what I would still do for a dynamic set that is not known at registration time.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not leave it? It worked.

Because it was not free — it was costing three recurring incidents and a flaky suite, and those costs were being paid by on-call and by CI rather than by anyone's roadmap, which is exactly how debt hides. The case I made was not "this is bad code", it was "we are already paying for this quarterly, here are the ticket numbers, and the payment is going up as the codebase grows". I would genuinely have left it alone if I could not have made that case; there is plenty of ugly code I have deliberately not touched because it was stable and nobody had to change it.

What if the team had refused to allocate the time?

Then I would have done the ratchet and skipped the cleanup — the analyzer that stops new usages costs an afternoon and delivers most of the risk reduction, because the debt stops compounding even if the existing sites remain. That is my general fallback for unfunded debt: make it non-growing and visible, and let the number sit on a dashboard where it becomes uncomfortable. Twice in my career the visible counter is what eventually got the work funded, without me having to advocate for it again.

At what scale does the incremental approach stop working?

When the sites are not independent. Three hundred and forty locator calls were independently fixable because each one was a local change with a local test; a refactor where every change forces a signature change up three layers cannot be done in increments and needs a real branch and a real freeze. The other limit is time: an incremental migration that will take longer than the team's memory of why it started will not finish, so if my estimate crosses roughly two quarters I either shrink the scope or accept it will need dedicated capacity. I have killed one migration for exactly that reason and it was the right call.

What did you trade away?

Some genuine flexibility, and about five months of low-grade attention. Constructor injection makes the dependency graph explicit, which also makes it rigid — a class that needs a new collaborator now advertises it, and a couple of classes ended up with six constructor parameters, which is a legitimate smell that we then had to address separately by splitting them. That is the trade I want: the locator was hiding the design problem, and removing it made the problem visible rather than creating it. The pure cost was reviewer time on 340 small diffs.

How would you approach this on .NET 10 today?

Largely the same, with two shortcuts. Keyed services (.NET 8) make the "resolve by name" cases first-class, so the population needing a hand-written factory is much smaller than it used to be. And I would lean on the container's own validation harder and earlier — ValidateOnBuild plus ValidateScopes in every environment, wired in the shared host builder so a new service gets it by default rather than by choice. The broader principle I would state is that the fastest way to retire a pattern is to make its correct replacement the path of least resistance in the template, not to write a migration guide.

10Teaching deferred execution the hard way

Senior mentoring / levelling someone up

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A mid-level engineer I was mentoring shipped a reporting endpoint that worked perfectly in review and took 90 seconds in production. She had written what looks like completely reasonable C#: a repository method returning IEnumerable<Order>, then some LINQ filtering, then a couple of aggregations. The endpoint issued 4,800 database queries per call. The interesting part of this story is not the bug — it is that I nearly fixed it for her, and did not.

The mechanism is deferred execution, and it has two distinct halves that people conflate. A LINQ-to-Objects operator like Where returns an iterator object that does nothing until you enumerate it; enumerating it twice runs the whole chain twice. Separately, LINQ-to-Entities builds an expression tree that EF Core translates into SQL — but only while the static type is IQueryable<T>. The moment the sequence is typed as IEnumerable<T>, every subsequent operator is LINQ-to-Objects, running in your process over rows the database has already sent. Her repository returned IEnumerable<Order>, so the Where ran client-side, and a lazy-loaded navigation property inside the projection produced one query per row.

// Reads fine. Runs the filter in the app, and lazy-loads once per row.
public IEnumerable<Order> GetForCustomer(Guid id) => _db.Orders.Where(o => o.CustomerId == id);
// ...
var large = repo.GetForCustomer(id).Where(o => o.Total > 1000);
var count = large.Count();          // enumerates once
var sum   = large.Sum(o => o.Total); // enumerates AGAIN - the whole chain re-runs

// The version we arrived at together
public async Task<IReadOnlyList<OrderSummary>> GetLargeForCustomerAsync(Guid id, CancellationToken ct) =>
    await _db.Orders
        .Where(o => o.CustomerId == id && o.Total > 1000)
        .Select(o => new OrderSummary(o.Id, o.Total, o.Lines.Count))  // projection -> one SQL statement
        .AsNoTracking()
        .ToListAsync(ct);

What I did instead of fixing it: I asked her to turn on EF Core's query logging at Information level and re-run the endpoint once. She watched 4,800 lines scroll past, and that was the entire lesson — I did not have to explain anything about expression trees for the next twenty minutes to land. Then I asked her to predict, before running it, how many queries three variants would produce. She got the third one wrong, which was the useful one, because it was the case where ToList() in the middle of a chain silently switches everything after it to client evaluation.

Senior scope / deadline negotiation

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We had three weeks to deliver an integration that touched 60-odd message contracts, and the design on the table involved writing a Roslyn incremental source generator to produce the mapping and validation code from attributes. The engineer proposing it was right that hand-writing 60 mappers was miserable, and right that reflection-based mapping at startup was a measurable cost. He was also proposing to learn Roslyn generators on a three-week deadline, which is the part I had to negotiate.

The technical trade-off is genuinely interesting and worth being able to argue both sides of. A source generator runs inside the compiler and emits C# that the compiler then compiles: zero startup cost, no reflection, trim- and AOT-safe, and you can read the generated code. The costs are real and under-appreciated — the generator is a separate project targeting netstandard2.0, it runs on every keystroke in the IDE, so a badly written one makes the whole solution feel slow; debugging it means launching a second compiler; and an incremental generator only stays fast if you are careful about what goes into the pipeline (holding a Compilation or a syntax node in the cached model silently defeats the incrementality and pins memory). None of that is hard once you have done it. All of it is expensive the first time.

12The hour that repeated itself

Mid data-corruption scare

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

On the last Sunday in October, a scheduling service created 1,400 duplicate appointment slots between 01:00 and 02:00, and lost the equivalent hour in spring six months earlier without anyone noticing. It was a data-corruption scare rather than a loss — nothing was overwritten — but it took two days to prove that, which is its own kind of expensive.

The root cause was DateTime doing exactly what it is documented to do. A DateTime carries a Kind — Utc, Local or Unspecified — and Unspecified is the default for anything parsed, read from most database drivers, or constructed with new DateTime(...). The service stored local wall-clock times as Unspecified, and when the clocks went back, 01:30 happened twice. There is no information in the value to distinguish the two, so the generator produced both and the deduplication key could not tell them apart. In spring the mirror-image bug had silently produced zero slots for an hour that does not exist, and no one had complained because it was 02:00 on a Sunday.

// The bug: a wall-clock instant that is ambiguous twice a year
var slot = new DateTime(2026, 10, 25, 1, 30, 0);      // Kind = Unspecified
if (slot > DateTime.Now) Schedule(slot);              // Now is Local; comparison is meaningless

// The fix: store the instant, carry the zone separately, render at the edge
DateTimeOffset instant = new DateTimeOffset(local, tz.GetUtcOffset(local));  // explicit offset
// or, for a genuine wall-clock booking:
DateOnly date = new(2026, 10, 25);
TimeOnly time = new(1, 30);
string zoneId = "Europe/London";     // IANA id; .NET 6+ accepts IANA and Windows ids on both platforms

The distinction I had to learn properly, and which I now use as my own check, is between an instant and a wall-clock time. "The moment this transaction committed" is an instant: store it as UTC or as a DateTimeOffset, and it is unambiguous forever. "The recurring 09:00 appointment in London" is not an instant — it is a local time plus a zone, and its UTC value changes when the rules change. Storing the second as UTC is the mistake that bites when a government moves a DST boundary, which happens more often than engineers expect. We had one of each in the same table, both typed datetime, which is why nobody had noticed.

The fix was a migration: split the column into an instant column and a wall-clock date/time-plus-zone pair, backfill using the historical zone rules rather than the current ones, and add a uniqueness constraint that included the offset so a genuine duplicate would fail loudly rather than insert quietly. The second part was banning DateTime.Now and DateTime.UtcNow in the codebase in favour of an injected TimeProvider, which is what made the whole thing testable: the regression test for this bug is a unit test that sets the clock to 01:30 on the transition day and asserts one slot, and it runs in milliseconds on any machine in any zone (see story 19).

What I would do differently: I would have checked for the spring version immediately. We fixed the autumn duplicate and only found the missing-hour bug a week later when someone thought to look, which meant two separate conversations with the same customers instead of one.

What the interviewer is really probing. Care with data, and honesty about a scare. They want to hear you establish blast radius before touching anything, and they very much want to hear that you did not "just fix it" in production without a backup and a plan. The failure modes: minimising ("it was only 1,400 rows"), a fix with no verification step, and — the one that stands out — not being able to say how you proved nothing was actually lost. At mid level this is a strong story precisely because it is about rigour rather than seniority.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just store everything in UTC and be done with it?

Because "always UTC" is correct for instants and wrong for future wall-clock commitments. If a customer books a recurring 09:00 appointment in London and you store the computed UTC value, you have baked in today's DST rules; when the clocks shift — or when a government changes the rules, which happens somewhere most years — the appointment silently moves to 08:00 or 10:00. The correct storage for that is the local time plus the zone id, converting to an instant at the moment you actually need one. "Always UTC" is good advice that becomes wrong at exactly the point it matters most, which is why I now ask "instant or wall clock?" about every date column.

What if the duplicates had already been sent to customers?

Then the technical fix becomes the smaller half of the problem, and the sequence changes: stop the sender first, work out exactly who received what from the outbound log rather than from the source table, and get a correction message drafted with whoever owns customer communication before touching the data. I would also resist the urge to delete the duplicate rows quickly — once something has been communicated externally, the row is evidence, and I would mark rather than remove it. In our case they had not gone out, because the send job ran on a schedule we managed to pause, which was luck as much as design.

At what scale does this repair strategy break?

The backfill was the fragile part and it scales badly. Fourteen hundred rows can be repaired in a transaction with a before-and-after count; a hundred million cannot, and the strategy has to change to a batched, resumable, idempotent job that you can stop halfway — plus a shadow column so the old value survives until you have verified the new one. The other thing that breaks at scale is verification: at 1,400 rows I checked every one against the audit log, and above some threshold you are sampling, which means stating a confidence rather than a certainty. I would rather say "we verified a 1% sample with no discrepancies" than imply I checked everything.

What did you trade away with the schema change?

Simplicity and some query convenience. Two columns plus a zone id is harder to sort and filter than one datetime, reporting queries had to be rewritten, and every consumer of that table had to be updated — which took a cross-team conversation because two of them were not ours. We also kept the old column, populated, for a deprecation window, which meant a period of dual writes and the risk of divergence that comes with it. It was worth it because the ambiguity was unfixable in the old shape, but "we made the schema more complicated on purpose" is exactly the sort of trade I would expect to have to justify.

How do you stop the next person reintroducing it?

Three layers, in increasing order of reliability. A rule in the coding standard, which helps a little; an analyzer banning DateTime.Now, DateTime.UtcNow and the parameterless DateTime constructor in favour of TimeProvider, which catches most of it mechanically; and a database constraint plus a test suite that runs the scheduler across both transition days every year, which catches the rest. The layer people skip is the last one, and it is the only one that would have caught the spring bug — a rule about types cannot tell you that your logic produces zero slots for a missing hour.

13Modelling a state machine C# had no type for

Staff ambiguous / shifting requirements

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were asked to model an onboarding flow that the business could not describe consistently. Different stakeholders gave us four, six and nine states depending on who was in the room, and the set changed twice during the build — once because a regulator's guidance landed mid-project. The engineering question underneath the ambiguity was: how do you model a set of cases that you know will change, in a language that has no discriminated union?

The first attempt was the one everyone writes: an enum Status plus a bag of nullable fields, because different states carry different data — a rejected application has a reason code, a pending one has a review deadline, an approved one has neither and has a policy id instead. That shape lies about the data. Every field is nullable, every consumer null-checks defensively, and nothing stops you constructing an approved application with a rejection reason. When the state set changed, we found the code paths that needed updating by running the tests and seeing what broke, which is not a strategy.

What I moved us to was a sealed hierarchy of records: an abstract base with a private constructor so no one outside the file can extend it, one record per state carrying exactly the data that state has, and switch expressions with pattern matching at every consumer. That is C#'s idiomatic stand-in for a union, and its properties are specific and worth stating precisely: the type system now makes an invalid combination unrepresentable, and the compiler warns (CS8509) when a switch expression does not handle every case it can see. It does not give you a true exhaustiveness error — the warning depends on the compiler being able to see the closed set, and a non-exhaustive switch still compiles and throws at runtime. We turned that warning into an error, which is the step that made the design actually pay.

// Closed by construction: nobody outside this file can add a case
public abstract record Application
{
    private Application() { }
    public sealed record Draft(DateOnly Started) : Application;
    public sealed record PendingReview(DateOnly Deadline, string Reviewer) : Application;
    public sealed record Approved(PolicyId Policy, DateOnly EffectiveFrom) : Application;
    public sealed record Rejected(string ReasonCode, DateOnly DecidedOn) : Application;
}

// Every consumer is a switch expression; adding a case breaks the build (CS8509 as an error)
public static string Describe(Application a) => a switch
{
    Application.Draft d               => $"started {d.Started:d}",
    Application.PendingReview p       => $"with {p.Reviewer}, due {p.Deadline:d}",
    Application.Approved { EffectiveFrom: var f } => $"live from {f:d}",
    Application.Rejected r            => $"rejected ({r.ReasonCode})",
};

When the regulator's change landed and two states became four, the compiler produced a list of every place that had to change — 31 switch expressions across three assemblies — and the change took a day rather than a fortnight of grep-and-hope. That is the entire argument for this shape, and it is the number I would lead with. The cost is verbosity, a slightly awkward serialization story (a type discriminator per case, see story 08), and the fact that persisting a hierarchy through EF Core means either table-per-hierarchy with lots of nullable columns — the very shape we were escaping — or a JSON column, which we chose.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not build a generic, configurable workflow engine so the business can change it themselves?

Because that turns a code change into a configuration change and moves the risk somewhere with no tests, no review and no rollback. Every workflow engine I have seen built for this reason ended up with a DSL that only engineers could edit safely, at which point you have the same coupling plus a bespoke interpreter to maintain. My threshold is the rate of change: if the state set were changing weekly by business decision, an engine is right; ours changed twice in six months, so a compiler-checked model plus a one-day deployment was strictly better. I would say that trade-off out loud in the design review rather than just declining.

What if a case were added by another team in another assembly?

It cannot be, by design — the private constructor closes the hierarchy — and that is exactly the property I wanted, because an open set gives you no exhaustiveness anywhere. If a genuine extension point were needed I would model it differently: an open set of behaviours behind an interface, resolved at runtime, with a default case that fails loudly rather than silently. The distinction I would draw is that closed sets buy you compile-time reasoning and open sets buy you extensibility, and you cannot have both in the same type; the mistake is pretending a half-open set is fine, which is how you get a default: branch that throws in production two years later.

At what scale does this model become painful?

Around a dozen states with meaningfully different data, or when transitions start carrying their own data. Four states and 31 switch sites was comfortable; twenty states would mean switch expressions long enough that people stop reading them, and I would move behaviour into the types (the state pattern) or split the model into a coarse outer state plus finer inner ones. It also gets painful across service boundaries — the closed set is closed in one assembly, and the moment another service deserialises it, your exhaustiveness guarantee stops at the wire. That is where the discriminator allowlist and a versioning policy have to take over.

What did you trade away?

Line count and persistence simplicity. The record hierarchy is roughly three times the code of the enum version, the JSON column means we cannot query by state-specific fields in SQL without indexing computed columns, and reporting had to be fed from a projection rather than the source table. I accepted that because the regulator's change was not going to be the last one, and because the reporting projection was something we wanted anyway. If the model had been genuinely stable I would have used the enum — the sealed hierarchy is a bet on change, and if change does not come you have simply paid more.

Does C# 15 change this?

Potentially quite a lot, and it is the thing I would watch rather than adopt. Union types are in preview in the .NET 11 SDK with GA expected in November 2026, and they give the language a genuinely closed set with real exhaustiveness rather than the warning-elevated-to-error trick I am using today. If they land as proposed, the hierarchy above becomes a declaration a few lines long, and the awkward parts — the private constructor, the nested records, the CS8509 configuration — go away. I would not put a preview language feature into a regulated production workflow, and I would say the same to anyone who asked; the sealed hierarchy is the shape that migrates cleanly to it later, which is the actual reason to build it that way now.

14One shared NuGet, eleven consumers

Principal cross-team / integration dependency

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I owned an internal package that eleven services depended on — the shared client for our identity and entitlements system. It had grown organically, its public surface was about 200 types, and roughly 60 of those were accidental: internal helpers made public because somebody once needed one from a test. Two teams were pinned to a version eighteen months old because upgrading kept breaking their build. The task, as I framed it, was not "clean up the package" but "make it possible for eleven teams to move at their own pace".

The technical content that makes this a C# story rather than a process story is what counts as a breaking change on the .NET platform, because the intuition most engineers have is source-level and the runtime cares about binary compatibility. Adding an overload is source-compatible and can still break a caller at compile time through ambiguity; adding a member to a public interface breaks every implementor unless you give it a default implementation (C# 8 default interface members, which have their own costs); reordering optional parameters or changing a default value is silently breaking because defaults are baked into the caller's compiled code; and changing a field to a property, or a class to a record, changes the binary contract even though the source looks identical. That last one caught us: someone turned a DTO into a record for the value equality, which is source-compatible and binary-breaking, and a service that had not recompiled got a MissingMethodException at runtime.

What I put in place, in order of value. First, the public API analyzer with PublicAPI.Shipped.txt and PublicAPI.Unshipped.txt files in the repo: every public symbol is listed, and adding or changing one is a build error until you edit the file. That single change turned "did this PR break the contract?" from a judgment call into a diff a reviewer can read, and it is the thing I would install first in any shared library I inherited. Second, a real deprecation path — [Obsolete("...", DiagnosticId = "IDN0001", UrlFormat = "...")] so consumers get a warning with a link and can suppress that specific id while they migrate, rather than an undifferentiated wall of warnings. Third, a policy with dates: two minor versions of overlap, a hard removal in the next major, published in the package's release notes and in a channel the consuming teams actually read.

The negotiation was harder than the engineering. Two teams wanted us to keep the old surface indefinitely; one wanted a clean break in the next release. I ran a single meeting with all eleven — not eleven meetings, which is what I had been doing and which produced eleven different understandings — and we agreed the policy as a group. What unlocked it was offering something in exchange: I committed to shipping a source-generated migration analyzer that rewrote the common call patterns, so upgrading was mostly "apply the code fix in the IDE". Ten of eleven services were on the current version within a quarter. The eleventh was a service being decommissioned, and we agreed in writing to leave it pinned, which is a perfectly good outcome that people are strangely reluctant to write down.

What I would do differently: I should have shipped the API analyzer years earlier — it costs a day and it is the control that makes every subsequent conversation cheap. I would also have published a support matrix from the beginning, because a large part of the friction was that nobody knew which versions were still supported, including me.

What the interviewer is really probing. Influence without authority, and whether you think about the people downstream of your code. At principal level they expect a mechanism, not heroics: a policy, a tool, a contract that keeps working when you are not in the room. The failure modes: breaking consumers and calling it their problem for not upgrading; freezing the API forever out of fear; and a story where you personally fixed everyone's code, which does not scale and quietly signals that you would rather do it yourself than change the system.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just version it and let old consumers stay on the old version forever?

Because "forever" has a cost that is invisible until it is urgent: every security patch and every platform migration has to be applied to every live major version, and at eleven consumers on four versions that is four times the work for the owning team, done by people who no longer remember the old code. What if a team simply refused to upgrade?

My first question is why, because "refused" is usually "cannot afford it this quarter", which is a scheduling problem with a solution. At what scale does the API analyzer approach break down?

It scales well in files and badly in politics. The PublicAPI.txt files handle a large surface fine — ours was 200 types and the file was long but reviewable. That is why I would not recommend the same cadence for a package on nuget.org, and I would say so if the question implied it.

What did you trade away?

Speed of change in the library itself. The analyzer means every genuine addition costs a file edit and a reviewer conversation, which slowed our own team down by a real amount — a few hours a month, and more friction than that emotionally. We also kept some ugly API shapes longer than we wanted because breaking them was not worth the coordination cost, so the library is less elegant than it could be. I think that is correct: the library exists for its consumers, and the cost of my team's convenience is being paid by eleven other teams if I get it wrong.

How do you decide when a major version is justified?

When the accumulated deprecations have reached the end of their published window, or when a platform change forces it — and, importantly, batched rather than trickled. A major version per quarter trains people to ignore your policy; one every year or two, with a migration guide, an analyzer with code fixes, and a clear list of what was removed, gets taken seriously. I also try to make majors boring: nothing new in a major release, only removals, so that a consumer's upgrade is purely mechanical and they can do it without deciding anything.

15The Native AOT push we cancelled

Staff a project that failed or was cancelled

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We spent six weeks trying to move a fleet of small internal APIs to Native AOT and then stopped. I proposed the project, I ran it, and I am the person who recommended killing it — which is the only reason it is worth telling, because a cancelled project you did not own is not a story about you.

The motivation was good. These were 30-odd small services, scaled to zero overnight and scaled up in bursts, and cold start was hurting: roughly 900 ms to first request, of which most was JIT compilation and assembly loading. Native AOT compiles the whole application ahead of time into a single native binary with no JIT and a smaller runtime, and the published numbers for that workload shape are dramatic — tens of milliseconds to start, and a fraction of the memory. Our prototype on the simplest service hit 18 ms and a 38 MB working set, down from 900 ms and 140 MB.

Then we tried it on a real one. Native AOT works by trimming everything not statically reachable, which means reflection is the enemy: anything that looks up a type or a member by name at runtime may have been removed. Our blockers, in the order we hit them: EF Core, whose query pipeline is not AOT-compatible in the general case; a validation library built entirely on reflection over attributes; the reflection-based configuration binder (fixable — .NET 8 added a source-generated binder); and our own dynamic proxy interception for logging, which has no AOT story at all because it emits IL at runtime by definition. Each one produced trim warnings rather than errors, which is its own trap: the build succeeds and the failure appears at runtime, in a code path that might be rare.

The source-generated JSON contexts and the compile-time logging we had written for AOT compatibility were straight wins we kept. And we now have a written note explaining exactly why AOT does not fit these services, which has saved the same conversation at least twice since — including once when a new architect proposed it, and I could hand him the note rather than re-litigate it.

What I would do differently: I picked the wrong pilot. I prototyped on the simplest service because it was fastest to get a number, and that number was the one I took to the planning meeting. If I had piloted on the most representative service, I would have hit EF Core in week one instead of week three, and the whole thing would have been a two-week investigation rather than a six-week project.

They are listening for: you owned it, you stopped it on evidence rather than on exhaustion, you communicated it well, and you extracted value. The failure modes: a "failure" that was actually someone else's fault; a failure with no lesson; and the humble-brag version where the project failed for reasons entirely outside your control and you were right all along.

Why did it take five weeks to find out?

I would now share the direction of travel as soon as I have it, even if the write-up is not finished.

16Cutting the bill by cutting the heap

Principal cost-optimisation win

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Finance asked for a 20% reduction in the platform's compute spend. The default engineering response — find the biggest service and optimise it — would have taken a quarter and delivered maybe 6%. I spent the first week not writing any code, and instead worked out what we were actually paying for. Across 34 .NET services, the fleet was provisioned at roughly 4 GB per pod with an average steady-state working set of about 700 MB. We were paying for memory nobody used, and the reason was a specific and very common misunderstanding about how .NET's garbage collector sizes itself in a container.

The mechanism is worth getting exactly right, because it is the whole story. Server GC — the default for ASP.NET Core — allocates per-core heaps and is tuned for throughput: it lets the heap grow and collects less often. In a container it reads the cgroup memory limit and sizes its budget as a fraction of it, so raising a pod's memory limit raises the heap the process will happily use, and a service with a 4 GB limit will show a 3 GB working set doing the same work it would do in 1 GB. Teams saw high memory usage, concluded the service needed memory, raised the limit, and made the observation true. I have watched that loop run in three different organisations.

What we did: for each service, took a dotnet-gcdump at peak, measured the actual live object graph rather than the working set, and set the limit to roughly 2.5x that with an alert on sustained pressure. Total: fleet memory allocation down 44%, which let the cluster autoscaler pack roughly 1.7x the pods per node, and the compute line dropped 31% — comfortably past the target, in three weeks rather than a quarter.

The part I am most careful to include when I tell this: about a fifth of the win came from three services that were genuinely leaking, and finding those was ordinary debugging work that the right-sizing exercise surfaced. Right-sizing does not fix a leak; it turns a slow leak into a fast crash, which is why we did the alerting and the canary before the limits.

What I would do differently: I would have started with the cost data rather than the engineering intuition, on every cost project since. The week I spent building a per-service cost view was the highest-leverage week of the project, and I nearly skipped it because it did not feel like engineering.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you optimise the system rather than the code, and whether you can speak the business's language without losing technical precision. At principal level they also want to see restraint: knowing which 80% of the win is cheap and stopping there. The failure modes: a story that is really a micro-optimisation with a dollar figure attached; savings quoted with no baseline or no reliability consequences; and — the one that concerns interviewers most — a cost cut that later caused an incident and is not mentioned.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just optimise the biggest service?

Because the biggest service was 9% of the spend and was already reasonably efficient, so a quarter of work there had a ceiling below the target. Fleet-wide misconfiguration was 40% of the spend and had a two-line fix per service. That is the general shape I look for on cost work: find the thing that is wrong in many places at once, because per-place effort is what makes optimisation expensive. I would have gone after the single service if the cost view had shown it dominating — which is exactly why the week of building the cost view mattered more than any of the engineering.

What if a right-sized service had then started OOM-killing under an unusual load?

Two of them did, in the canary, which is where I wanted it to happen. The design assumed it: every limit change went out to one pod first with an alert on sustained GC pressure and on the container's memory working set relative to the limit, and the rollback was a config change that took under a minute. For the two that failed, we found that the peak live set was genuinely larger during the monthly close — a load pattern that did not appear in the week I sampled — and the fix was to sample over a full business cycle rather than a week. Below roughly a dozen services it is not a programme, it is an afternoon per service, and you would just do it as part of normal operations. Our code reviews had become an argument about the same eight things. The second attempt worked and had four parts. Nobody argues about brace style once it formats itself and never fails a build. Second, a baseline: existing violations were suppressed wholesale in a generated file with the count in its name, so the rule was enforced for new code from day one while old code stayed legal. Third, dotnet format in a pre-commit hook, so the mechanical part never reached a human. Fourth — and this is the part I would repeat everywhere — a rule cannot be introduced without an owner and a one-line rationale in the .editorconfig, so a year later nobody has to guess why CA1307 is an error.

# CA2007: ConfigureAwait in library code. Applies to src/Libraries/** only; see docs/async-policy.md.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just enforce the rules in code review, like before?

Because human enforcement of mechanical rules is expensive, inconsistent and demoralising for everyone involved. It costs reviewer attention that should go to the design, it varies with who reviews and how tired they are, and it makes the reviewer the bad guy for something a machine could say without any social cost. The rules that genuinely need human judgment — is this the right abstraction, does this test test anything — are the ones I want reviewers spending their attention on, and every mechanical comment crowds one of those out. That was the argument that landed with the team, more than any appeal to consistency.

What if the analyzer flags something that is genuinely fine?

Then it gets suppressed at the site with a justification comment, and if it happens more than a handful of times the rule is wrong and we change it. I made that explicit at rollout because the fastest way to kill an analyzer's credibility is to make suppression feel like cheating — people then either fight it or write worse code to satisfy it. We review suppressions quarterly, and twice that review has led to demoting a rule from error to suggestion. A rule with a lot of justified suppressions is data, not defiance.

At what scale does the baseline approach break down?

When the baseline file becomes a place things go to be forgotten. Eleven thousand suppressed diagnostics with no plan is just a nicer-looking backlog, and I have seen baselines outlive the people who created them. What made ours work was that the count was visible and monotonically decreasing by policy — touching a file meant clearing its suppressions — and that the correctness rules were not baselined at all; we fixed those 60-odd instances up front because a rule you are not willing to fix immediately probably should not be an error. Above a few thousand baselined items I would question whether the ruleset is right rather than the code.

What did you trade away?

Build time and a little flexibility. Full analysis added roughly 25 seconds to a clean build, which we clawed back by running the expensive ruleset only in CI and a lighter set locally. The flexibility cost is more interesting: a rule as an error means an engineer with a genuinely good reason has to write a suppression and defend it in review, which is friction applied to exactly the person most likely to be right. I decided that was acceptable for a small set of rules with incident numbers attached, and not acceptable for anything stylistic — which is why the error list stayed at eleven rules rather than growing.

How would you introduce this into a team you had just joined?

Much more slowly, and starting with the formatter rather than the analyzers. dotnet format plus an .editorconfig with only formatting rules is nearly uncontroversial, delivers a visible win in the first week, and buys credibility. Then I would wait for the first review argument or the first incident that a rule would have caught, and propose exactly that one rule with the incident attached — one rule at a time, each with a reason someone in the room remembers. Introducing a ruleset on day one in a team whose norms you do not understand reads as an outsider imposing their previous employer's standards, and it usually is.

18The leak that lived in a static event

Mid on-call firefighting

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I was on call. A service restarted itself every 40 hours or so, always with an OutOfMemoryException, always at a different time of day. It had been doing this for a month and the team had normalised it — there was a runbook entry that said "restart it", which is the detail I would want an interviewer to notice, because the on-call story here is as much about the runbook as about the leak.

Managed memory leaks are not leaks in the C sense: nothing is unreleased, everything that is reachable is simply still reachable. So the diagnostic question is always the same — what is holding a reference to this? — and the tool is a heap dump. I took two dotnet-gcdump snapshots four hours apart on a live pod and diffed them. One type had grown by 180,000 instances: a per-request pricing context that should not have survived its request.

The path to root was three hops and completely mundane: the pricing context subscribed to a static event on a configuration class so it could react to config changes, and never unsubscribed. A static event is a GC root. Every subscriber is kept alive by the publisher for as long as the publisher lives, which for a static is the life of the process, and the subscriber's entire object graph comes with it. This is the single most common managed leak in .NET and it has been for twenty years.

// The leak: a static publisher keeps every subscriber alive forever
public static class ConfigWatcher
{
    public static event EventHandler<ConfigChanged>? Changed;   // static -> roots every handler
}

public sealed class PricingContext              // scoped, one per request
{
    public PricingContext() => ConfigWatcher.Changed += OnChanged;   // never removed
    private void OnChanged(object? s, ConfigChanged e) { /* ... */ }
}

// The fix we shipped in the incident: unsubscribe deterministically
public sealed class PricingContext : IDisposable
{
    public PricingContext() => ConfigWatcher.Changed += OnChanged;
    public void Dispose() => ConfigWatcher.Changed -= OnChanged;
}

// The fix we shipped afterwards: don't hand a per-request object an event at all
public sealed class PricingContext(IOptionsMonitor<PricingOptions> options)
{
    private PricingOptions Current => options.CurrentValue;   // pull, don't subscribe
}

The immediate mitigation was to add the IDisposable unsubscribe, which the DI container calls at the end of the scope — a four-line change that went out within the hour and took the restart cycle from 40 hours to indefinite. The better fix landed the following week: a per-request object should not be subscribing to anything long-lived in the first place. IOptionsMonitor<T> lets it read the current value on demand, so there is no subscription and therefore nothing to leak. The general principle I took away is that the safest handler lifetime rule is the subscriber should not outlive the publisher, and if it might, do not subscribe.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just add a memory limit and let it restart cleanly?

Because a scheduled restart hides a bug whose growth rate is not guaranteed to stay constant — a leak that takes 40 hours today takes 4 hours when traffic triples, and now your mitigation is a restart loop during your busiest period. It also costs you every in-flight request at the restart and makes the service's memory behaviour unreadable, so the next genuine leak is invisible against the noise. I do use automatic restarts, as a safety net beneath a fix rather than instead of one, and I would want an alert on the restart itself so it can never become silent.

What if the dump had shown nothing growing on the managed heap?

Then I would be looking outside it, and the diagnosis changes shape: native memory from an interop library or a native dependency, unmanaged handles from undisposed objects, memory-mapped files, or fragmentation of the large object heap making the working set grow while live bytes do not. The tools change too — the working set versus the GC heap size counters diverging is the tell, and from there it is dotnet-dump and native heap tracking rather than a gcdump. LOH fragmentation is the one that fools people most often, because the managed heap looks stable while the process climbs.

At what scale would the IDisposable fix have been insufficient?

It is only correct while every subscriber is actually disposed, and that is a property of the container's scope handling rather than of the code — anything constructed outside a scope, or captured by a background task that outlives the request, leaks again silently. At higher complexity I would not rely on it: the pull-based IOptionsMonitor version has no subscription and therefore no invariant to maintain, and that is why it was the real fix. The general rule I apply now is that a correctness property maintained by discipline will fail at some team size, and if I can restructure so the property is unnecessary, that beats enforcing it.

What did you trade away with the quick fix?

I shipped a four-line change to production during an incident with a single unit test, which is a real risk I took knowingly — the alternative was another 40-hour cycle and a colleague being paged at 4 a.m. What made it acceptable was that the change was additive and trivially reversible, and that I said in the channel exactly what I had done and what I had not verified. The debt I created was the invariant nobody else knew about, and I paid it down the following week rather than leaving it, which is the part I would want to be judged on.

How do you stop this class of bug generally?

Three things, in order of leverage. Avoid long-lived publishers with short-lived subscribers as a design rule — prefer pull over push, which is exactly what IOptionsMonitor gives you. Where a subscription is genuinely needed across lifetimes, return an IDisposable registration from the subscribe call so the API makes unsubscription the obvious thing (which is what IChangeToken and OnChange do). And, operationally, alert on memory growth rate rather than only on the threshold, because a leak is visible days before it is fatal, and that alert would have caught this in the first week rather than the fifth.

19The suite that only failed in Istanbul

Senior CI / test reliability improvement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Our CI suite passed about 88% of the time on an unchanged commit. Everyone knew the number and nobody owned it; the ritual was to re-run the build, and a re-run took nine minutes, so the team was burning something like four hours a week on retries plus the much larger hidden cost — a red build no longer meant anything, so genuine failures got re-run too. I picked it up as a two-week project and the first thing I did was stop calling them "flaky tests", because that word describes a symptom and lumps together at least four unrelated bugs.

I made the failures visible before fixing anything: every test run published its results, and a small job aggregated failures by test name over 200 runs. Twelve tests accounted for 90% of the noise, and they fell into four groups with four different root causes.

The culture one is the story's namesake and the one worth knowing cold. "file".ToUpper() in the Turkish locale produces "FİLE" — a dotted capital I — so a case-insensitive comparison written as a.ToLower() == b.ToLower() is wrong in a way that only shows up on some machines. Our build agents had been re-imaged and some came up with a different locale, which is why a suite that had been stable for a year started failing. The correct code is an explicit comparison:

// Wrong: allocates, and is culture-dependent (the Turkish 'I' problem)
if (header.ToLower() == "content-type") { }

// Right: no allocation, no culture, and the intent is stated
if (header.Equals("content-type", StringComparison.OrdinalIgnoreCase)) { }

// And for anything that must render the same everywhere
var s = value.ToString(CultureInfo.InvariantCulture);

The rule I would state in an interview: ordinal for anything the machine reads, culture-aware only for what a human reads. Identifiers, headers, file paths, protocol tokens and database keys are all machine-read and should all be ordinal. Sorting a list of names for display is the rare case that genuinely wants the user's culture. We made CA1307 and CA1309 build errors (story 17) so the class could not come back.

The result was a suite at 99.7% on unchanged commits, and — the number I actually cared about — a red build that people investigated instead of re-running. I also introduced a quarantine mechanism with teeth: a test that fails intermittently gets moved to a quarantined suite that does not block the build, but the quarantine has a two-week expiry, after which the test is either fixed or deleted. Quarantine without an expiry is just a slower way of deleting tests, and I have watched that happen.

What I would do differently: I would have started with the data collection on the first day rather than the third. I spent two days fixing the tests I personally found annoying, which turned out to be three of the twelve — my intuition about which tests were flaky was mostly wrong, which was humbling and is exactly why the aggregation job was worth building.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you take responsibility for a shared, unowned problem, and whether you treat "flaky" as a diagnosis or as a category to break down. They want measurement before and after and a mechanism that keeps the gain — because everyone has cleaned up a test suite once, and almost nobody has kept one clean. The failure modes: deleting or [Skip]-ing the failures and calling it fixed; adding retries at the test-runner level, which hides real race conditions; and a story with no number attached to either end.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just enable automatic retries and move on?

Because a retry converts a race condition into a probabilistic pass, and the race is usually in the production code rather than in the test. Every flaky test I fixed in that batch except the culture ones was pointing at something real: a shared static, an assumption about ordering, a wait that would also be wrong under load. Retries would have hidden all of them and would have quietly told the team that non-determinism is acceptable. I do use one retry for genuinely external dependencies in an integration suite, tagged and counted so we can see if the rate changes — but never on a unit test, where a retry is an admission that the test does not know what it is asserting.

What if a test is flaky because the system genuinely is non-deterministic?

Then the assertion is wrong, not the system. A test for something concurrent should assert an invariant that holds under any interleaving — no lost updates, no duplicate side effects, the total is conserved — rather than a specific sequence. When I cannot express the invariant, that is usually a sign the design has an ambiguity I have not resolved, and the test is telling me something useful about the system rather than about itself. For genuinely timing-dependent behaviour I control the clock with TimeProvider and the scheduling with explicit synchronisation, so the test is deterministic even though production is not.

At what scale does this approach stop working?

The classification approach scales fine; the fixing does not, once the suite is large enough that twelve tests is not 90% of the noise. On a suite of tens of thousands of tests across many teams, you need the flakiness data per owning team with the fix as their responsibility, plus automatic quarantine with an expiry — the central team provides the mechanism and the visibility, not the fixes. The other thing that changes is runtime: at that size the nine-minute feedback loop becomes an hour, and test selection and sharding matter more than flakiness. I would still start the same way, with measurement, because unmeasured flakiness is never as evenly distributed as people assume.

What did you trade away?

Two weeks of feature work, and a slightly less realistic test suite. Pinning the culture and injecting the clock means the tests no longer exercise the machine's real environment — so a genuine culture bug in production code could pass a test that pins InvariantCulture everywhere. I covered that with a small number of tests that deliberately run under a hostile locale, which is the shape I would recommend: determinism by default, plus a handful of tests whose entire purpose is the environment. The quarantine also traded some coverage for signal, temporarily and with an expiry date, which I think is the right way round.

How did you keep it from regressing?

The analyzer rules made the culture class unrepeatable, the TimeProvider rule did the same for the clock, and the aggregation dashboard stayed up so the pass rate is a number the team sees weekly rather than a feeling. The quarantine expiry is the part that does the ongoing work: it makes ignoring a flaky test cost something, on a schedule, without anyone needing to advocate. My general belief after this project is that reliability work regresses unless the prevention is mechanical, because the human attention that fixed it is exactly the resource that gets reallocated the following quarter.

20"Ship it on the C# 15 preview"

Principal pushing back on a stakeholder

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A senior stakeholder — not my manager, but someone whose opinion set direction — came back from a conference convinced we should build our new rules engine on C# 15's union types. He was not wrong that they fit the problem; discriminated unions are close to the ideal shape for a rules engine's result type, and I had said so myself in a design review. The problem was that C# 15 is in preview, shipping with .NET 11 in November 2026, and he wanted to start in September and go live in December.

The mistake I did not make, and have made before: arguing about the technology. If I had opened with "preview features are risky", the conversation becomes my caution against his ambition, and that is a conversation I lose regardless of who is right. Instead I asked what he was buying. The answer was real — he wanted the rules engine to be understandable by the domain experts, and he believed a closed set of cases was the way to get there. That is a goal I agreed with, which meant we were no longer arguing.

Then I laid out what preview actually means, factually and without adjectives. Preview language features require <LangVersion>preview</LangVersion>, and the resulting build is not covered by the support policy — no servicing, no security patches for the preview SDK, and Microsoft explicitly does not support preview releases in production. Preview features change between previews; the shape of a feature in Preview 6 is not a promise about GA, and code written against the earlier shape has to be rewritten. And the ecosystem lags: analyzers, the debugger, the formatter and third-party tooling all handle a new language construct at their own pace, so the developer experience is worse in ways that are hard to price up front.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

What if he had insisted?

I would have made the risk explicit and owned in writing — an email naming the specific exposures (unsupported runtime in production, no security servicing, feature shape may change before GA) and asking him to confirm he accepted them, then I would have built it well. That is not passive aggression; it is how a decision with real risk should be recorded, and I would have said as much to his face rather than sending it silently. What I would not do is drag my feet or build it badly to prove a point. I would also have set a checkpoint — if a preview change broke us once, we revisit — so the decision had a review date rather than being permanent by default.

Is there a case where you would ship on a preview?

Yes, and being able to say so is what stops this reading as reflexive conservatism. An internal tool with no customer exposure, a spike branch, a service we could roll back in minutes, or a case where the preview feature is the only way to meet a genuine requirement and the alternative is worse. I have shipped preview runtime features to an internal build system for exactly that reason. The distinguishing questions are blast radius and reversibility, not novelty — and for a customer-facing rules engine in a regulated domain, both answers were wrong.

At what point does staying on LTS become the risky choice?

When the version you are on stops being serviced, which is precisely the situation .NET 8 and .NET 9 are in right now — both reach end of support on 10 November 2026, and after that "stable" means "unpatched". Conservatism has an expiry date, and the failure mode I have seen more often than reckless adoption is the team that stayed on a comfortable version until upgrading became a multi-quarter project. That is why my adoption policy has both halves: previews go in spike branches, and there is a named date each year for evaluating and scheduling the move to the new LTS. Standing still is a decision that also accrues risk, just more quietly.

What did you trade away?

A better type model for six months, and some of his enthusiasm — he was excited, and "not yet" is deflating however well you deliver it. The sealed hierarchy is genuinely more verbose than the union version will be, and the team paid that cost in every switch expression for two releases. I also spent real capital: saying no to a director is a withdrawal, and I would not spend it on something with a smaller downside than an unsupported production runtime. Getting the migration done in March is what refilled the account, and I made a point of crediting the original idea to him when we shipped it.

How do you keep this from happening every November?

The one-page adoption policy, written in advance and agreed once: LTS in production, current STS considered for services with fast rollback, previews confined to spike branches with no production deployment, and a fixed slot each January to evaluate what shipped in November. The value is not the content, which is unremarkable — it is that the argument happens in the abstract, when nobody has a project riding on the answer, so when it comes up under pressure I am pointing at a shared decision rather than making a personal judgment call. That is the general move: convert recurring judgment calls into policy while the stakes are low.

They want: the shift from doing to enabling. The signal phrase is turning a repeated human correction into a mechanical one, and knowing which rules deserve to be errors. Someone who lists their own code standards and not the adoption mechanism is describing a preference, not leadership.

Tell me about a time you had to get non-feature work funded.

Deploy: story 09 (killing the service locator) — the debt attached to three named incidents; story 16 (the cost project) for the version where the business asked first.

They want: translation. Can you express engineering value in the currency the business already counts — incidents, hours, money, risk — without inflating it? And the ratchet: what stopped it from stalling at 60%.

How do you make a decision when the team is split?

Deploy: story 02 (records versus entities) for the design version, story 05 for the peer version, story 04 (migration sequencing) for the "here is the invariant, here is the flexible part" version.

They want: a decision procedure. Time-boxed evidence gathering, an explicit decider, a written outcome, and a review date. The anti-pattern is consensus-seeking that never converges, and its twin, deciding by seniority and calling it alignment.

Ambiguity, prioritisation and delivery

Tell me about a project with unclear requirements.

Deploy: story 13 (the state machine nobody could agree on).

They want: progress under uncertainty, plus a move that reduced the ambiguity at source. The best detail in that story is discovering that two "different" states were the same state with different departmental labels — it proves you talked to people rather than waited for a document.

Describe a time you had to cut scope to hit a date.

Deploy: story 11 (source generators deferred, with a written trigger).

They want: a designed fallback and a deferral that actually happened. Name the seam that made the later change cheap, and give the date the deferred work shipped. The failure is the hero narrative — delivering everything by working weekends — which reads as a future burnout risk for the team you would lead.

How do you decide what not to do?

Deploy: story 16 (skipping the obvious optimisation for the fleet-wide misconfiguration), story 15 (stopping a project you owned).

They want: an explicit opportunity-cost argument with numbers in it. The 9%-of-spend-versus-40%-of-spend comparison is the kind of reasoning they are listening for. Vague prioritisation frameworks recited by name land much worse than one real trade-off with arithmetic.

Tell me about a time you had to work with incomplete information.

Deploy: story 12 (proving what had and had not been corrupted), story 06 (a silent wrong answer with no exception to guide you).

They want: how you bound uncertainty rather than eliminate it. Establishing blast radius, stating a confidence level honestly ("we verified a 1% sample"), and choosing the reversible action first. Overstating certainty is the failure they are watching for.

Craft and technical judgment

What do you look for in a code review?

Deploy: story 17 (what a machine should catch versus what a human should), plus a concrete catch from story 06 or story 09.

They want: a hierarchy. Correctness and lifetime issues first, design and naming second, style never — because style should be automated. Naming one specific C# hazard you always check for (a mutable struct in a collection, an undisposed subscription, an IEnumerable returned from a repository) makes the answer concrete instead of aspirational.

How do you keep current with C# and .NET?

Deploy: facts rather than a story — but anchor them in something you actually did. C# 14 on .NET 10 LTS (GA 11 November 2025, servicing 10.0.11 as of August 2026); .NET 8 and .NET 9 both end support on 10 November 2026; C# 15 and .NET 11 are in preview with GA expected November 2026, bringing union types, extension indexers and collection expression arguments.

They want: currency with judgment attached, not a feature list. The strong shape is: here is a recent change, here is where I would use it, here is where I would not yet. Story 20 is the "would not yet" and story 04 is the "and here is how we actually moved".

What is a C# feature you think is overused, and one you think is underused?

Deploy: overused — dynamic, reflection-based magic, and mutable structs used as a micro-optimisation (story 06). Underused — TimeProvider (story 12), required members (story 03), source-generated logging and serialization (story 11), analyzers as executable standards (story 17).

They want: an opinion with a reason and a boundary. "Never use X" is weaker than "X is right when Y, and I have seen it misapplied when Z". This question is also a low-risk chance to show you know what shipped recently, so reach for something from C# 12 to 14 rather than from 2015.

Values and motivation

What kind of work do you want to be doing, and why this role?

Deploy: whichever two stories genuinely energised you — for most people at this level it is the systemic ones (14, 16, 17) rather than the heroic ones (18). Say which and why.

They want: coherence between what you say you value and what your stories show you spending time on. If you say you love mentoring, story 10 had better exist. If you say you care about reliability, you should be able to name what you fixed that nobody asked you to. The failure is a values answer that none of your other answers support — interviewers cross-reference far more than candidates expect.

22Night-before cheat sheet

10 sentences read aloud once

Read these out loud once the night before. They are delivery rules, not content — the content is in your own stories by now.

  1. Lead with the situation in one sentence, then stop. "A pricing endpoint had a 40 ms p99 that was breaking a client timeout." One line of context earns you the next two minutes; three minutes of preamble spends them.
  2. Say what was yours in the first thirty seconds. "I was incident lead" or "I proposed it and I ran it" or "I was one of four" — the interviewer is trying to work this out anyway, and volunteering it buys more credibility than any technical detail.
  3. Quantify the result, and quantify the baseline. "p99 from 40 ms to 11 ms" beats "much faster", and both beat a number with nothing to compare it to. If you only have one number, give the one the business cared about.
  4. Separate the isolated number from the system number. "The parser got 8x, the pipeline got 5x, the customer got their 20-minute window" is the honest shape. Quoting a micro-benchmark as a system result is the most common way good engineers accidentally overclaim.
  5. Own the "what I would do differently" before you are asked. Volunteering it converts the question into evidence of judgment. Make it a real cost — the two days you spent reading code before taking a trace — not a humble-brag about thoroughness.
  6. Separate "I" from "we" honestly, in both directions. Take the mistake in the first person and share the recovery. Interviewers notice a candidate who says "we" about a failure and "I" about the win, and they notice the reverse even faster.
  7. Tie every technical decision back to a real C# trade-off. Records buy value equality and cost you EF's change tracking; spans buy zero allocation and cost you readability and error messages; a sealed hierarchy buys compile-time exhaustiveness and costs you a persistence story. Naming the cost is what makes the choice sound like a decision.
  8. Be precise about versions. C# 14 on .NET 10 LTS is the current baseline; required is C# 11, field and extension members are C# 14, TimeProvider is .NET 8, DATAS is on by default with Server GC from .NET 9, and C# 15's unions are preview until November 2026. Vagueness about when something arrived reads as second-hand knowledge.
  9. Have one very recent fact ready. .NET 8 and .NET 9 both reach end of support on 10 November 2026, and .NET 10 is serviced monthly (10.0.11 in August 2026). It takes eight seconds to say and it establishes that you are current without any claim about yourself.
  10. Answer the drill-down that was asked. When they ask "at what scale does that break?", give a threshold and a mechanism — not a longer version of the story. And when you do not know, say "I have not run it at that scale; here is what I would expect to break first and how I would find out", which is a strong answer and is never mistaken for one.

The one-line version. Situation in a sentence, your role immediately, a number with a baseline, the trade-off named out loud, and the thing you would do differently offered before it is asked. Every other rule on this page is a special case of those five.
