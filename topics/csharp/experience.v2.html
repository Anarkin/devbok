01The p99 that only appeared under load

Senior Performance problem diagnosed thread pool · sync-over-async

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The order-lookup API was fine in every environment except the one that mattered. In dev and staging a request took 12 ms; in production, between 09:00 and 09:20 every weekday, p99 latency climbed from 40 ms to 6–9 seconds while CPU sat at 15%. The pods were not busy. They were waiting. Autoscaling fired on request latency, added three pods, and the new pods were slow within a minute of taking traffic, which told me the problem shipped with the process, not with the machine.

I took a dotnet-counters capture from a live pod. Two numbers told the story: ThreadPool Thread Count creeping upward by roughly one thread per second, and ThreadPool Queue Length sitting in the hundreds. That is the signature of thread-pool starvation: every worker thread is blocked, the queue fills, and the pool's hill-climbing heuristic injects new threads slowly (on the order of one or two per second once it is above the minimum), so recovery is measured in minutes. A dotnet-stack dump showed 30 of 32 worker threads parked in Task.Result under a method called GetCustomerTier. Somebody had wrapped an async HTTP call to the CRM service in a synchronous helper, tier = GetTierAsync(id).Result, because the caller was a legacy synchronous interface. That is sync-over-async: the blocked thread holds a pool thread while the continuation it is waiting for also needs a pool thread to complete. Under light load the pool has spares and nobody notices. At 09:00, when the CRM slowed to 400 ms for its own reasons, the arithmetic flipped: more requests arrived per second than the pool could free threads, and the whole process wedged. The classic UI-context deadlock is a different mechanism (a captured SynchronizationContext), but ASP.NET Core has no sync context, so people wrongly assume .Result is safe there. It is not deadlock-safe under load; it is starvation-prone.

The fix had three layers. The immediate mitigation was ThreadPool.SetMinThreads raised from the default (one per core) to 200 so the pool injected threads without waiting for hill-climbing; that got p99 under a second inside ten minutes and bought us the day. The real fix was making the interface async end to end (Task<Tier> GetTierAsync), which touched 14 call sites and one third-party adapter. The lasting fix was a Roslyn analyzer rule that fails the build on .Result, .Wait() and .GetAwaiter().GetResult() outside a small allow-listed folder, plus an alert on ThreadPool Queue Length > 50 for 60 seconds.

// Before: a pool thread blocks while its continuation also needs a pool thread.
public Tier GetCustomerTier(Guid id)
    => _crm.GetTierAsync(id).Result;          // sync-over-async

// After: async all the way; the pool thread is returned while the HTTP call is in flight.
public async Task<Tier> GetCustomerTierAsync(Guid id, CancellationToken ct)
    => await _crm.GetTierAsync(id, ct);

// Diagnosis, from a live pod (no restart, no code change):
//   dotnet-counters monitor -p <pid> System.Runtime
//   dotnet-stack report -p <pid>      // which frames are parked in Task.Result?

What I would do differently: I had the counters, but I had no alert on them, so a customer told us before the dashboard did. I also under-communicated the SetMinThreads change, which is a global setting with a memory cost per thread (roughly 1 MB of reserved stack each) and should be a temporary lever, not a permanent one. On .NET 10 I would still reach for the same tools; the runtime's starvation heuristics have improved since .NET 6 (the pool detects blocking on Task.Wait and injects faster), but they only soften the cliff, they do not remove it.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you diagnose from evidence or from folklore. They want to hear the counter names, the reasoning from "CPU is idle but latency is high" to "threads are blocked", and a fix that separates mitigation from root cause. The common failure on this archetype is jumping to the answer ("it was sync-over-async") with no story of how you knew, or describing a heroic solo debug when three people were in the war room. Name the mechanism precisely: starvation, not deadlock.

(c) Recall prompts

When did you last watch C# behave differently under load than in dev?

Think of anything with a "works on my machine" gap: thread-pool starvation, connection-pool exhaustion, GC pauses only visible with a real heap size, lock contention that needs real concurrency. .Result, .Wait(), Task.Run(...).Result in a controller, a synchronous IDisposable flushing over the network, an async void event handler. Where was it, who wrote it and why (usually an interface that could not change), and what did it cost?

A raised min-thread count, a longer timeout, a bigger pod. What was the cost of leaving it, and what process would have retired it?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does blocking on a task starve the pool, when the pool can create more threads?

Because thread injection is deliberately slow. The pool keeps a minimum number of threads (by default one per logical core) ready; above that, its hill-climbing algorithm adds threads only when throughput measurably improves, at roughly one to two per second. A blocked thread produces no throughput signal, so the pool is reluctant. Meanwhile each incoming request consumes a thread that will block, so demand for threads grows faster than supply. The continuation that would unblock the waiter needs a free pool thread too, which is exactly what is scarce. Newer runtimes (since .NET 6) detect blocking in Task.Wait and inject more aggressively, which is why the cliff is less sharp on .NET 10, but a downstream slowdown still turns into a queue.

What if the interface genuinely cannot become async, say a third-party synchronous plugin contract?

Then I isolate the damage rather than pretend it is gone. Options in order of preference: push the async boundary outward so the synchronous contract is entered from a dedicated thread rather than a pool thread; run the blocking work on a bounded dedicated scheduler with its own threads and a queue limit so it cannot consume the shared pool; or, as a last resort, keep .GetAwaiter().GetResult() but on a code path that is rate-limited and load-tested. What I would not do is raise SetMinThreads and call it solved: that hides the cost until the next slow downstream.

At what scale does async-all-the-way itself stop being enough?

When the bottleneck moves from threads to the resources behind them. Async frees pool threads, so a slow downstream now shows up as thousands of in-flight requests holding memory, sockets and database connections instead of thirty blocked threads. That is where you need explicit backpressure: a concurrency limiter or rate limiter in front of the downstream call, timeouts with a budget, and circuit breaking so a dead dependency fails fast. Async changes where the queue lives; it does not delete the queue.

What did you trade away by making the interface async?

A larger diff and a viral signature change: every caller became async, tests changed shape, and one third-party adapter needed a wrapper. Async methods also allocate a state machine when they actually suspend, so a hot path that almost never awaits can be slightly slower; ValueTask helps where the result is usually synchronous, at the cost of stricter usage rules (await once, do not store). I accepted all of that because a correct signature that costs a few nanoseconds beats a fast one that wedges the process.

How did you get the team to accept an analyzer that fails the build on .Result?

By showing the incident timeline next to the rule, allow-listing the two legitimate call sites in one folder with a comment naming the reason, and starting it as a warning for one sprint so people saw the count go down rather than the build go red. Rules land when they are traceable to pain the team felt and have an escape hatch that is visible in review.

What would you do differently on .NET 10 today?

Same diagnosis, quicker: dotnet-counters now exposes the pool's queue and thread-count counters cleanly, and I would export them through OpenTelemetry from day one rather than attaching tools during the incident. I would also load-test with a deliberately slow fake downstream, because that is the condition that reveals sync-over-async, and it is never the condition a normal load test creates.

02The leak that survived every restart

Senior Nasty debugging session GC · event handlers · gcdump

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A background worker that processed pricing updates had a daily restart in its deployment manifest, added by someone who had left, with the comment "memory". Nobody had questioned it. When we moved the worker to a 512 MB container it started being OOM-killed around 14:00 every day, so the restart no longer hid the problem. Managed heap grew about 18 MB an hour, flat during the night, steeper during trading hours, which tied it to message volume rather than time.

The instinct on the team was to blame the message client, so my first job was to stop the guessing. I took two dotnet-gcdump captures an hour apart and diffed them in Visual Studio's memory analyser. The type whose count grew was PriceUpdatedEventArgs: 41,000 instances at 10:00, 63,000 at 11:00, all retained. Walking the retention path showed each one held by a closure, held by a delegate, held by the invocation list of a static event on a PriceCache singleton. The subscriber was a per-message PriceHandler that subscribed in its constructor and never unsubscribed. This is the GC lesson worth telling in an interview: the .NET garbage collector is a tracing collector, it frees anything not reachable from a root, and a static field is a root that lives as long as the AppDomain. An event is a multicast delegate whose invocation list holds strong references to every subscriber's target, so a subscriber that forgets to unsubscribe from a long-lived publisher is reachable forever. It is not that the GC "missed" anything; we told it, precisely, to keep those objects. The generational side explained the shape of the graph: the objects survived gen0 and gen1 and were promoted to gen2, where collections are rare and expensive, so the growth looked smooth and the occasional gen2 collection freed nothing.

The fix was mechanical but the design was the point. The handler became a scoped service resolved per message, the subscription moved to an IDisposable registration that unsubscribed on dispose, and the static event was replaced with a System.Threading.Channels pipeline so publishers no longer held subscribers at all. I removed the daily restart the same week, which was the real test. Memory settled at 140 MB flat. Six weeks later the manifest still had no restart line.

// The leak: a static publisher holds every subscriber via the delegate's invocation list.
public static class PriceCache
{
    public static event EventHandler<PriceUpdatedEventArgs>? PriceUpdated;   // GC root
}

public sealed class PriceHandler                                                // created per message
{
    public PriceHandler() => PriceCache.PriceUpdated += OnPrice;               // never removed
    private void OnPrice(object? s, PriceUpdatedEventArgs e) { /* ... */ }
}

// The fix: ownership is explicit and the subscription's lifetime is the subscriber's.
public sealed class PriceHandler : IDisposable
{
    public PriceHandler() => PriceCache.PriceUpdated += OnPrice;
    public void Dispose()  => PriceCache.PriceUpdated -= OnPrice;
    private void OnPrice(object? s, PriceUpdatedEventArgs e) { /* ... */ }
}

// Evidence, not opinion:
//   dotnet-gcdump collect -p <pid>       (twice, an hour apart; diff by type count)
//   dotnet-counters monitor -p <pid> --counters System.Runtime[gc-heap-size,gen-2-gc-count]

What I would do differently: I should have asked why the restart existed before the container move, not after. A daily restart is a leak with a bow on it. I would also now put a heap-size alert on every long-running worker, with a slope rather than a threshold, because a threshold fires at 14:00 and a slope fires at 10:00.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can explain the GC as a reachability machine rather than magic, and whether your debugging is evidence-driven. The mistakes on this archetype: naming a tool without saying what it showed, blaming a library without a retention path, and skipping the "why did this exist for a year" question. A senior story includes the systemic reason the leak went unnoticed.

(c) Recall prompts

Which .NET process have you seen leak, and what was actually holding the objects?

Static events, caches without eviction (a Dictionary used as a cache), HttpClient instances per request, timers that were never disposed, ILogger scopes captured in closures, MemoryCache with no size limit, EF Core change trackers on a long-lived DbContext. Name the root.

When did you take a memory dump or heap snapshot, and what did you compare it with?

One snapshot shows what is there; two show what is growing. If you only ever took one, say what you would do next time (two, an hour apart, diff by type count).

Have you found a "daily restart" or similar workaround hiding a real bug?

Cron restarts, recycling settings, an oversized pod, a cache clear on a timer. What did removing it reveal?

Can you explain, in two sentences, why a gen2-heavy heap means a leak rather than churn?

Objects that die young are collected in gen0 cheaply; a rising gen2 size with gen2 collections that free little means the objects are still reachable from a root, so the collector is behaving correctly and the program's ownership is wrong.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does the GC not collect the subscribers when nothing "uses" them?

Because "used" is not the criterion, reachability is. The collector marks from roots (statics, thread stacks, GC handles, finalization queue) and frees only what it never reached. A static event's delegate holds a strong reference to each subscriber's target object, so every subscriber is reachable from a root and is, by definition, live. The generational design makes it worse to observe: survivors get promoted to gen2, which is collected rarely, so the graph grows smoothly and the occasional full GC frees nothing. That is the difference between a leak and churn, and it is why the fix is ownership, not tuning.

What if you cannot change the publisher, say it is in a library you do not own?

Then I control the subscriber's lifetime instead. A weak event pattern (the subscriber is held through a WeakReference and the handler unsubscribes itself when the target is gone) works but is fiddly and hides ownership. In practice I prefer a single long-lived adapter that subscribes once to the library event and republishes into a channel or an observable with explicit disposal, so exactly one object is held by the library forever and everything downstream has a normal lifetime.

At what scale would the fix you chose (channels) break?

An unbounded channel turns a leak of subscribers into a leak of messages when consumers fall behind, so I used a bounded channel with BoundedChannelFullMode.Wait to apply backpressure to the producer; for price updates where only the latest matters, DropOldest would be the better policy. Beyond one process, a channel is the wrong tool and the same question becomes "which broker, what retention, what consumer-lag alert".

What did you trade away?

The event-based design was decoupled and easy to add to; channels made the flow explicit, which is more code and a small learning curve for the team. I also traded a little latency for the bounded queue. I accepted it because explicit ownership is the property that made the leak impossible, not merely fixed.

How does DATAS on .NET 9+ change this story?

It does not change the leak, and that is worth saying. Dynamic Adaptation To Application Sizes (default for Server GC since .NET 9) adjusts the number of heaps and the gen0 budget to the workload, which reduces committed memory for bursty services; it cannot free reachable objects. If anything it makes a leak more visible, because the committed heap tracks live data more closely instead of hiding under a large fixed budget.

03The outage the retry policy caused

Staff Production incident / outage HttpClient · resilience · incident command

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

At 11:42 the checkout service went from 0.1% errors to 70% in four minutes, and the postmortem later showed the trigger was a 30-second blip in the address-validation service. A blip should have cost us thirty seconds of degraded validation. It cost us 38 minutes of checkout because of what our own C# did in response. I was incident commander that day, which meant my first job was not to debug; it was to get one person on customer impact, one on the downstream, and one on our own service's metrics, and to stop everyone else from restarting things.

The metrics person found two things within ten minutes. First, socket count on the checkout pods was at the ephemeral port ceiling: tens of thousands of connections in TIME_WAIT. Second, outbound requests to address validation were running at eleven times the inbound request rate. The code review afterwards explained both. The address client was created as new HttpClient() per request inside a helper (the pre-IHttpClientFactory pattern that still exists in older codebases), so each request opened a fresh connection and, on dispose, left a socket in TIME_WAIT for the OS timeout; under normal load the OS kept up, under a slow downstream it did not, because slow responses meant more concurrent connections. Second, the retry policy retried three times with no backoff and no jitter, and the caller of that helper also retried three times, so one failing validation became up to twelve outbound calls. Nine hundred inbound requests a second became ten thousand outbound. We DDoSed our own dependency and then ran out of ports.

The mitigation in the incident was blunt and correct: scale the checkout pods to zero for the address feature via a feature flag (validation became advisory), which dropped outbound traffic to nothing and let the downstream recover in under two minutes. The durable fix was the modern shape: a typed client registered through IHttpClientFactory (or, equivalently, a singleton HttpClient over a SocketsHttpHandler with PooledConnectionLifetime set so DNS changes are honoured), and a single resilience pipeline using Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience with a total-request timeout, exponential backoff with jitter, a retry budget, and a circuit breaker. Retries were removed from the caller so that exactly one layer owns retry. I also introduced the rule that any retry policy must be able to state its worst-case amplification factor in the pull request.

// Legacy shape still common in codebases: sockets pile up, retries multiply.
using var client = new HttpClient();                       // per call: new connection, TIME_WAIT on dispose
for (var i = 0; i < 3; i++) { /* immediate retry, no jitter */ }

// Modern shape (.NET 8+, Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience): one owner of retry, bounded amplification.
services.AddHttpClient<AddressClient>(c => c.BaseAddress = new Uri("https://addr.internal"))
    .ConfigurePrimaryHttpMessageHandler(() => new SocketsHttpHandler
    {
        PooledConnectionLifetime = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(2)   // rotate connections so DNS changes are seen
    })
    .AddStandardResilienceHandler(o =>
    {
        o.Retry.MaxRetryAttempts = 2;                        // worst case 3 calls, not 12
        o.Retry.BackoffType = DelayBackoffType.Exponential;
        o.Retry.UseJitter = true;
        o.CircuitBreaker.SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);
        o.TotalRequestTimeout.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5);
    });

What I would do differently: the feature flag that saved us existed by luck, because a previous engineer had wanted to A/B the validator. Every non-critical dependency now has a kill switch by policy. And I would have written the amplification rule before the incident; it is a five-minute calculation that would have failed this design in review.

What the interviewer is really probing. Incident-command judgement: did you stabilise before you diagnosed, did you delegate, did you resist the restart reflex, and did the postmortem produce a systemic change rather than a patch. The failure modes: narrating the whole timeline with no decisions in it, taking credit for the metric hunt someone else did, or a "root cause" that is a single line of code rather than the missing constraint (retry ownership, amplification budget).

(c) Recall prompts

When did your own client code make a dependency's problem worse?

Retry storms, thundering herds after a cache expiry, connection-per-request patterns, timeouts longer than the caller's. Even a small one counts if you can name the multiplier.

Commander, scribe, the person on metrics, or the one who wrote the bug. Each is a valid story if you own it; the story that fails is the one where you were "leading" and also personally found every clue.

Have you seen socket or port exhaustion on a .NET service?

Symptoms: SocketException: Only one usage of each socket address, huge TIME_WAIT counts, timeouts that correlate with connection count rather than CPU. The fix is always the same: share the handler.

Which postmortem action of yours changed a review rule rather than a line of code?

That is the Staff-level result. If yours only changed code, say what rule you would add now.

When did you decide not to restart something during an incident?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does new HttpClient() per request cause port exhaustion, and why is a single static instance also wrong?

Each instance owns its own handler and connection pool, so disposing it closes connections whose sockets sit in TIME_WAIT for the OS timeout (up to four minutes on Windows), and the ephemeral port range is finite. A single long-lived instance fixes that but, with the old handler, never re-resolves DNS, so a failover behind a load balancer is invisible until restart. The modern answer is IHttpClientFactory (handlers are pooled and recycled every two minutes by default) or SocketsHttpHandler with PooledConnectionLifetime, which gives you sharing and DNS rotation together.

What if the downstream was not slow but genuinely down for an hour?

Then retries are pure waste and the circuit breaker is the mechanism that matters: after the failure ratio crosses the threshold it opens, calls fail immediately for the break duration, and one probe call decides when to close. The behaviour I want is fail-fast with a degraded experience (advisory validation, cached result, queued for later) rather than every request paying the full timeout. The incident showed the worse case, a slow dependency, because slow keeps the breaker half-alive and the connections occupied.

At what scale does a resilience pipeline itself become the bottleneck?

When the retry budget across a fleet still exceeds what the dependency can absorb. Per-instance backoff does not coordinate across 200 pods, so I add a retry budget as a ratio (say, retries at most 10% of first attempts, which Polly's rate limiting and hedging options support) and, at real scale, put the concurrency limit at the dependency's edge (a gateway or the dependency's own admission control) rather than trusting every client. The principle is that the callee, not the callers, should own the last line of defence.

How did you get four teams to stop writing their own retry loops?

By shipping a shared client-registration extension that made the correct thing the shortest thing to write, publishing the amplification rule with the incident as the worked example, and adding an analyzer that flags new HttpClient( outside test projects. Nobody argued with the rule after seeing the 11x number; the argument had been about taste, and the incident made it about arithmetic.

What would you do differently now, on .NET 10?

Use the standard resilience handler from the start, keep one owner of retry per call chain, export http.client metrics through OpenTelemetry (the built-in System.Net.Http meters cover connection counts and durations), and put an alert on outbound-to-inbound ratio, which is the earliest signal of amplification. I would also chaos-test the slow-dependency case quarterly; it is cheap with a fault-injecting handler in the same resilience library.

04Off .NET Framework in six months

Staff Migration / version upgrade .NET Framework 4.8 → .NET 10 · strangler

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The billing platform was 380,000 lines of C# on .NET Framework 4.8: two ASP.NET MVC 5 sites, a WCF service, eleven class libraries and a Windows Service, all on Windows VMs we wanted to retire. (a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were building an in-process ingestion pipeline: a document arrives, gets parsed, enriched with three external lookups, scored and written to a store, at a target of 2,000 documents a second per instance with a hard rule that memory stays under 1 GB no matter how far behind the writer falls. Three engineers proposed three shapes in the same week: TPL Dataflow blocks (one of them had used it for years), IAsyncEnumerable<T> with LINQ-style operators (elegant, testable), and System.Threading.Channels with hand-written stages. I owned the decision, and the trade-off was genuinely close, so I made everyone build the same three-stage slice and measure it rather than argue.

The results shaped the decision more than the opinions did. IAsyncEnumerable was the cleanest to read, but it is a pull model with a single consumer per enumerator: parallelising the enrichment stage meant either buffering (which broke the memory rule) or a custom parallel operator that was, in effect, a channel in disguise. TPL Dataflow gave us bounded capacity and parallelism per block out of the box, and the slice hit 2,400 docs/s, but its completion and fault propagation model is subtle (a faulted block does not complete its linked targets unless PropagateCompletion is set, and exceptions surface as AggregateException from Completion) and it lives in a separate NuGet package that the runtime team maintains but does not evolve. Channels won on control: a bounded channel between each stage with BoundedChannelFullMode.Wait gave real backpressure (the parser awaited WriteAsync when the enricher fell behind), stage parallelism was N consumer tasks reading the same channel, cancellation was a plain CancellationToken, and the whole thing was about 120 lines with no dependency beyond the BCL. The slice did 2,200 docs/s with a 60 MB working set under a stalled writer, against 700 MB for the unbounded first draft.

// Bounded stages: the queue has a size, so a slow writer slows the parser instead of growing the heap.
var parsed   = Channel.CreateBounded<ParsedDoc>(new BoundedChannelOptions(500) { FullMode = BoundedChannelFullMode.Wait, SingleWriter = true });
var enriched = Channel.CreateBounded<EnrichedDoc>(new BoundedChannelOptions(500) { FullMode = BoundedChannelFullMode.Wait });

// Stage 2: four parallel consumers of one channel, one writer target.
var enrichers = Enumerable.Range(0, 4).Select(_ => Task.Run(async () =>
{
    await foreach (var doc in parsed.Reader.ReadAllAsync(ct))
        await enriched.Writer.WriteAsync(await EnrichAsync(doc, ct), ct);

// Completion flows forward: when all enrichers finish, the next stage's reader drains and ends.
_ = Task.WhenAll(enrichers).ContinueWith(t => enriched.Writer.TryComplete(t.Exception), TaskScheduler.Default);

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is IAsyncEnumerable a poor fit for a parallel stage, given it is async?

Because async is about not blocking a thread, not about concurrency. An IAsyncEnumerator is a single cursor: MoveNextAsync is called by one consumer at a time and the next element is produced on demand. To have four workers enriching concurrently you must either fan out into a buffer (which is a channel) or write an operator that runs N inner tasks and merges their results, which is also a channel. It is the right shape for streaming results to one reader, such as a minimal API endpoint returning IAsyncEnumerable, which ASP.NET Core streams as JSON without buffering.

What if a stage must preserve document order?

Then parallelism inside that stage needs a reorder step: tag each item with a sequence number, let N workers process out of order, and have a single consumer hold a small buffer that releases items in sequence, bounded so a stuck item cannot grow it without limit. Dataflow gives you this with EnsureOrdered on a TransformBlock, which is one of the places where its block library earns its keep; with channels you write about thirty lines. The honest answer is that ordering plus parallelism always costs a buffer, so I would first ask whether ordering is required end to end or only per key.

At what scale does an in-process pipeline break?

When you need durability across restarts, more than one instance's worth of throughput, or consumers that are not in the same deployment. At that point the channel becomes a broker (Kafka, a queue, or a database outbox) and every question changes: partitioning replaces ordering, consumer lag replaces bounded capacity, and at-least-once delivery means every stage must be idempotent. I would keep the in-process design until one of those three needs appears, because a broker adds operational cost that a 2,000/s single-instance workload does not justify.

What did you trade away by rejecting Dataflow?

A tested library for joins, broadcasts and batching, and an engineer's deep familiarity with it. Hand-written channel stages have to get completion and fault propagation right themselves, and I have seen pipelines hang because a stage swallowed an exception and never called TryComplete. We mitigated that with a stage base class and a test that injects a failure at each stage and asserts the pipeline ends. The revisit trigger in the ADR is the acknowledgement that this trade could reverse.

How did you keep the engineer whose option lost engaged?

By making their objection the revisit trigger in the decision record, in their words, and by asking them to review the completion and error handling, which was exactly the area they knew better than the rest of us. Losing an argument is fine; being ignored afterwards is what makes people stop bringing options.

Doing that clarified the actual disagreement. Their strongest point was that a declined card is not exceptional; it is one of the three normal outcomes of an authorisation, the caller must handle it, and an exception makes that handling invisible in the signature and expensive at runtime (a thrown exception in .NET costs on the order of tens of microseconds, with stack-trace capture, which matters when 8% of authorisations decline at 500 a second). We agreed on a boundary, which is the real answer. The Result type itself was dropped in favour of the domain types, because the generic wrapper had been the thing I distrusted, not the idea. In 2026 this is worth updating: C# 15 (preview in .NET 11 RC1, September 2026) adds union types and the closed modifier, so the compiler itself can prove a switch over AuthorisationOutcome is exhaustive; today on C# 14 the sealed-hierarchy plus analyzer approach is the shipping version of the same idea.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why are exceptions expensive in .NET, and when does it matter?

Throwing walks the stack to find a handler (two-pass SEH on Windows, a managed unwinder on all platforms since .NET 9, which made throw/catch roughly two to four times faster than .NET 8), captures the stack trace and allocates the exception object; a throw-catch round trip is on the order of ten to fifty microseconds depending on depth, against nanoseconds for a return. It matters when the rate is high, say thousands per second, or when it is on a latency-sensitive path; it does not matter for a genuinely rare failure. The design argument is stronger than the performance one: a signature that returns the outcome documents the cases; a throw hides them.

What if the team had already adopted a Result library everywhere?

Then the pragmatic move is to keep it and add the missing discipline: an analyzer that flags .Value access without a prior IsSuccess check, a rule that infrastructure failures still throw, and a mapping at the API edge so results do not leak into HTTP handlers as ad-hoc status codes. Ripping out a widely used pattern costs more than making it safe. I would schedule the migration to language-level unions when C# 15 is stable, because the compiler-checked version removes the need for the analyzer.

At what point does a closed outcome hierarchy break down?

When the set of outcomes is genuinely open, such as a plugin system where third parties add cases, or when the same outcome type is reused across bounded contexts and grows arms nobody handles. A closed set is a promise that the callers enumerate every case; if new cases arrive monthly, every caller changes monthly, and you have rediscovered why exceptions exist for the unforeseen. The tell is a switch with a growing default arm.

What did you concede, concretely?

That "card declined" was not exceptional, which meant admitting that the throw-based code I had defended for years had hidden a real case (a decline reason we never surfaced to support). I also conceded the performance point once I measured it. What they conceded was the generic Result wrapper and the idea that infrastructure failures belong in the same type.

How would the story change with C# 15 unions and closed hierarchies?

The compiler would prove exhaustiveness, so the analyzer and the UnreachableException arm disappear, and the runtime's IUnion support (from .NET 11 preview 5) gives a uniform way to inspect cases. I would not ship union types in production code until C# 15 is stable in .NET 11 in November 2026, because preview language features are outside the go-live support and their shape has already shifted between previews; see story 20 for that argument.

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A telemetry-ingest endpoint accepted batches of line-protocol text, parsed them and forwarded structured events. It handled 5,000 lines a second comfortably. The parser did line.Split(','), then Substring for each field, then string.Concat to build a key, then boxed each numeric field into an object[] for a reflection-based mapper. None of that is wrong at 5,000 a second. At 50,000 the allocation rate itself was the workload. The lesson worth teaching in an interview is that .NET's gen0 allocation is cheap (a pointer bump) but not free: every gen0 budget exhausted is a collection that stops the world briefly and promotes survivors, and a high allocation rate multiplies the chance that an in-flight batch is live during a collection and gets promoted, which is how a throughput problem becomes a latency problem.

The rewrite took the parser to ReadOnlySpan<char> end to end: slicing instead of substrings, MemoryExtensions.Split over spans (the Split enumerator on ReadOnlySpan<char> shipped in .NET 8), Utf8Parser and double.TryParse(ReadOnlySpan<char>) for numbers, a ref struct tokenizer, and ArrayPool<byte>.Shared for the output buffers. Why does a high allocation rate raise p99 latency and not just CPU?

Because gen0 collections happen when the gen0 budget fills, so the rate of allocation sets the rate of collections; each one pauses the threads briefly and, worse, promotes whatever is live at that instant. A request that is mid-flight during a collection gets its objects promoted to gen1 and later gen2, where they are collected far less often and more expensively. At a high enough rate you are effectively promoting your working set continuously, gen1 and gen2 collections become frequent, and those are the pauses that show up at p99. Cut allocations and you cut both the pause frequency and the promotion.

What if the data had to cross an await, where Span<T> is not allowed?

Then I use Memory<T> / ReadOnlyMemory<T> for the storage that crosses the await and slice a span from it inside the synchronous section, or restructure so the parsing is synchronous over a buffer that the async reader fills, which is the System.IO.Pipelines model: PipeReader.ReadAsync gives you a ReadOnlySequence<byte> and the parser is a synchronous function over it. The constraint is a feature: it forces the allocation-free part to be a pure function, which is also the testable part.

At what scale does this approach stop being enough?

When you have removed allocations and the bottleneck is genuinely CPU (the parsing work itself) or I/O. Then the levers are SIMD (System.Numerics.Vector or the SearchValues and vectorised IndexOfAny the BCL already uses internally), parallelism across cores, and finally scaling out, which is now cheap because each pod does ten times the work. The order matters: scaling out an allocation-bound service buys ten times the GC work for ten times the money.

What did you trade away?

Readability and onboarding cost. A span-based tokenizer with stackalloc and a ref struct is harder to read than Split, easier to get wrong, and the compiler's escape-analysis errors confuse newcomers. I kept the change to one file with a header comment stating the allocation budget, left the old parser as the reference implementation used by the property-based test, and made the benchmark part of CI so the fast path cannot silently regress. I also spent an hour teaching the ref-struct rules to the team.

How did you get sign-off for a week of profiling instead of the "obvious" scale-out?

With an estimate on both sides: ten extra pods at the cloud rate for a year versus one engineer-week, plus the observation that the scale-out plan had no answer for the next customer. I promised a go/no-go after two days of profiling, with the scale-out as the fallback. Framing it as a reversible time-box got a yes; framing it as "let me optimise" would not have.

What is different on .NET 10 for this kind of work?

The JIT does more stack allocation of small objects and arrays whose lifetime it can prove (escape analysis expanded in .NET 10), which removes some allocations for free; SearchValues, span-based Split and the Utf8 parsing APIs are mature; and C# 14's implicit span conversions mean fewer explicit .AsSpan() calls and more overloads picking the span version automatically. None of it replaces measuring: I would still run the same benchmark before and after the runtime upgrade to see what the JIT now does for me.

The classic example is a payload that sets a process-start object's properties; ysoserial.net generates these. Our consumer ran with a service account that could reach the database.

The technical lesson is that deserialisation is object construction driven by input, and any serialiser that lets the input choose the type is an object-instantiation primitive handed to the sender. BinaryFormatter is the historic worst case, which is why Microsoft removed it from the runtime in .NET 9 (calls throw unless you opt into the unsupported compatibility package). Newtonsoft's TypeNameHandling other than None, DataContractSerializer with unrestricted known types, SoapFormatter, NetDataContractSerializer and LosFormatter are the same class of problem. I treated it as a security incident, not a refactor: confirmed exploitability with a benign gadget in a sandbox (a payload that wrote a file), opened a private ticket, and paged the team lead rather than posting in the channel. The fix in the first 24 hours was a SerializationBinder that allow-listed the six concrete step types, deployed as a hotfix. The durable fix over two weeks moved the payload contract to System.Text.Json with declared derived types and a version field, kept a reader for the old shape until the queue drained, and added a CodeQL rule and a Roslyn analyzer (CA2326-CA2330 cover TypeNameHandling) to the build. We also rotated the service account and narrowed its database grants, because the fix that matters most is the one that limits what a successful exploit could have done.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is letting input choose the type equivalent to code execution?

Because deserialisation constructs objects and calls their setters, constructors and sometimes callbacks (OnDeserialized), and there exist types in common libraries whose setters do things: start a process, load an assembly from a path, write a file. A sender who can name the type and supply its properties can chain those behaviours; that is a gadget chain. The serialiser is doing exactly what it was told. The only real defence is that the set of constructible types is fixed by the code, not by the message.

What if the payload genuinely needed open polymorphism, say a plugin system?

Then the type set is still closed, just larger and registered at startup: each plugin registers the payload types it owns with a discriminator, the binder maps discriminators to that registry, and anything else is rejected. The message never carries an assembly-qualified type name. If a plugin could register arbitrary types, then plugin installation is the trust boundary and needs the controls (signing, review) that any code deployment needs.

At what scale does the allow-list binder break?

When there are dozens of services each maintaining their own list and the lists drift, so a new type works in one consumer and fails in another. The fix is to make the contract the source of truth: a shared contracts package with the [JsonDerivedType] declarations, versioned, so adding a step type is one change consumed everywhere. That is also why I preferred System.Text.Json's attribute-declared model to a runtime binder: the closed set is visible in the type, reviewed with it, and cannot be forgotten.

By keeping the finding about the trust boundary, which had moved, not about their reasoning, which had been correct at the time. I asked them to co-own the fix, which also meant they knew the code best. Blame makes the next person hide the next finding.

The pitch was the exception count and the on-call hours attached to it: 140 NREs a week in production, four of them customer-visible, roughly 30 hours a month of investigation.

The technical model matters for telling this story well. Nullable reference types are annotations plus flow analysis, not a runtime change: string? compiles to string with an attribute, and the compiler tracks the null-state of each variable through the method, warning where a maybe-null value is dereferenced or assigned to a non-nullable target. That gives you three properties to exploit. It is per-file granular (#nullable enable), so you can enable it from the leaves of the dependency graph outward and every enabled file is immediately protected. Annotations on public APIs are the contract that downstream files rely on, so annotating a shared library first makes every consumer's warnings meaningful instead of noise. The rollout was mechanical and measured. Week one: enable nullable at the solution level with warnings as warnings, count them per project, and commit the count as a baseline file. Week two onward: leaf projects first (contracts, then domain, then infrastructure, then hosts), one project per pull request, each PR reducing the count and setting <WarningsAsErrors>nullable</WarningsAsErrors> for that project once it hit zero so it could never regress. The rule for each warning was strict: fix the design if it is cheap (make the property required with C# 11's required, initialise in the constructor, return an empty collection), annotate if the null is real, and use the null-forgiving ! only with a comment naming the invariant that guarantees it. We tracked ! usage as a metric; it peaked at 300 and finished at 40. Entity Framework entities were the hardest area, because navigation properties are null until loaded; we settled on the documented pattern (= null!; for required navigations, ? for optional ones) and a required keyword on scalar properties.

Staff Scope / deadline negotiation records · primary constructors · collection expressions

The way I split it was by semantic risk: does the change alter behaviour, or only text? File-scoped namespaces and collection expressions (C# 12) are purely syntactic; the compiler emits the same code and a fixer can apply them across the solution in an hour with the IDE's dotnet format and code-style rules (IDE0161, IDE0300). I had a real example from a previous team: a record entity with a mutable collection property whose GetHashCode changed after it was added to a HashSet, which silently lost items. Primary constructors on classes (C# 12) capture parameters as hidden fields; a parameter used both for base initialisation and in a method body double-captures, and a captured IDisposable that nobody disposes is a leak with new syntax. Extension members (C# 14) are safe but pointless for an audit and unfamiliar to the team, so the review cost exceeded the benefit. Those three were deferred with a written reason each.

12The dictionary that ate our writes

Mid Data-loss / corruption scare thread safety · Dictionary · Lock · ConcurrentDictionary

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Support reported that a handful of customers' notification preferences were reverting overnight. Not all, not reproducibly, maybe twenty a week out of a million. The preferences service kept an in-memory Dictionary<Guid, Preferences> as a write-through cache in front of the database; reads hit the dictionary, writes updated the dictionary and then the database. I was the mid-level engineer who got the ticket and the first thing I did was fail to reproduce it for two days, because on my machine there was one request at a time.

The clue was in the logs, once I looked for the right thing: a InvalidOperationException: Operations that change non-concurrent collections must have exclusive access about once an hour, swallowed by a catch-all in the request pipeline. That exception is Dictionary<TKey,TValue> detecting concurrent mutation, and it means the class is not thread-safe and was being written from multiple request threads at once. The corruption mechanism is worth being able to explain: a Dictionary is an array of buckets and an array of entries; adding an item can trigger a resize that allocates new arrays and rehashes every entry, and a concurrent add during the resize can be written into the old arrays and simply disappear, or two adds can chain into the same bucket and create a cycle that makes a later lookup spin forever at 100% CPU. We had seen both: the lost writes were the "reverted preferences", and a pod that had pegged a core for hours two months earlier, restarted with a shrug, had been the same bug.

The fix had a right answer and a tempting wrong one. The tempting one was wrapping every access in lock, which works but serialises all reads. I used ConcurrentDictionary<Guid, Preferences>, which is lock-striped internally and lock-free for reads, but that alone was not enough: the code did a read-modify-write (get, change one flag, set), and two threads doing that on the same key still lose one update, thread-safe container or not. The atomic version is AddOrUpdate with an update function, and it only works if Preferences is immutable so the function returns a new instance; making it a record with with expressions gave exactly that. For the database side, the last-writer-wins race remained, so we added a row version and optimistic concurrency, which is a lesson in its own right: fixing the in-process race exposed the cross-process one. The .NET 9 System.Threading.Lock type came up in review as the modern lock target; we used it for the one place a critical section spanned the cache and a metric counter, because lock (someLock) on a Lock compiles to the faster EnterScope path rather than Monitor.

// The bug: Dictionary is not thread-safe, and read-modify-write is not atomic even if it were.
private readonly Dictionary<Guid, Preferences> _cache = new();
public void SetEmailOptIn(Guid id, bool value)
{
    var p = _cache[id];            // thread A and B both read the same instance
    p.EmailOptIn = value;          // mutable: both mutate, one write to the DB wins
    _cache[id] = p;                // concurrent mutation: resize races, lost entries, bucket cycles
    _db.Save(p);
}

// The fix: a concurrent map, an immutable value, and an atomic update function.
private readonly ConcurrentDictionary<Guid, Preferences> _cache = new();
public async Task SetEmailOptInAsync(Guid id, bool value, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var updated = _cache.AddOrUpdate(id,
        addValueFactory:    _ => Preferences.Default with { EmailOptIn = value },
        updateValueFactory: (_, current) => current with { EmailOptIn = value });
    await _db.SaveAsync(updated, ct);      // row version on the table; concurrency exception surfaces the cross-process race
}

// .NET 9+: a dedicated lock type for the remaining critical sections.
private readonly Lock _statsLock = new();
lock (_statsLock) { _writes++; _lastWrite = now; }     // compiles to Lock.EnterScope, not Monitor

I now treat InvalidOperationException from collections as a page, not a log line. And I would have looked at the "pod at 100% CPU, restarted" incident from two months earlier as evidence rather than noise.

The mistakes: "I switched to ConcurrentDictionary" with no mention of the read-modify-write race, no explanation of how a dictionary corrupts, or no follow-through on the data that was already lost.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why can a concurrent write to a Dictionary lose data or hang, rather than just throw?

Because the version check that throws is a best-effort detection, not a guard. The structure is two arrays (buckets and entries) plus a free list; an add may resize, which allocates new arrays and rehashes, and a concurrent add can land in the arrays being discarded. Two concurrent adds to the same bucket can link entries into a cycle, and a subsequent lookup follows the chain forever. The exception fires when the enumerator or a mutation notices a version mismatch, but by then the damage may be done. That is why the fix is a type designed for concurrency or exclusive access, never "catch the exception and retry".

What if the read-modify-write must span two keys, or the cache and the database, atomically?

Then a concurrent map's per-key atomicity is not enough and you need a real critical section: a Lock around the multi-key operation for in-process state, and for the database the transaction with a row version or a serialisable isolation level. I would keep the lock's scope tiny and never await inside it (lock does not allow await, and a SemaphoreSlim used as an async lock needs the same discipline). If the cross-key operation is common, the design is probably wrong: model the aggregate as one value under one key.

At what scale does ConcurrentDictionary stop being the answer?

When the state must survive a restart or be shared across instances, at which point the in-memory map is a cache and the source of truth is the database or a distributed cache, and the concurrency problem becomes optimistic concurrency, idempotency and cache invalidation. Also when a single hot key is updated thousands of times a second: the per-key lock stripe serialises those updates, and a design that batches or sharded counters wins.

Why immutability, rather than just locking the mutable object?

Because an immutable value makes the atomic update function trivially correct: it cannot be observed half-updated, it can be shared between threads without a lock, and the update is "replace the reference", which AddOrUpdate already does atomically. Locking a mutable object works but spreads the discipline to every reader, and one forgotten lock is the same bug again. Records with with expressions make the immutable version as short as the mutable one.

What would you say to the reviewer who suggested "just add lock everywhere"?

That it would have fixed the corruption and been acceptable as a first patch, and that I chose the concurrent map plus immutable values because the service was read-heavy (reads would have serialised under one lock) and because it made the read-modify-write race structurally impossible rather than just guarded. Both are defensible; the difference is where future mistakes can happen.

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We shipped a C# SDK, as a NuGet package, that customers embedded in their own applications to talk to our platform. When it started there were three customers and one use case; two years later there were 200 consumers, some on versions eighteen months old, and the product team's requirements changed monthly: a new authentication mode, then a retry policy customers could override, then telemetry hooks, then a request to remove a method a big customer said was "confusing". Every requirement was reasonable alone. Together they were a shifting target, and the SDK's real requirement, the one nobody had written down, was that a customer who updated the package must not have to change code or recompile their own dependents. I took ownership of the SDK's design at that point with the aim of making the requirement churn survivable rather than fighting it.

The engineering substance is compatibility, and it is more subtle in C# than people expect. Source compatibility (their code still compiles) and binary compatibility (their already-compiled assemblies still run against the new version) are different, and the second one bites when a customer's plugin was compiled against SDK 2.1 and runs against 2.4. Adding an optional parameter to a method is source-compatible and binary-breaking: the caller's compiled IL called a method with N parameters and that overload no longer exists. Adding a member to an interface is binary-breaking for any implementer, unless it is a default interface member (C# 8), which is precisely the tool for "extend an interface consumers implement". Changing a struct to a class, adding a field to a public struct, changing a constant's value (it is inlined into the caller), reordering enum values, sealing a class, or changing a method's return type from Task to ValueTask are all breaks that compile fine on our side and fail on theirs at runtime with a MissingMethodException or worse.

The design I put in place had four parts. First, an options-object pattern for every extensibility point (new ClientOptions { Retry = ... }) so new settings were added properties, never new parameters. Second, every interface consumers might implement got default members for additions, and every class they might derive from was either sealed or designed for it (the framework design guidelines' rule). Third, the Microsoft.CodeAnalysis.PublicApiAnalyzers package with PublicAPI.Shipped.txt and PublicAPI.Unshipped.txt, so every public surface change appeared as a diff in review and removals required an explicit entry; combined with the [Obsolete] attribute carrying a diagnostic id and a URL, removals became a two-major-version process. Fourth, a compatibility test that compiled a sample consumer against version N-2 and ran it against the current build, which is the only way to catch binary breaks mechanically. The "confusing method" removal became a deprecation with a message and a documented replacement, and shipped as a removal 14 months later when telemetry showed zero callers.

// Source-compatible, binary-BREAKING: existing compiled callers referenced Send(Request).
public Task<Response> Send(Request req, CancellationToken ct = default);   // was Send(Request)

// Compatible: keep the old overload, add a new one.
public Task<Response> Send(Request req) => Send(req, CancellationToken.None);
public Task<Response> Send(Request req, CancellationToken ct);

// Extending an interface consumers implement: default interface member (C# 8) so old implementers still load.
public interface IRequestHook
{
    void OnRequest(Request req);
    void OnResponse(Response res) { }            // default body: no break for existing implementers
}

// Removal is a process, not a commit. Analyzer-visible, discoverable, with a replacement.
[Obsolete("Use SendAsync; this overload will be removed in 4.0. https://sdk.example/migrate/send", DiagnosticId = "SDK0007", UrlFormat = "https://sdk.example/diag/{0}")]
public Response SendBlocking(Request req) => Send(req).GetAwaiter().GetResult();

// *REMOVED*Sdk.Client.Send(Sdk.Request req) -> System.Threading.Tasks.Task<Sdk.Response!>!   <- would fail review

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is adding an optional parameter binary-breaking when the caller's source still compiles?

Because optional parameters are a compile-time feature: the C# compiler fills in the default at the call site and emits a call to the full signature. A consumer compiled against Send(Request) has IL referencing exactly that method token; if the new assembly only has Send(Request, CancellationToken), the runtime throws MissingMethodException when it binds. Recompiling fixes it, which is why it is source-compatible, but a plugin or a transitive dependency built earlier will not be recompiled. The safe move is to keep the old overload and forward it.

What if a customer demanded a breaking change now, and they were 30% of revenue?

Then I would look for the design that gives them the behaviour without breaking the other 199: a new API alongside the old, a feature flag in options, or a major version that they adopt first while the old major stays supported. If it genuinely had to be a break, I would ship it as a new major with a migration guide and an analyzer-driven fixer where possible, and keep the previous major in security-fix support for a stated period. What I would not do is break the minor version; the one promise a library can make is that a minor update is safe, and spending that promise for one customer costs every other customer's trust.

At what scale does the deprecation process break down?

When the number of public members deprecated at once exceeds what consumers can absorb in a release, or when the deprecation period spans so many versions that nobody remembers why. Two majors was our ceiling; beyond that the analyzer noise trains consumers to suppress warnings, which defeats the purpose. Usage telemetry is what makes removal safe at scale: a member with zero callers for two quarters can go, and one with a thousand needs a different plan regardless of the calendar.

What did you trade away?

Elegance. The options-object pattern and forwarding overloads accumulate; sealed-by-default disappoints people who wanted to subclass; default interface members can hide the fact that an implementer never handled a case. The SDK's surface is larger and less pretty than a greenfield design, and that is the price of 200 consumers never being broken. I also traded the ability to fix a naming mistake quickly; a bad name lives for two majors.

How did you get product management to accept a 14-month removal?

By reframing "confusing method" as "customers pick the wrong one", which the deprecation warning fixed in the next minor: the IDE steered every new caller to the right method immediately, and the confusion complaints stopped within a release. Removal then became a cleanup with no urgency. Solve the actual problem behind the request and the request itself often stops mattering.

What is the modern tooling for this on .NET 10?

Package validation in the pack step is the one I would add first; it fails the build on a binary break with no test to write.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely in an interview. NuGet resolves a dependency graph by picking, for each package, the lowest version that satisfies every constraint, with "nearest wins" when the graph disagrees (a direct reference beats a transitive one). A package compiled against version 8 of a dependency and loaded with version 10 works only if version 10 is binary-compatible; that is usually true for Microsoft's BCL packages within a major and false across majors, and true of internal packages only if their authors were careful (story 13). The build cannot know what will be loaded at runtime because each service's graph is different. On top of that, .NET 8's SDK introduced NuGet audit, which warns on known vulnerabilities in direct and transitive dependencies, so the version decisions now had a security dimension the teams could not ignore.

The solution was a shared Directory.Packages.props, that is Central Package Management, in a small repository that every service and package repository consumed as a versioned git submodule, later as a NuGet package of its own with <Import>. Every third-party and internal package version was declared once, with CentralPackageTransitivePinningEnabled so transitive dependencies were pinned to the central versions too, which removed the "nearest wins" surprise entirely. A weekly pipeline bumped Microsoft packages to the latest servicing release (this month, the 10.0.12 servicing update of 8 September 2026 patched eight CVEs, so the pipeline matters), ran every service's tests against the proposed versions, and opened one pull request per repository with a green build already attached. Internal packages moved to a compatibility contract: a package validation step on pack that failed on binary breaks, and a rule that a breaking change ships as a new major with both majors supported for one quarter. Governance was a 30-minute fortnightly meeting with one person per team, which sounds bureaucratic and replaced roughly four days a month of incident time.

    <CentralPackageTransitivePinningEnabled>true</CentralPackageTransitivePinningEnabled>
    <NuGetAuditMode>all</NuGetAuditMode>          <!-- direct and transitive vulnerabilities -->
    <NuGetAuditLevel>moderate</NuGetAuditLevel>
    <WarningsAsErrors>$(WarningsAsErrors);NU1901;NU1902;NU1903;NU1904</WarningsAsErrors>
  </PropertyGroup>
  <ItemGroup>
    <PackageVersion Include="System.Text.Json" Version="10.0.12" />
    <PackageVersion Include="Company.Contracts" Version="3.4.1" />
    <PackageVersion Include="Company.Messaging" Version="5.0.2" />

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does NuGet's resolution let a build pass and the runtime fail?

Because the compiler checks each project against the exact assemblies restored for that project, while at runtime a single version of each assembly is loaded for the whole process, chosen by the application's resolved graph and recorded in its deps.json. A library compiled against version 8 is loaded with whatever the app resolved, say version 10, and the CLR binds members by signature at first call. If the signature moved or vanished, that is a MissingMethodException at runtime, not a build error, because the library's own build was correct against version 8. Transitive pinning makes every project restore the same versions, so the build and the runtime agree.

What if a team refused to adopt central package management?

I would first find out what it costs them, because usually it is a legitimate constraint (a vendor SDK pinned to an old major, a service in maintenance mode). For a constraint, the answer is the documented VersionOverride with an owner, or a separate central file for legacy services. For a preference, the security angle does the persuading: audit warnings as errors mean their build fails on a known CVE until they take the central patch, and the weekly pipeline does the work for them. If it is still a no, the escalation is about the fleet-wide patch SLA, not about the tool.

At what scale does a single central props file break down?

When services legitimately need different majors of the same package at once (a slow migration of a large dependency), when the fleet spans multiple .NET target frameworks with different BCL package versions, or when a single file becomes a merge hotspot for a hundred repositories. The fixes are conditional groups per target framework, a small number of named "rings" (current, previous, legacy) each with its own props, and a bot that owns the file so humans rarely edit it directly. The principle (one declared version per ring, transitive pinning, automated bumps) survives.

What did you trade away?

Team autonomy over versions and the speed of a one-repo experiment: trying a new package version now means a change to the central file or a documented override. Some teams felt slowed down for the first month. We recovered most of it by making the override cheap and visible, and by having the bot open the bump PR with the tests already green so adopting a new version was one approval rather than a chore.

How did you get four teams to agree without a mandate?

By quantifying the incident time per team (each thought the others caused it; the numbers showed everyone paid), by building the pipeline before asking for adoption so the first pull request arrived pre-green, and by giving each team the veto in the fortnightly meeting. The first team to adopt was the one with the most recent painful incident. The fourth adopted when the security audit asked how long a CVE took to patch and only they could not answer.

The technical reality is that Native AOT is a different runtime contract, not a compiler switch. The BCL and ASP.NET Core have been annotated for this since .NET 7 and 8, and by .NET 10 minimal APIs, gRPC, System.Text.Json with source generation, configuration binding with the source generator, and much of the Aspire and OpenTelemetry stack are AOT-clean; that is why the pilot services converted in a week each. Each of those was solvable in isolation. Together they were a rewrite of about 30% of the shared code, and the trimming warnings count across the fleet came out at 2,300 after the annotations we could do quickly.

16A third off the compute bill via the GC

Principal Cost-optimisation win Server vs Workstation GC · DATAS · container limits

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Finance asked engineering for a 20% reduction in cloud spend without a headcount conversation, and the easy answers (reserved instances, rightsizing the obvious) had been taken. I took the .NET fleet as my piece: 60 services, about 900 pods, with memory requests that had been set by copying the previous service's manifest. The claim I made, and had to substantiate, was that most of those pods were sized for the garbage collector's habits rather than the application's needs, and that we could cut a third of the memory footprint with no code changes and no latency regression.

The runtime facts that made this true are the ones to teach. .NET has two GC flavours: Workstation, which uses one heap and is tuned for responsiveness on a desktop, and Server, which creates one heap per logical core with a dedicated GC thread each and is tuned for throughput; ASP.NET Core defaults to Server GC. Server GC's cost is committed memory: each heap reserves its own gen0 budget, and on a 16-core node with a pod limited to 4 cores the runtime still sees the host's core count unless the container's CPU limit is set, so a pod could be running 16 heaps for a 4-core workload. Before .NET 9, Server GC also kept a large gen0 budget per heap that grew with allocation rate and shrank slowly, so a bursty service held memory it had needed an hour ago. .NET 9 made DATAS (Dynamic Adaptation To Application Sizes) the default for Server GC: it starts with one heap and adds or removes heaps based on the measured throughput, and it adapts the gen0 budget continuously to the live data size, which for most services means committed memory tracks actual need. The runtime is also container-aware: with a memory limit set, the GC hard limit defaults to 75% of it, and with a CPU limit set, Server GC's heap count follows the limit rather than the host.

The programme was measurement first. We exported dotnet-counters GC metrics (heap size, committed bytes, gen0/1/2 counts, pause time percentage) through OpenTelemetry for two weeks to get a baseline per service under real load. Three findings: 38 services had memory requests over three times their p99 committed heap; 11 services on .NET 8 still, without DATAS, showed the sawtooth of a large gen0 budget; and 7 latency-sensitive services had pause-time percentages that would not tolerate fewer heaps. The changes, per service and reviewed by the owning team: move the .NET 8 stragglers to .NET 10 (DATAS by default); set CPU and memory limits explicitly so the runtime sized itself to the pod; set memory requests from the measured p99 plus 30% instead of the inherited number; for the 7 latency-sensitive services, leave Server GC with DATAS but set GCHeapHardLimitPercent conservatively and keep the heap count, and verify pause time did not move; and for a dozen tiny sidecars and workers, switch to Workstation GC (<ServerGarbageCollection>false</ServerGarbageCollection>) because a background worker processing a queue does not need a heap per core.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does Server GC use so much more memory than Workstation, and when is it worth it?

One heap per core, each with its own gen0 budget and GC thread, so allocation is contention-free and collections run in parallel across all cores; that is a throughput design, and for a busy web service handling thousands of requests a second it is worth every megabyte. For a worker that processes one message at a time, or a sidecar, the extra heaps are idle reservations. DATAS in .NET 9+ narrows the gap by starting with one heap and scaling by measured throughput, which is why the .NET 8 to .NET 10 move was itself a cost lever.

What if a service's latency had regressed after the change?

Then the change is reverted for that service first and understood second; a cost programme that costs latency is a net loss. The likely mechanisms are fewer heaps meaning more contention at allocation, or a tighter hard limit meaning more frequent gen2 collections as the heap approaches it. The diagnostics are the pause-time percentage and gen2 count from the same counters; the remedy is a larger limit for that service or pinning the heap count with GCHeapCount, and accepting that the seven sensitive services are sized for latency and documented as such.

At what scale does this stop being a runtime problem and become a platform one?

Immediately, in a sense: the saving came from setting limits and requests correctly, which is a scheduling and governance question. Beyond a few dozen services, humans cannot keep sizes current, so a vertical pod autoscaler in recommendation mode fed by the GC counters, plus a policy that new services inherit measured sizing after a week, is what keeps the saving. I also had to argue with a platform team that had banned CPU limits for throttling reasons; the resolution was a CPU limit high enough not to throttle but present, so the runtime sized its heaps.

What did you trade away?

Headroom. Tighter requests mean a burst that would once have been absorbed by an oversized pod now triggers scaling or, in two cases, an OOM. We priced that in with the 30% margin over p99 and with horizontal autoscaling on memory pressure for the bursty services. I also traded a quarter of my own time and some political capital with the platform team over the CPU-limit policy.

How did you get sixty service owners to accept changes to their manifests?

Each change came as a pull request with the service's own two-week graphs attached, the proposed numbers, and a one-line rollback. Owners approved or asked questions; nobody was bypassed. The finance target gave the programme a mandate, but the per-service evidence is what made the approvals fast: most took under ten minutes because the graph made the oversizing obvious.

18The 3 a.m. regex

Mid On-call firefighting catastrophic backtracking · NonBacktracking · GeneratedRegex

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The page came at 03:10: the import service was at 100% CPU on every pod, throughput had gone to zero, and the queue behind it was growing. I was the on-call engineer, mid-level, and the runbook said "restart the pods". I restarted one, it went to 100% CPU within a minute of picking up the queue, and that told me the restart was not the fix and the problem was in the messages. The tempting next move was to restart everything and hope; the disciplined one was to capture evidence from a stuck pod before it was recycled, so I ran dotnet-stack report against it and then, because the stack showed something I wanted to be sure of, dotnet-dump collect for the morning.

Every worker thread was inside System.Text.RegularExpressions.RegexInterpreter under a method called ValidateAddressLine. The regex was ^(\w+\s?)*$, a validation someone had written years ago to check that a line was "words separated by single spaces". That pattern is the textbook case of catastrophic backtracking: a nested quantifier where the inner group can match the same characters in exponentially many ways, so on an input that almost matches but fails at the end (a line of 30 words followed by a comma), the backtracking engine tries every partition before giving up. Thirty words is on the order of a billion paths. A customer's import file that night had an address column with trailing commas, and each row parked a thread for minutes. The fix at 03:40 was a feature flag that skipped that validation, which drained the queue; by 04:00 the pods were idle and I went back to bed with a note for the morning.

The durable fix was a lesson in the modern regex toolbox. .NET 7 added RegexOptions.NonBacktracking, which uses a derivative-based automaton with linear-time matching guaranteed at the cost of no lookarounds, backreferences or atomic groups, and it would have made that pattern harmless. Independently, every regex now gets a matchTimeout, so a pathological input throws RegexMatchTimeoutException after 100 ms instead of parking a thread. And the pattern moved to the [GeneratedRegex] source generator (.NET 7+), which emits the matcher as C# at compile time, removes the construction cost and the interpreter, and lets you read the generated code to see what the pattern actually does; in the generated source the nested quantifier was visible as the loop-within-a-loop it was. The pattern itself was rewritten as ^\w+(?: \w+)*$, which has no ambiguity, and a test with a 10,000-character near-miss input runs it in CI under a 50 ms budget.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does (\w+\s?)* go exponential, and why only on a near-miss?

Because the group can match a run of word characters in many ways: "abc" can be one iteration matching "abc", or "ab" then "c", or "a" then "bc", and so on, and the optional \s? makes adjacent iterations ambiguous. On a successful match the engine takes the first path and stops. On an input that fails at the end, the backtracking engine must prove no path works, so it enumerates them all, and the count grows exponentially with input length. That is the general rule: backtracking cost is about ambiguity plus failure, which is why pathological cases appear only on bad input, in production, at night.

What if you could not change the pattern, say it came from customer configuration?

Then the engine and the timeout are the controls: compile customer patterns with RegexOptions.NonBacktracking where the pattern's features allow it (it rejects lookarounds and backreferences at construction, which is itself a useful validation), always with a matchTimeout, and run the match on a bounded worker with a queue rather than on request threads. A pattern that fails the NonBacktracking construction check gets the timeout plus a warning to the customer. Untrusted regex is an input-validation problem, and the .NET engine gives you the tools to bound it.

At what scale does the mitigation (a feature flag to skip validation) become dangerous?

Immediately if the validation was protecting something downstream: skipping address validation meant rows with bad addresses reached the database. I checked that the downstream had its own constraints (it did: a length limit and a not-null check) before flipping the flag, and the morning follow-up included a query for rows imported during the window. A mitigation that trades correctness for availability needs the correctness debt written down before you go back to bed.

What did you trade away with NonBacktracking?

Lookarounds, backreferences, atomic groups and RegexOptions.RightToLeft, which the automaton cannot express, plus a higher construction cost and a larger memory footprint per pattern. For validation patterns none of that matters; for the two places we used a backreference, the answer was to keep the backtracking engine with a timeout and a pathological-input test. Choosing the engine per pattern is the modern answer, not a global switch.

How did you handle the handover at 04:00?

The morning engineer thanked me for the dump more than for the fix. A handover that lets someone else continue without re-deriving the night is the on-call skill interviewers are listening for.

19Killing the flaky test suite

Senior CI / test / reliability improvement TimeProvider · test isolation · Testcontainers · Microsoft.Testing.Platform

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The main pipeline for a 6,000-test C# solution failed about one run in five for reasons unrelated to the change, and the team had adapted: "re-run" was the most common comment on pull requests, a re-run cost 25 minutes, and people had started merging on a red build with a note. I took it on because a flaky suite is not a test problem, it is a trust problem: once nobody believes red, the suite catches nothing. The first thing I did was measure, with a script that pulled a month of CI results and classified every failure by test name; 61 tests accounted for 90% of the flakes.

The 61 tests sorted into five mechanisms, which is the technical part of the story. Time: tests that used DateTime.Now or DateTime.UtcNow and asserted on durations or on "today", failing around midnight or when the CI agent was slow; the fix was the TimeProvider abstraction in .NET 8's BCL, injected in production and replaced with FakeTimeProvider from Microsoft.Extensions.TimeProvider.Testing in tests, so time is a value the test controls. Shared state: a static cache and a singleton with a mutable field, fine when tests ran serially and racy once xUnit's parallel collections kicked in; the fix was scoping the state per test through the DI container and, for the two cases where a static was unavoidable, an explicit [Collection] that serialises just those tests rather than turning parallelism off globally. Async misuse: async void test helpers whose exceptions landed on the wrong test, and a Task.Delay(100) used as a synchronisation mechanism, replaced with awaiting the actual signal (a TaskCompletionSource or the FakeTimeProvider's Advance). Real infrastructure: tests that hit a shared development database, so two pipelines running at once corrupted each other's data; the fix was Testcontainers, a fresh PostgreSQL container per test class, with EF Core migrations applied on start, which made the tests slower individually and reliable collectively. Order dependence: tests that passed only after another test had seeded something; the fix was running the suite in random order locally until they surfaced, then seeding explicitly.

// Time as a dependency (.NET 8+): production takes TimeProvider, tests control it.
public sealed class TokenCache(TimeProvider clock)
{
    public bool IsExpired(Token t) => clock.GetUtcNow() >= t.ExpiresAt;
}

[Fact]
public void Token_expires_exactly_at_the_boundary()

The policy change mattered as much as the fixes: a quarantine. That sounds harsh, and it is the only thing that kept the suite honest: a test nobody will fix in two weeks is a test nobody trusts. The suite also moved to the Microsoft.Testing.Platform runner with xUnit v3, which cut the runner overhead and gave reliable per-test timeouts. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is Task.Delay in a test a bug even when it passes?

Because it encodes an assumption about how fast something else runs, and that assumption is false on a loaded CI agent. The test is asserting on a race it hopes to win. The correct shape awaits the actual condition: a TaskCompletionSource the code under test completes, an event, or a FakeTimeProvider whose Advance triggers the timer deterministically. Removing sleeps also made the suite faster, because every sleep was a fixed cost paid on every run whether needed or not.

What if a test needed real infrastructure that cannot be containerised, say a cloud service?

Then it is not a unit or integration test; it is a contract or smoke test and should run in its own stage with its own reliability expectations, against a dedicated environment per pipeline (or a namespace-per-run convention), and it should not gate merges to main on the same terms. Most services have an emulator or a local mode (storage emulators, a local queue); where they genuinely do not, a recorded-response fake for the gating suite plus a nightly real-service run is the shape I use.

At what scale does the quarantine policy break down?

When more tests enter quarantine than leave it, which means the root causes are still being written into new tests. That is the signal to fix the environment: test templates that inject TimeProvider by default, a base fixture for infrastructure, and an analyzer rule for DateTime.Now and Thread.Sleep in test projects. Quarantine handles the backlog; the templates stop the inflow.

How did you get people to stop merging on red?

By making red mean something again. This one is set this month. .NET 11 RC1 shipped on 8 September 2026 with a go-live licence, and with it the C# 15 preview: union types, closed hierarchies, extension indexers, labelled break/continue, and the first step of a redesigned memory-safety model. A VP of engineering who reads the .NET blog asked, reasonably, why our shared domain library was not already using union types for its outcome hierarchies, given that "RC1 is go-live" and a competitor's engineering blog had a post about it. He wanted the platform team to adopt C# 15 across the shared libraries before the November GA, as a recruiting and modernity signal. My job as the principal engineer for the platform was to say no to part of it, yes to part of it, and keep his goal intact.

The precision matters, because "RC is go-live" is true and incomplete. The go-live licence covers the .NET 11 runtime and SDK at RC quality: you can run production workloads on RC1 and get support. It does not make preview language features stable: C# 15 features in RC1 are compiled only with <LangVersion>preview</LangVersion>, the compiler stamps the assembly as using preview features, and the language team's own note says some parts of the union proposal are not yet implemented and will land in later previews, which means the shape can change between RC1 and GA. The runtime's IUnion and UnionAttribute support only arrived in preview 5. A shared library compiled with LangVersion preview forces every consumer onto the same preview compiler and, if a union's emitted shape changes, breaks them at GA. There is a second, quieter risk: the memory-safety preview changes what unsafe means, so code that compiled under the old rules may need edits, and that is exactly the kind of change you want to take deliberately, not as a side effect of chasing a feature.

My response was a one-page decision with three parts. No: the shared libraries stay on C# 14 with LangVersion pinned explicitly (not the SDK default, so a machine with the .NET 11 SDK cannot silently move us), until C# 15 is GA in November. Yes: one internal, leaf application with no consumers moves to .NET 11 RC1 now, under the go-live licence, so we learn the runtime changes, measure the startup and GC improvements and find the breaking changes before GA; that team was enthusiastic and it gave the VP a blog-worthy pilot. Yes, with a date: the outcome hierarchies in the domain library are refactored now to the closed-shaped design on C# 14 (private constructors, sealed nested records, exhaustive switch with an analyzer, exactly the story 06 pattern), so that adopting closed and union at GA is a mechanical change with no consumer impact. And a principle for the record: language previews are for experiments in code with no consumers; runtime RCs with go-live are for pilots with an owner; shared libraries move at GA. He agreed, and the internal blog post about the RC1 pilot went out two weeks later with his name on it, which was the goal he had actually had.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is a preview language feature riskier in a shared library than in an application?

Because the library's compiled output is consumed by others. The compiler marks assemblies built with preview features, consumers must also compile with preview enabled, and if the feature's emitted shape changes at GA (a union's runtime representation, say) every consumer compiled against the RC shape breaks with a runtime binding error, not a build error. What if the VP had overruled you?

Then I would have executed with the risk contained: the union types confined to one shared library with a strict consumer list, all consumers pinned to the same SDK version via global.json, a compatibility test that rebuilds each consumer on every compiler update, and a written note that GA may require a coordinated rebuild. And I would have asked for the decision and its rationale to be recorded, because when the rebuild came the team should not be the ones explaining it. Overruled is fine; ambiguous is not.

At what scale does the "shared libraries move at GA" policy break?

When GA itself ships breaking behaviour and the fleet has no experience with it, which is the argument for the pilot: one team on the RC means the platform knows the migration issues before GA, and the November move for the shared libraries is informed rather than blind. Choose one visible in the stories and already being worked on: fixing things yourself before teaching (19, 10), first answers that are correct but not useful (20, 11), letting a safe phase run too long (04). Give the evidence that you have changed, not a promise that you will.

Where do you want to be in three years, technically?

Tie it to the altitude you are interviewing at: at Staff, owning the technical direction of a platform area; at Principal, setting the standards several teams build on (14, 16, 20 are the shape). Keep it specific to the topic and honest about what you would give up to get there.

22Night-before cheat sheet

Ten sentences to say out loud before you sleep. Every one is speakable as-is.

  1. Open with the situation in one line, with a number in it: "a C# service whose p99 went from 40 ms to 8 s every weekday at nine", then stop and let them ask.
  2. Name the mechanism precisely and once: starvation not deadlock, reachability not "the GC missed it", backtracking not "the regex was slow". It is the cheapest way to prove depth.
  3. Separate mitigation from fix from guard rail, in that order; the interviewer hears three decisions instead of one.
  4. Quantify the result in the unit the business used: minutes of outage, pods not bought, nodes removed, re-runs that stopped, NREs per week.
  5. Say "I" for the decision and the mistake, "we" for the work; the moment you take solo credit for a war room, the rest of the story is discounted.
  6. Own the "what I'd do differently" as a specific practice you now follow, not a regret: "alert on slope, not threshold", "kill criterion before staffing".
  7. Tie every technical choice to a real C# trade-off you can defend under drill-down: async frees threads but moves the queue, records give value equality and take away identity, Server GC buys throughput with memory, spans remove allocations and readability.
  8. Know the version facts cold: C# 14 on .NET 10 LTS since November 2025, supported to November 2028; .NET 10.0.12 servicing on 8 September 2026; .NET 11 RC1 with the C# 15 preview the same day, go-live for the runtime, not the language.
  9. When a drill-down reaches the edge of what you did, say so and reason forward: "we did not hit that scale; at that point I would expect the bottleneck to move to connections, and I'd add a limiter at the callee".
  10. Pick the altitude before you start: the same story is "what I did" at Mid, "why and what it cost" at Senior, "how the team and adjacent systems moved" at Staff, and "the standard that now exists" at Principal; land on the one the role is for.
