01The reconciliation job that took six hours to find eleven cents

SeniorPerformance problem diagnosed

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own The nightly reconciliation job matched settlement-report line items against ledger entries by pulling every unreconciled ledger row into memory and scanning it for a same-amount, same-day match — an O(n·m) sweep that was fine at a few hundred rows and took six hours once volume grew past a few hundred thousand. Worse, it wasn't even reliably correct: "same amount, same day" is a heuristic, not a key. The actual key was sitting in both records the whole time — the PSP's charge ID — but nobody had indexed on it because the ledger's `Transactions` table stored the PSP reference as a nullable, unindexed free-text column left over from an early integration. Re-keying the join on that ID turned the sweep into a single indexed hash join, and the run dropped from six hours to under four minutes. The last eleven cents of unreconciled balance turned out to be a rounding-mode mismatch: the ledger converted minor units with MidpointRounding.ToEven (banker's rounding) while the PSP's settlement report used round-half-up, so a handful of multi-currency conversions landed a cent apart and accumulated to eleven cents across a month of FX settlements.

    foreach (var line in settlementLines)
        if (line.Amount == entry.Amount && line.Date == entry.Date && !line.Matched)
        { line.Matched = true; entry.Reconciled = true; break; }

// After: indexed join on the PSP's own charge id

    if (byChargeId.TryGetValue(entry.PspChargeId, out var line))

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you reach for the domain's actual identity (a PSP-issued reference) instead of a heuristic when reconciling two independent systems, and whether "it's slow" and "it's wrong" register as two separate bugs you'd diagnose separately rather than one you'd patch with a timeout.

(b) STAR scaffold

Situation
Nightly reconciliation between PSP settlement data and the internal ledger had grown from minutes to six hours, and a small unreconciled balance had persisted for weeks.
Task
Bring the job back under budget and close out the unreconciled balance before month-end close.
Action
Profiled the job to find the nested comparison, re-keyed the match on the PSP charge ID with a proper index, then bisected the remaining discrepancy down to a rounding-mode mismatch in currency conversion.
Result
Run time dropped from six hours to under four minutes; the ledger balanced to the cent, and the rounding fix was documented so the next multi-currency integration wouldn't repeat it.

(c) Recall prompts

What made the original match "correct most of the time" instead of always correct?

It kept up because collisions were rare at low volume, not because the key was actually unique — a classic case of a heuristic passing tests until scale exposes it.

Why index on the PSP's ID rather than a computed hash of amount+date+currency?

The PSP already guarantees that ID is unique and stable; inventing your own composite key just recreates the ambiguity with extra steps.

How would you have found the eleven cents without already suspecting rounding?

Bucket the unreconciled deltas by currency and by transaction type before looking at individual rows — a rounding bug clusters by currency pair, a matching bug doesn't.

What's your own equivalent — a report, a batch job, or a reconciliation that got slow or subtly wrong as data grew?

Look for anywhere you joined two systems on something other than a real shared identifier.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the original design match on amount and date instead of the charge ID?

The charge ID column existed but was an afterthought from an earlier integration — nobody had gone back to make it the join key once it was available.

What if the PSP's settlement report didn't include your internal reference at all?

Then the fix is upstream: store the PSP's ID at charge-creation time so reconciliation always has a real key to join on, rather than trying to infer one after the fact.

At what row count does the nested scan actually start hurting?

Roughly once n·m comparisons stop fitting in the batch window — for us that was a few hundred thousand rows against an hourly SLA, but the honest answer is "profile it," not a fixed number.

What did you trade away by re-indexing on the PSP ID?

A small migration cost to backfill the ID on old rows, and a new invariant to enforce going forward: every charge write must persist the PSP reference, or reconciliation degrades again.

Would you build the reconciliation job the same way today?

No — today it's a set-based SQL join with the charge ID as a proper indexed foreign key from day one, plus an automated daily assertion that the unreconciled delta is zero, so a regression pages someone instead of sitting for weeks.

02Buying our way out of PCI scope with Checkout

StaffHard architectural trade-off

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own The team's own card form posted PANs through our servers before tokenizing, which put every server that touched the request in PCI DSS scope under SAQ D — the long, expensive self-assessment with the widest control set. The trade-off was between three ways of shrinking that scope: keep a custom form but swap in the PSP's hosted iframe fields (SAQ A-EP: the DOM is ours, the field is theirs, so we're still in scope for the page's integrity but never touch a PAN), redirect entirely to the PSP's hosted checkout page (SAQ A: our servers never render or touch cardholder data at all), or keep the fully custom form and accept SAQ D. We picked the hosted redirect for the primary checkout flow and reserved a hosted-fields (SAQ A-EP) fallback for one embedded widget where a full redirect broke the UX too badly. The real cost wasn't PCI paperwork — it was giving up control over checkout page styling and losing the ability to add client-side fraud signals inline with card entry, which we partly clawed back with the PSP's JS SDK events.

(b) STAR scaffold

Situation
A custom card form kept every backend service that handled checkout inside PCI DSS SAQ D scope, and the annual assessment was consuming weeks of engineering time.
Task
Reduce PCI scope without breaking conversion on the primary checkout flow.
Action
Moved the primary flow to a PSP-hosted redirect (SAQ A), kept one embedded widget on hosted fields (SAQ A-EP) where a redirect wasn't acceptable, and re-added inline fraud signals through the PSP's client-side SDK events instead of raw field access.
Result
Scope dropped from SAQ D to a mix of SAQ A and A-EP across the two flows, the next assessment took days instead of weeks, and conversion on the redirect flow held steady after two rounds of styling iteration.

(c) Recall prompts

What's the actual difference in scope between SAQ A and SAQ A-EP?

SAQ A means cardholder data never touches anything you control, even the page's DOM structure around the card fields; SAQ A-EP applies when your JavaScript still constructs the page the PSP's iframe sits inside, so a script-injection risk on your page is still in scope.

Why not just tokenize client-side with your own form and skip hosted fields entirely?

A same-origin form that reads the PAN before tokenizing still exposes the PAN to your JavaScript execution context, which is exactly what SAQ D is scoped around — tokenizing after the fact doesn't undo that exposure.

What did the business give up for the scope reduction?

Pixel-level control of the card entry UI and the ability to run custom validation logic directly against field values before submission.

What's your own equivalent trade-off between a compliance/security constraint and a UX or feature goal?

Look for a place you gave up control to a third party specifically to shrink an audit or compliance surface.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not put every flow on SAQ A and skip A-EP entirely?

One embedded widget lived inside a partner's iframe where a full-page redirect broke the surrounding flow, so hosted fields were the least-bad option that still avoided touching a PAN.

What if the PSP's hosted checkout page didn't support your currency or locale mix?

Then SAQ A stops being available for that flow and you're negotiating between A-EP and D again — scope reduction is bounded by what your PSP's hosted surface actually covers.

At what point does the assessment cost justify the UX cost, and at what point does it not?

Once SAQ D's control set (quarterly scans, segmentation validation, much larger evidence collection) starts costing more engineering time than the UX compromise costs in conversion — for us that was after one full SAQ D cycle.

What did you trade away by moving to the PSP's hosted surface?

Control over checkout page branding and the ability to A/B test the payment step's layout independently of the PSP's release cycle.

How does this decision age as the product grows a second, third PSP?

Each PSP's hosted fields and redirect pages have their own styling limits and event hooks, so "one hosted checkout" becomes "an abstraction over several hosted checkouts" — see story 17 for where that abstraction started leaking.

03The webhook that fired twice, and the charge that rode along with it

SeniorProduction incident / outage

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own The order-fulfillment service treated a payment_intent.succeeded webhook as "create the order," with no check for whether that order already existed. PSPs redeliver webhooks — on a slow handler response, a transient 5xx, or just at-least-once delivery guarantees — so the same event arrived twice a few seconds apart during a traffic spike when the handler's response time crept past the PSP's retry timeout. Two orders were created for one payment. The fix had two layers: verify the webhook signature first (so a duplicate can't be forged, only redelivered), then make the handler idempotent by keying on the event's own ID — Stripe-Signature verification confirms authenticity, but the event id field is what you dedupe on, stored with a unique constraint so a second insert fails fast instead of creating a second order. We also learned the event delivery order isn't guaranteed, so the handler couldn't assume succeeded always arrives after created — it had to be safe to process out of order too.

[HttpPost("webhooks/stripe")]
public async Task<IActionResult> Handle()
{
    var json = await new StreamReader(Request.Body).ReadToEndAsync();
    var stripeEvent = EventUtility.ConstructEvent(json,
        Request.Headers["Stripe-Signature"], _webhookSecret);

    // Dedupe on the event id before doing anything with side effects.
    var inserted = await _db.ProcessedEvents.TryInsertAsync(stripeEvent.Id);
    if (!inserted) return Ok(); // already handled this exact event

    if (stripeEvent.Type == "payment_intent.succeeded")
        await _orders.MarkPaidIdempotentAsync(stripeEvent);

    return Ok();
}

What the interviewer is really probing: whether "webhooks are at-least-once, not exactly-once" is a fact you designed around up front, or one you learned from an incident — and whether your idempotency key is the event ID (correct) versus something derived from the payload (fragile).

(b) STAR scaffold

Situation
A traffic spike slowed webhook handler response times past the PSP's retry window, causing a duplicate delivery of a payment_intent.succeeded event and a duplicate order.
Task
Stop the duplicate orders immediately and make the handler safe against redelivery permanently.
Action
Added a unique-constrained table of processed event IDs checked before any side effect, moved slow post-processing off the request path onto a queue, and confirmed signature verification was already correctly rejecting forged events.
Result
No further duplicate orders across two subsequent high-traffic periods, and the handler's own response time dropped enough that redelivery became rare rather than just harmless.

(c) Recall prompts

Why key deduplication on the event ID rather than the payment intent ID?

A single payment intent can legitimately generate several distinct events over its lifecycle (requires_action, succeeded, and so on); deduping on the intent ID would silently drop real, different events.

Why verify the signature before checking for duplicates?

An unverified request could be a replay or forgery designed to probe your dedupe logic itself — authenticity has to be established before the event is trusted enough to reason about at all.

What made the handler slow enough to trigger a redelivery in the first place?

Order creation, inventory reservation, and a confirmation email were all synchronous in the request path — any one of them being slow under load pushed the whole handler past the PSP's timeout.

What's your own equivalent — an at-least-once delivery guarantee that surprised you the first time it actually redelivered?

Look for any integration point where you assumed "the caller only calls this once" without the system actually promising that.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the handler do all its work synchronously instead of enqueuing?

It was written early, before webhook volume mattered, and nobody revisited it once traffic grew — a common way idempotency bugs get introduced: correct-enough at the volume it was written for.

What if two duplicate events arrived within milliseconds of each other, racing the unique-constraint check?

The unique constraint at the database level, not an application-level "check then insert," is what actually closes that race — a check-then-act pattern in application code has the exact same race the bug started as.

At what request volume does synchronous webhook processing stop being safe?

Whenever handler latency has any chance of approaching the PSP's redelivery timeout under load — that's a moving target, which is why the real fix was making redelivery harmless rather than making the handler merely fast enough for today.

What did you trade away by moving processing onto a queue?

Immediate consistency — the webhook now returns 200 before the order is fully created, so any code that assumed "webhook acknowledged means order exists" needed to be found and fixed too.

Would this design hold up against out-of-order delivery, not just duplicate delivery?

Yes, because side effects are keyed off event ID and applied idempotently per event, not assumed to arrive in a particular sequence — see story 05 for the disagreement that shaped this into a queue-based design from the start.

04Migrating off 3-D Secure 1.0 before a deadline that kept moving

SeniorMigration / version upgrade

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own The checkout flow was still triggering 3-D Secure 1.0 for a slice of card-issuing banks, using an iframe-based redirect that had no App-based flow, weaker device data, and a noticeably worse mobile UX than 2.x. Card networks kept announcing and then pushing back their 3DS1 decommission dates, which made "migrate before the deadline" a moving target rather than a fixed one. Migrating to EMV 3DS 2.x meant switching from the PSP's older `handleCardAction`-style redirect helper to its newer frictionless-first flow: the PSP now sends 150+ device and browser data points to the issuer up front, and roughly two-thirds of authentications came back frictionless — no challenge screen at all — with the rest falling back to an in-app or browser challenge instead of a bank-hosted redirect page. The actual migration risk wasn't the API change, it was that we couldn't test issuer-side frictionless rates in advance; we shipped behind a feature flag, ramped by issuer country, and watched authorization rates before cutting over the remaining traffic.

(b) STAR scaffold

Situation
A remaining slice of checkout traffic still used 3-D Secure 1.0, and the card networks' decommission date for 3DS1 rails had already slipped once.
Task
Complete the migration to EMV 3DS 2.x without depending on the mandate date holding, and without regressing authorization rates.
Action
Rebuilt the authentication flow on the PSP's 2.x SDK, shipped it behind a feature flag ramped by issuer country, and monitored frictionless rate and authorization rate at each ramp step before proceeding.

Full migration completed two months ahead of the (again-revised) deadline, with a measured frictionless rate high enough that overall checkout friction net decreased despite adding SCA.

(c) Recall prompts

Why ramp by issuer country instead of by percentage of traffic?

Frictionless-flow behavior is driven by the issuing bank's own risk engine, which varies sharply by country and card network relationship — a random percentage ramp mixes good and bad cohorts together and hides which segment is actually a problem.

Why not just wait until the mandate date was closer to firm?

3DS1 traffic was already degrading UX and conversion on mobile every day it ran, so the business case for migrating existed independent of the compliance deadline.

What's the practical difference a "frictionless" 3DS2 authentication makes versus a 3DS1 redirect, from the cardholder's side?

Frictionless means no challenge screen at all — authentication happens invisibly using device/behavioral signals, so from the shopper's perspective checkout looks identical to no-3DS at all.

What's your own equivalent — a migration gated by an external deadline that moved on you?

Look for any dependency on a vendor's or regulator's announced timeline, and whether your plan assumed that date was fixed.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not migrate everyone at once given the UX was strictly better?

Frictionless rates and issuer support for the new device-data fields were unknown in advance for many banks, so a full cutover risked authorization-rate regressions we couldn't predict or quickly roll back from.

What if a ramped country segment showed a lower authorization rate than 3DS1 gave it?

That's exactly what the flag and per-segment monitoring were for — hold that segment on 3DS1, investigate the specific issuer behavior, and only advance once the regression was understood.

At what frictionless rate would the migration not have been worth it?

If a meaningful segment came back mostly challenge-flow with a real conversion drop, the answer isn't "abandon 3DS2" — it's usually an SCA exemption request (low-value or TRA) submitted with that transaction, which the PSP's 2.x integration supports and 1.0 never did.

What did you trade away by building the ramp machinery instead of a hard cutover?

Weeks of running two authentication code paths side by side, with the operational cost of monitoring both — worth it because a bad cutover on payments directly costs revenue, not just engineering time.

Would you keep the flag-and-ramp approach for the next PSD2-driven change?

Yes — the pattern (flag by segment, monitor authorization rate, ramp on evidence) generalizes to any change where a regulator's or network's timeline is out of your control but the blast radius of a bad rollout is real money.

05Synchronous webhook handlers vs. a queue: the fight that cost us a week

SeniorTechnical disagreement with a colleague

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own A colleague wanted webhook handlers to stay fully synchronous — verify, process, respond — arguing it was simpler to reason about and easier to debug than adding a queue in between. I wanted the handler to do only verification and dedupe, then drop the event onto a queue for a worker to process, arguing that a PSP's webhook timeout (a handler that doesn't respond quickly gets marked failed and retried) meant any slow downstream step — inventory, email, a partner API call — put every webhook at risk of the exact duplicate-delivery problem from story 03. We each had a real point: synchronous code is easier to trace end-to-end in a debugger, and my design added an operational dependency (the queue and its worker) that could itself fail silently. We resolved it by prototyping both under a simulated slow-downstream-API and measuring actual redelivery rates — synchronous handling produced duplicates within minutes of a 2-second delay injected into the email step; the queued design didn't, because the webhook endpoint's own response time never depended on downstream latency. We kept the queue, but added structured logging and a per-event trace ID so the "harder to debug" objection had a concrete answer instead of just being asserted.

A colleague and I disagreed on whether webhook handlers should process events synchronously or hand off to a queue, with real trade-offs on both sides and no clear precedent in the codebase.

What was genuinely correct in the position you didn't adopt?

Synchronous handling really is easier to step through with a debugger — the eventual design had to earn back that clarity with correlation IDs rather than pretend the concern didn't matter.

Why settle it with a prototype instead of a design doc or a vote?

Neither of us could actually predict redelivery behavior from first principles with confidence — it depended on real timeout thresholds and real downstream latency, which a doc can't settle but a measurement can.

What would have changed your mind back to the synchronous design?

If the prototype had shown the queue's own failure modes (stuck consumers, poison messages) causing more incidents than the redelivery problem it solved.

What's your own equivalent — a technical disagreement you resolved with evidence rather than by outlasting the other person?

Look for a disagreement where both sides had a real point, and the resolution mechanism mattered as much as the outcome.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the disagreement take a week to resolve instead of a day?

Building a realistic prototype — one that actually simulated production-like downstream latency and PSP redelivery timing rather than a toy case — took longer than either of us expected, but a rushed comparison would have been unconvincing to whoever lost it.

What if the prototype had shown both approaches performing about the same?

Then the decision would have defaulted to the simpler-to-operate option — synchronous, since it doesn't add a queue — because a tie on the measured trade-off should be broken by operational simplicity, not by whoever argued harder.

Once handler concurrency limits start throttling incoming webhooks — a queue decouples ingestion rate from processing rate, which synchronous handling can never do regardless of how fast each step is.

What did the losing side of the argument get in return for conceding?

Direct input into the observability design, and confirmation that the concern was taken seriously rather than steamrolled — which mattered more to the working relationship than the technical outcome itself.

Would you run the disagreement the same way today, with story 03's incident already having happened?

Yes, and faster — story 03 is the evidence this discussion needed and didn't have yet; today I'd point to that incident directly instead of needing a synthetic prototype.

06The retry loop that charged a customer four times

MidNasty debugging session

(a) Worked example

EXAMPLE — replace with your own A customer reported four identical charges for one order. The checkout code called the PSP's create-payment-intent endpoint inside a retry loop that caught HttpRequestException and simply tried again on any failure — including timeouts, where the first request had actually succeeded on the PSP's side but the response never made it back before the client's own timeout fired. Each retry generated a brand-new payment intent because no Idempotency-Key header was set, so the PSP had no way to recognize "this is the same logical request" and dutifully created four separate charges. The fix was to generate one idempotency key per logical checkout attempt (not per HTTP call) and pass it on every retry of that attempt; Stripe's API then recognized the retried request from a previous 24-hour window and returned the original result instead of creating a new charge, and a request retried with the same key but different parameters was rejected outright rather than silently doing something ambiguous.

var idempotencyKey = Guid.NewGuid().ToString(); // one key per logical attempt
var options = new RequestOptions { IdempotencyKey = idempotencyKey };

for (var attempt = 0; attempt < 3; attempt++)
{
    try
    {
        return await _paymentIntentService.CreateAsync(createOptions, options);
    }
    catch (HttpRequestException) when (attempt < 2)
    {
        await Task.Delay(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(Math.Pow(2, attempt)));
        // same idempotencyKey reused here — this is what makes the retry safe
    }
}

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you know that a network timeout doesn't mean "the request didn't happen" — it means "you don't know if it happened" — and whether idempotency keys are scoped to the right unit of work (one per attempt, not one per HTTP call, not omitted).

(b) STAR scaffold

Situation
A customer was charged four times for a single order after a checkout retry loop repeated a payment-intent creation call with no idempotency protection.
Task

Then the honest answer would have been negotiating the deadline itself, the way story 11 negotiated SCA scope — compliance risk isn't a lever available to trade away just because a schedule is tight.

At what point would raw PAN storage ever be an acceptable trade-off?

Essentially never, for a team that isn't itself a PSP — the liability, audit burden, and breach risk so heavily outweigh any convenience gained that this is one of the few payments decisions with almost no legitimate trade-off space at all.

What did the pushback cost in terms of the stakeholder relationship?

Some initial friction in the conversation, resolved once the alternative was on the table — the relationship came out stronger, not weaker, because the stakeholder saw the pushback paired with a real solution rather than an obstacle.

Would you handle this differently if the stakeholder had outranked you more significantly?

No — the explanation and the alternative are the same regardless of hierarchy; PCI scope doesn't become more negotiable because the request came from someone more senior, and that's exactly the point worth making plainly if asked.

22Night-before cheat sheet

Ten sentences to say out loud once, the night before. If one doesn't come out fluently, that's the story to re-read.

  1. Idempotency keys are scoped to one logical attempt, not one HTTP call, and a mismatched retry gets rejected, not silently reused.
  2. Webhooks are at-least-once and not strictly ordered — dedupe on the event ID, never assume delivery sequence.
  3. A decline is the PSP doing its job and telling you no; a failure is the PSP telling you nothing — only the second one is safe to retry.
  4. PCI scope follows where the data has been, not intent or duration — there is no such thing as "temporary" raw-PAN storage.
  5. SAQ A, A-EP, and D trade UX control for shrinking what's in scope, in that order, from most control given up to least.
  6. 3-D Secure 2.x is frictionless-first by design; the challenge flow is the fallback, not the default.
  7. A double-entry ledger should balance by construction — a failed balance assertion means the invariant broke, treat it like a correctness incident, not a data-entry bug.
  8. A dispute deadline is a hard, short, non-negotiable window; a missed one is usually an automatic, permanent loss regardless of the evidence's strength.
  9. An abstraction over two PSPs has to be built on the intersection of their real guarantees, not one PSP's contract with an adapter bolted on for the other.
  10. Retrying a hard decline isn't just wasteful, it can cost real processing fees and, on some issuers, hurt future authorization odds.
