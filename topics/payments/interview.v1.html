Payment systems & integrations

interview · PCI DSS 4.0.1 · released 2024-06-11

Payments interviews at the lead/principal level rarely dwell on "how do I charge a card" — screeners check that once, then the loop moves to whether you can reason about money moving through a system that is asynchronous, partially observable, and unforgiving of double-counting: idempotency under retries, webhook delivery that is at-least-once and out-of-order, SCA/3DS friction traded against conversion, PCI scope you actively engineer away rather than merely audit, and a ledger that has to reconcile to the cent against a PSP you don't control. Work down the sub-area table in frequency order — the first six show up in nearly every payments-adjacent loop regardless of level, the rest is what separates mid from senior from principal. Two currency notes worth having ready: the EU's PSD3/Payment Services Regulation reached provisional political agreement on 27 Nov 2025 (directly-applicable PSR text, mandatory payee-name verification, mandatory fraud reimbursement for impersonation scams) with application expected roughly 18–24 months after entry into force; and EMVCo published the first draft of EMV 3-D Secure 2.4.0.0 for comment in mid-2026 (current production baseline is 2.3.1.1), the version most candidates studying from 2023-era material haven't seen yet. This bank assumes PSP APIs generally (Stripe- and Adyen-shaped, since those are the two a .NET shop most often integrates) rather than one vendor's SDK trivia.

01 PSP integration fundamentals & API design

This is the five-minute filter: whether you know what actually moves money versus what merely calls an API, and whether you've built against a PSP's API shape rather than a tutorial's happy path. Screeners get the vocabulary and the request lifecycle; senior and principal candidates are expected to reason about integration architecture — versioning against a vendor that changes weekly, multi-PSP failover, and where control (and PCI scope) shifts as you move from server-side API calls to hosted UI.

Docs: Stripe Payment Intents · Adyen online payments

What's the actual division of labour between a merchant, a payment gateway, a PSP, an acquirer, and a card network — and where does an issuer fit?screener

Answer. The merchant is you, selling something. A gateway encrypts and forwards transaction data to an acquirer or processor — historically a distinct box, now usually bundled. A PSP (Stripe, Adyen, Braintree) is the merchant-facing aggregator: it gives you one API/contract and, underneath, holds or brokers the acquiring relationship, fraud tooling, and settlement so you don't need a direct bank relationship or a PCI Level 1 audit of your own infrastructure. The acquirer is the bank that actually holds the merchant account and submits transactions into the card network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex) on the merchant's behalf; the issuer is the cardholder's bank, the one that approves or declines and whose funds eventually move. A single "charge" therefore hops: merchant → PSP/gateway → acquirer → network → issuer, and back, in a few hundred milliseconds.

Example

Cardholder -> Merchant (checkout) -> PSP (Stripe/Adyen) -> Acquirer -> Card network (Visa/Mastercard) -> Issuer
                                                                                                     <- approve/decline -

Follow-ups

  • Is Stripe an acquirer? No, not in the classic sense — Stripe operates as a payment facilitator/PSP with acquiring relationships (and in some markets holds acquiring licences directly), abstracting the acquirer entirely from the merchant's view.
  • Why would a large enterprise ever want a direct acquirer relationship instead of a PSP? Lower per-transaction cost at volume and more control over routing, at the price of owning PCI scope, integration complexity, and multiple vendor relationships (gateway, acquirer, fraud tools) directly.
  • What does the network itself charge for? Network/interchange fees for routing and the issuer's risk of extending credit — separate from the PSP's own markup.

Common wrong answer: Treating "Stripe" and "the acquirer" as the same thing, or saying a gateway "processes" the payment. The gateway/PSP is a courier and orchestrator; the actual approve/decline and fund movement is an issuer-and-network decision the PSP only relays — candidates who conflate these can't reason about who's actually responsible when a call times out mid-flight.

Direct server-side API calls, a hosted checkout page, and a client-side SDK (Stripe Elements / Adyen Components) — what actually changes across these three integration styles?screener

Answer. Direct server-to-server API calls mean raw card data (or at least the sensitive fields) can transit your own servers, which is why virtually nobody does this today without a very good reason — it maximizes PCI DSS scope (SAQ D) and gives you full control over UX. A hosted/redirect checkout page sends the customer entirely off your domain to the PSP's own page; you get minimal PCI scope (SAQ A) but no control over styling or flow. Client-side SDKs (Stripe.js/Elements, Adyen Web Components) render the card fields inside iframes the PSP controls, tokenizing card data in the browser before it ever reaches your server — your server only ever sees a token/PaymentMethod ID, which keeps you at SAQ A or A-EP while still letting you build a fully custom-styled checkout on your own domain. The trade-off axis is always the same: how much of the sensitive data path do you want to touch, versus how much UX control you need.

Example

// Elements: card data never reaches your server as PAN/CVC
const {error, paymentMethod} = await stripe.createPaymentMethod({
  type: 'card',
  card: cardElement, // lives in an iframe Stripe controls
});
// your server only ever receives paymentMethod.id

Follow-ups

  • Why do iframes matter here, not just "JS runs in the browser"? A same-origin script injection on your page still can't read into a cross-origin iframe's DOM, which is the actual isolation guarantee — a plain `<input>` styled to look like a card field would not have it.
  • Can you fully custom-style an Elements/Components field? Yes, via CSS-in-JS options passed to the element — visual customization without touching the data path.
  • Does using Elements mean you're PCI-exempt? No — you still fill out SAQ A/A-EP and meet requirements like TLS, dependency patching, and website integrity monitoring; scope is reduced, not eliminated.

Common wrong answer: "We use HTTPS to POST the card number to our own API, so it's secure." Encryption in transit says nothing about PCI scope — the moment raw PAN touches your server (even briefly, even to immediately forward it), that server, its logs, its memory, and everything network-adjacent to it is in scope for the full DSS assessment.

Walk through a Payment Intent's (or Adyen's equivalent) state machine — why isn't "charge a card" a single atomic call anymore?mid

Answer. Modern PSP APIs model a payment as a stateful object precisely because a single call can't represent something that may need an intermediate step (3DS challenge, a redirect for a bank-transfer method, a manual review) between "customer intends to pay" and "money is authorized." Stripe's PaymentIntent moves through requires_payment_method → requires_confirmation → requires_action (SCA/3DS challenge, if triggered) → processing → succeeded (or requires_payment_method again on failure, or canceled). Adyen's /payments call similarly returns a resultCode that can be a terminal Authorised/Refused or a non-terminal RedirectShopper/IdentifyShopper/ChallengeShopper requiring a follow-up /payments/details call. The object exists so your server has one durable, idempotent-by-ID resource to poll, retry against, and reconcile — instead of the client racing to re-POST a raw charge and risking a double authorization.

Example

requires_payment_method -> requires_confirmation -> requires_action (3DS) -> processing -> succeeded
                                                                                        \-> requires_payment_method (declined, try again)

Follow-ups

  • What should your server do on requires_action? Return the client secret to the frontend so Stripe.js can drive the 3DS challenge in an iframe/redirect — the server doesn't handle the challenge itself.
  • Can a PaymentIntent go from succeeded back to a failed state? Not directly — a later dispute or chargeback is a separate object/event, not a mutation of the original intent's terminal state.
  • Why processing as its own state rather than blocking until terminal? Some payment methods (ACH debit, some bank redirects) settle asynchronously over hours/days; the API can't make you block a request thread for that.

Common wrong answer: Assuming a successful HTTP 200 from the initial "create payment" call means the money is authorized. It means the PSP accepted your request and is telling you what state the payment is now in — that state is very often not yet terminal, and code that doesn't branch on it will "succeed" client-side while the payment is actually stuck in requires_action.

Authorization, capture, and settlement — what's the difference, and why would you ever delay capture deliberately?mid

Answer. Authorization is the issuer placing a hold on the cardholder's funds/credit line and returning an approval code — no money has moved yet, and it's normally only guaranteed to hold for a few days (varies by network/issuer, commonly around 7). Capture is the merchant telling the acquirer "actually take that authorized amount," which converts the hold into a real transaction destined for settlement. Settlement is the batch/net movement of funds between acquirer and issuer (and eventually into the merchant's bank account), typically happening in daily batches, T+1 to T+several depending on the market and PSP payout schedule. Merchants delay capture — auth now, capture later (up to the auth's validity window) — when the final amount isn't known yet: hotels and car rentals authorize a higher estimate and capture the actual folio; e-commerce authorizes at order time and captures at ship time so a cancelled or out-of-stock order never actually takes money.

Example

// Stripe .NET: authorize now, capture on fulfillment
var intent = await service.CreateAsync(new PaymentIntentCreateOptions {
    Amount = 5000, Currency = "usd", CaptureMethod = "manual", // auth-only
});
// ...days later, at ship time:
await service.CaptureAsync(intent.Id, new PaymentIntentCaptureOptions { AmountToCapture = 4800 });

Follow-ups

  • What happens if you never capture an authorization? It expires and the hold is released automatically — no money moves, but you've burned the authorization and would need a fresh one to actually charge.
  • Can you capture less than you authorized? Yes, generally — partial capture is standard and releases the remainder of the hold; capturing more than authorized is restricted or disallowed by most networks.
  • Does delayed capture protect you from chargebacks? No — that's a separate risk entirely; capture timing is about not moving money prematurely, not about dispute liability.

Common wrong answer: "The money leaves the customer's account at authorization time." For credit cards it doesn't — it's a hold against available credit/funds; conflating this with actual fund movement leads to wrong assumptions about refund timing and about what a "successful payment" webhook actually guarantees.

A PSP ships a new API version every few weeks (Stripe's named monthly releases, e.g. 2026-08-26.dahlia) without asking you. How do you keep an integration stable against that?mid

Answer. Pin an explicit API version in every request (Stripe's Stripe-Version header, set per-account by default but overridable per-request; Adyen versions per-endpoint path, e.g. /v72/payments) rather than riding whatever your account's default happens to be — your account's default can be bumped by an admin, a support action, or a dashboard click, independent of your deploys. Treat an upgrade as a reviewed change: read the changelog, run your integration test suite against the new version in a sandbox, and only then move the pin, rather than silently absorbing whatever "current" resolves to at request time. Backward-compatible changes (new optional params, new response fields, new webhook event types) are safe to ignore; named major releases (Stripe's codenamed versions) can include breaking changes and need code changes.

Example

StripeConfiguration.ApiKey = secretKey;
var options = new RequestOptions { StripeVersion = "2026-08-26.dahlia" }; // pinned, not the account default
var intent = await new PaymentIntentService().CreateAsync(createOptions, options);

Follow-ups

  • What happens to your webhooks if you don't pin a version there too? Webhook payload shape follows the endpoint's configured version if set, otherwise your account default at the time the event was generated — an unpinned endpoint can silently start receiving a different payload shape after an account-level upgrade.
  • How do you test a version bump safely? Stripe allows a 72-hour rollback window after a Workbench-driven account upgrade; more robust is exercising the new version against your test suite in a sandbox before touching production at all.
  • Is a "new event type" a breaking change? No — both PSPs document additive changes (new resources, new optional fields, new event types) as safe; your webhook handler must already tolerate unknown event types gracefully.

Common wrong answer: "We don't set a version, so we always get the latest and greatest automatically." That's exactly backwards from a stability standpoint — it means your integration's request/response shape can change out from under you the moment someone clicks "upgrade" in the dashboard, with zero correlation to your own release cadence.

Card-not-present (CNP) vs. card-present (CP) — why does this distinction reshape your fraud liability and authentication obligations, not just your hardware?senior

Answer. Card-present means the physical card (chip/contactless) was read by a certified terminal at the point of sale, which cryptographically proves the card was physically there — issuers treat this as strong evidence and generally hold significant fraud liability themselves under EMV chip liability-shift rules. Card-not-present — e-commerce, phone orders, most SaaS billing — has no such physical proof, so historically the merchant bore most fraud liability for CNP fraud (the classic "friendly fraud" and stolen-card problem), and that's precisely the gap 3-D Secure/SCA exists to close: a successfully completed 3DS authentication shifts liability for that specific fraud type back to the issuer, the same way chip-and-PIN did for CP. This is why a CNP-only business (nearly all SaaS/e-commerce) has to actively engineer for SCA participation, while a CP-heavy retailer's liability story is largely solved by terminal certification instead.

Example

CP:  chip terminal -> cryptographic proof of physical card -> issuer bears most fraud liability
CNP: browser/API   -> no physical proof -> merchant bears liability, UNLESS 3DS/SCA completed -> liability shifts to issuer

Follow-ups

  • Does 3DS liability shift apply even if the transaction turns out to be fraudulent? Yes — that's the entire point: a completed, successful 3DS authentication shifts liability regardless of the eventual fraud outcome, which is why merchants push authentication rates even when it costs some conversion.
  • What about a frictionless 3DS flow (no challenge shown)? Liability shift still generally applies if the issuer authenticated the transaction data risk-based, even without showing the cardholder a challenge screen — frictionless isn't "no authentication," it's "authentication the issuer decided didn't need a challenge."
  • Is a digital wallet (Apple Pay/Google Pay) transaction CP or CNP? Treated as CNP for the online-purchase flow, but the tokenized, device-authenticated nature usually earns favorable liability treatment similar to CP — a nuance interviewers use to check you're not over-simplifying the binary.

Common wrong answer: Treating CP vs. CNP as purely a technology/channel distinction ("CP is in-store, CNP is online") without connecting it to liability. The channel label is a proxy for a legal/financial consequence — missing that means missing why a business would invest engineering effort in 3DS at all.

Design an integration that can fail over from a primary PSP to a secondary one mid-outage without double-charging a customer or losing idempotency guarantees. What actually breaks first?senior

Answer. The thing that breaks first is idempotency keys, because they're scoped per-PSP — a key you generated for Stripe means nothing to Adyen, so failing over isn't "retry the same request against a different endpoint," it's "start a brand-new attempt with its own identity" and you must guarantee the first attempt is provably dead (not just slow) before you do. Practically: maintain your own payment-attempt aggregate keyed by your own idempotency key, record which PSP and PSP-side reference each attempt used, and only originate a second attempt against the failover PSP after confirming via the primary's status API (not just a client-side timeout) that the first attempt did not succeed — a timeout is not evidence of failure. Route decisions (which PSP is "primary" right now) should live in a health-checked routing layer, and both attempts should write to the same ledger entry keyed by your internal reference so a late success callback from the "failed" PSP still reconciles instead of creating a second, unexplained credit.

Example

1. Create PaymentAttempt(internal_id, status=pending) in YOUR db, in a transaction.
2. Try PSP-A with idempotency-key = internal_id. Timeout.
3. Query PSP-A's status endpoint for internal_id's reference -- don't assume timeout == failure.
4. Only if PSP-A confirms not-succeeded/not-found: mark attempt A dead, open attempt B against PSP-B.
5. Both attempts' terminal webhooks reconcile against the SAME internal_id in your ledger.

Follow-ups

  • What if PSP-A's status endpoint is also down during the outage? That's the actual hard case — you're forced to wait out a bounded uncertainty window (informed by the PSP's own SLA/incident communication) rather than fail over blindly; some shops accept a small double-charge risk with automated refund reconciliation instead.
  • Should the customer see two "processing" states? No — the failover is entirely a backend routing decision; the customer-facing state machine should stay on your internal PaymentAttempt, not leak which PSP is being tried.
  • Does this need a saga/orchestrator, or can it be done with simple retries? At this level of cross-provider coordination it's effectively a saga — simple retry-with-backoff logic assumes a single idempotent target, which multi-PSP failover explicitly isn't.

Common wrong answer: "Just retry the failed request against the backup PSP with the same idempotency key." Idempotency keys are not portable across PSPs, and worse, this framing skips the actual hard problem — proving the first attempt is dead, not just unresponsive — which is exactly the gap that produces a real double-charge incident.

What does multi-acquirer/"smart" routing actually buy a merchant, and what operational cost does it introduce that a single-PSP integration doesn't have?senior

Answer. Smart routing sends a given transaction to whichever acquirer/PSP currently has the best authorization rate, cost, or capability for that card BIN/issuer/geography/currency combination — issuers approve at different rates through different acquirers for reasons that aren't fully public (routing history, risk scoring quirks), so large merchants can measurably lift approval rates and lower interchange-plus cost by routing dynamically rather than always through one PSP. The cost is that you now own an orchestration layer: normalizing wildly different request/response/webhook shapes across PSPs, running the same reconciliation and dispute logic against multiple sources of truth, and maintaining routing rules and their observability (you need to know why a router chose PSP-B over PSP-A for a given failure to debug it at all) — which is exactly the complexity a single-PSP integration or a payment orchestration platform (Primer, Spreedly, Basis Theory) exists to absorb for you, at their own cost premium.

Example

BIN range 4111xx (issuer X) -> historically higher approval via Acquirer B -> route there
BIN range 5500xx (issuer Y) -> equal approval, lower cost via Acquirer A -> route there

Follow-ups

  • At what transaction volume does smart routing typically become worth it? Roughly when the basis-point lift in approval rate, multiplied by transaction value, exceeds the fully-loaded cost of the orchestration/engineering effort — usually a mid-to-large scale decision, not a startup one.
  • Does routing affect your PCI scope? Not directly, if tokenization happens once at the orchestration layer and each PSP receives a token/network-token rather than raw PAN — but the orchestration layer itself now sits in scope.
  • Can routing decisions themselves introduce fraud risk? Yes — naive retry-on-decline-via-different-PSP behavior looks like card testing to fraud systems if not rate-limited and correlated across providers.

Common wrong answer: "Route to whichever PSP is cheapest per transaction." Cost-only routing ignores approval-rate differences, which usually dwarf per-transaction fee differences in total revenue impact — a cheaper PSP that approves 2% fewer transactions can easily cost more in lost revenue than it saves in fees.

You're deciding between integrating directly against Stripe/Adyen APIs versus adopting a payment orchestration layer (Primer, Spreedly, or building your own abstraction). What's the trade-off a principal engineer actually has to name?principal

Answer. Direct integration gives you full access to each PSP's native features the day they ship (new payment methods, new fraud signals, new APIs) and no intermediary's outage or pricing to absorb, at the cost of owning every future PSP-swap or multi-PSP effort yourself — and PSP migrations without an abstraction layer are notoriously expensive precisely because tokens, webhooks, and reconciliation logic are all vendor-shaped. An orchestration layer buys portability and unified reconciliation/reporting across providers, but adds a vendor, a cost layer, and a lowest-common-denominator API surface that lags each PSP's newest features and adds its own latency/outage risk to every transaction. The honest trade-off a principal should name unprompted: build the abstraction internally only when you already know you need multi-PSP for a real reason (geographic acquiring requirements, negotiating leverage, redundancy at your risk tolerance) — premature abstraction against a single PSP mostly reproduces the orchestration platform's cost without its portability benefit, since you're guessing at an interface you have no second implementation to validate against.

Example

Single-PSP, no abstraction: fast to ship, cheap, hard to migrate later.
Own thin abstraction, single PSP: pays the interface-design tax now, for a benefit realized only if you actually add PSP #2.
Third-party orchestration: portability now, at ongoing cost + feature lag + one more outage surface.

Follow-ups

  • What's the single biggest hidden cost of a PSP migration done without any abstraction? Re-establishing chargeback/dispute history and reconciliation continuity — the ledger and dispute tooling built around one PSP's object model rarely transfers cleanly, more than the payment-taking code itself.
  • Does using Stripe's or Adyen's own multi-processor features (Stripe's "Financial Connections"-adjacent tooling, Adyen's own acquirer network) avoid this decision? Partially — it gets you routing within one vendor's ecosystem without a second full integration, which is often the pragmatic middle ground before justifying a true multi-PSP abstraction.
  • How do you communicate this trade-off to a non-technical stakeholder? Frame it as "build now for a future we're not sure we need" vs. "pay rent forever for optionality" — and tie the decision to a concrete trigger condition (a specific market, a specific negotiating deadline) rather than a vague someday.

Common wrong answer: "Always build an abstraction layer so you're never locked into one vendor." Vendor lock-in avoidance sounds prudent but, absent a concrete second-PSP need, it's speculative generality — the abstraction is designed against one real implementation and one imagined one, and it reliably guesses wrong about which parts of the interface actually needed to be abstracted.

02 Idempotency & safe retries

Networks fail, and a payment API call that times out is fundamentally ambiguous — did it never arrive, or did it succeed and only the response got lost? This unit is the second filter after fundamentals: nearly every serious candidate has to reason about safe retries, but the depth expected scales fast from "know the header exists" to "design the key scheme and its storage."

Docs: Stripe idempotent requests · Adyen idempotency

What is an idempotency key and what problem does it solve?screener

Answer. An idempotency key is a client-generated unique string sent with a mutating request (usually in a header, e.g. Stripe's Idempotency-Key) that lets the server recognize "this is the same logical attempt I already processed" and return the original result instead of executing the operation again. It solves the ambiguity of a failed-looking request that may have actually succeeded server-side: without it, retrying a timed-out "charge $50" call risks charging twice; with it, the PSP can safely dedupe the retry and hand back the first attempt's result.

Example

POST /v1/payment_intents
Idempotency-Key: order_38213_attempt_1
Content-Type: application/x-www-form-urlencoded

amount=5000¤cy=usd

Follow-ups

  • How long does Stripe cache an idempotency key's result? 24 hours by default — after that a resend with the same key is treated as a brand-new request.
  • What if you reuse a key with different request parameters? Stripe returns an error rather than silently executing either the old or new parameters — it refuses to guess your intent.
  • Is the key sent on GET requests? No — idempotency is only meaningful for requests with side effects, so it applies to POST/mutating calls.

Common wrong answer: "It's a key so you don't get rate-limited on retries." Idempotency keys are about correctness (no duplicate side effects), not about throughput or rate limiting — conflating the two suggests the candidate hasn't actually hit the double-charge problem it exists to prevent.

Where should the idempotency key come from — generated fresh per HTTP call, or tied to a business-level identity like an order ID?screener

Answer. It must be tied to the logical business operation, not to the HTTP call itself — if you generate a new random key every time you retry, you've defeated the entire mechanism, because the PSP now sees each retry as a brand-new request. The key should be deterministic from something that identifies "this exact attempt to charge this exact order," commonly derived from your own order/attempt ID (e.g. order_38213_attempt_1), generated once and reused across every retry of that specific attempt until it reaches a terminal state.

Example

var key = $"order_{order.Id}_attempt_{attemptNumber}";
// same key reused for every retry of THIS attempt; a genuinely new attempt (new amount, new card) gets a new key

Follow-ups

  • What if the customer clicks "pay" twice, impatient with a slow page? That should still map to the same attempt/key if it's within the same checkout session — debouncing the UI and keying off the session/order, not the click event, prevents a second real attempt.
  • Should retries after a genuine decline reuse the same key? No — a declined payment is a terminal, known outcome; trying again (possibly with a different card) is a new attempt and needs a new key.
  • Is a random UUID ever the right choice? Only if you durably persist it against the business operation before the first send, so every retry of that exact operation can look it up and reuse it.

Common wrong answer: "Generate a new UUID for the idempotency key on every request, that's what idempotent means." This inverts the mechanism entirely — idempotency requires the *same* key across retries of the *same* operation; a fresh key every time provides zero protection against duplicate charges.

Your client library throws a network timeout on a charge request. What do you do next, step by step?mid

Answer. First, do not treat the timeout as either success or failure — it's unknown. Retry the exact same request with the exact same idempotency key; if the original request had actually reached the PSP and succeeded, the retry returns the cached result with no new side effect. If retries also keep timing out, fall back to querying the resource directly (e.g. GET /v1/payment_intents/{id} using your own generated ID, or listing by your idempotency key) to determine ground truth before giving up or escalating to the customer. Only after confirming via a read that no successful attempt exists should you consider the operation genuinely failed and eligible for a real new attempt.

Example

1. POST with Idempotency-Key=K -> timeout
2. Retry POST with SAME K (backoff) -> if PSP got #1, returns cached result; else executes once
3. Still uncertain? GET the resource by your own reference to check ground truth
4. Only after confirmed non-existence/failure: treat as failed, allow a NEW attempt (new key)

Follow-ups

  • How many retries, and with what backoff? A handful with exponential backoff and jitter (e.g. Stripe's own SDKs default to a small bounded retry count) — endless retrying just delays the eventual "I don't know" state.
  • Is it safe to retry a non-idempotent-by-design endpoint the same way? No — this pattern only works for endpoints that explicitly document idempotency-key support; retrying a plain POST without one on ambiguous failure can double-execute.
  • What should the customer see while this resolves? A "processing" state, not an immediate error — surfacing failure prematurely on an ambiguous timeout is how support tickets about "I got charged but the site said it failed" happen.

Common wrong answer: "If the request times out, show the customer an error and let them try again." Without a stable idempotency key backing that "try again," you've just described the exact sequence that produces a duplicate charge — the timeout tells you nothing about whether the original attempt landed.

Design the idempotency key storage on your own side (not the PSP's) — what does it need to persist, and what's the failure mode if you get it wrong?mid

Answer. You need a durable record, written in the same transaction as (or before) the outbound call, mapping your idempotency key to the operation's current state: pending, and once known, the terminal outcome and the PSP's own reference ID. This has to survive a process crash between "we decided to charge" and "we got a response" — if that record only exists in memory or is written after the PSP call returns, a crash mid-flight loses the key entirely and a naive retry-on-restart will generate a fresh key, defeating the whole mechanism. The classic pattern: insert a `PaymentAttempt` row with status=`pending` and the chosen key inside the same DB transaction that decides to attempt payment, *before* calling the PSP; on restart/retry, look up by your own business key (order ID + attempt number) to recover the idempotency key you already committed to, rather than minting a new one.

Example

-- written BEFORE the PSP call, in the same transaction as the business decision to charge
INSERT INTO payment_attempts (order_id, attempt_no, idempotency_key, status)
VALUES (38213, 1, 'order_38213_attempt_1', 'pending');

Follow-ups

  • What if the process crashes right after the insert but before calling the PSP? Fine — on recovery you find the pending row, still have the same key, and can safely call the PSP (which may itself be a genuinely-first call, or a dedupe if some earlier process had actually reached it).
  • What if it crashes after the PSP responds but before you write the outcome? This is exactly why the retry with the same key matters — the recovery path replays the same key, and the PSP's own idempotency layer returns the already-computed result.
  • Should the idempotency-key table have a uniqueness constraint? Yes, on (order_id, attempt_no) at minimum, so a race between two workers picking up the same order can't mint two different keys for what should be one attempt.

Common wrong answer: "Generate the idempotency key right before calling the PSP's API, in the request-building code." If that code path can run twice (a retried job, a double-clicked button, a re-delivered queue message) before anything is durably recorded, each run mints a different key and idempotency protection never engages — the key has to be committed to storage first, independent of the call.

Beyond payment creation, which other operations in a payment flow need idempotency, and does the same key scheme work for all of them?senior

Answer. Refunds, captures, and payouts are all mutating, money-moving operations and need the same protection — a refund retried without a stable key can refund twice. But the key scheme differs by operation because the "logical attempt" differs: a refund's natural key is tied to (payment ID, refund reason/attempt), a capture's to (payment intent ID, capture attempt), and a payout's to a settlement batch or scheduled run ID. You cannot reuse the original payment's idempotency key for these follow-on operations — they're different logical operations against the same underlying payment, and PSPs scope idempotency keys per-operation-type, not globally per-payment.

Example

Charge:  Idempotency-Key: order_38213_charge_attempt_1
Refund:  Idempotency-Key: order_38213_refund_attempt_1   (different key, same order)
Capture: Idempotency-Key: order_38213_capture_attempt_1

Follow-ups

  • Does a webhook retry need idempotency on your receiving side? Yes — that's a distinct problem (deduping inbound events, covered in the webhooks unit), not the outbound idempotency-key mechanism, but it rhymes: both exist because at-least-once delivery is the default assumption everywhere in payments.
  • What happens if you issue two different-amount refunds with the same key? Same as payment creation — the PSP treats mismatched parameters under a reused key as an error, refusing to guess which one you meant.
  • Is a scheduled payout run idempotent by nature? Only if the batch job itself is keyed by the settlement period/date so a re-run of the same job doesn't re-trigger already-paid-out transfers — this needs the same discipline even though there's often no explicit "idempotency key" field for it.

Common wrong answer: "Refunds are just reversing a charge, so they're automatically safe to retry." A refund is its own mutating operation with its own duplicate-execution risk — "it's just a reversal" is exactly the reasoning that leads to double-refunding a customer when a retry isn't keyed properly.

Your idempotency-key table is growing forever and query latency on it is creeping up. How do you handle expiry, and what has to be true before you can safely delete a key?senior

Answer. A key can be safely expired/deleted once its associated operation has reached a terminal state and enough time has passed that no legitimate retry (client-side timeout-and-retry window, queue redelivery, webhook-driven reconciliation) could plausibly still be replaying that request — commonly a window well beyond the PSP's own idempotency-cache TTL (Stripe caches 24h server-side, so your own retained record should outlive that, often 7-30 days, to also protect against slow downstream retries you don't control). Terminal-but-not-yet-safe states matter: a payment that's `processing` (e.g., an async bank debit) cannot have its key reclaimed just because some time passed — you need the actual terminal outcome, not just elapsed time, as the primary gate, with a TTL as the secondary safety margin after that.

Example

DELETE FROM payment_attempts
WHERE status IN ('succeeded','failed','canceled')
  AND resolved_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days';

Follow-ups

  • Why not just rely on the PSP's own 24-hour idempotency cache and drop your own record immediately? Your own retries can be triggered by things far slower than the PSP's cache window — a stuck queue message redelivered days later, a batch reconciliation job — and losing your own record means you can no longer recognize "we already tried this" independent of the PSP.
  • Does archiving instead of deleting change the calculus? It's often the better choice — move resolved attempts to cold storage instead of deleting, keeping the hot table small while retaining the audit trail dispute/chargeback handling will eventually want.
  • What if a key is deleted and then a very late retry arrives? It gets treated as a brand-new attempt — which is why the retention window needs to comfortably exceed any retry path you actually have in production, verified against real telemetry, not guessed.

Common wrong answer: "Expire keys after a fixed short TTL like an hour, same as a cache." Idempotency records aren't a performance cache you can evict cheaply — evicting one too early re-opens the exact duplicate-execution window the key existed to close, for whatever slow retry path you didn't anticipate.

Two instances of your service both pick up the same "retry this failed payment" job at the same time (a scheduler double-fire, or an at-least-once queue). How does idempotency alone fail to protect you here, and what else is needed?senior

Answer. Idempotency keys protect against duplicate *execution at the PSP*, but they don't protect against a race in your own application logic before the request is even sent — if both instances read "status=pending, no key assigned yet," both can independently decide to mint a key and proceed, and depending on timing you can get two different keys for what should be one attempt (defeating dedup) or a benign race where both send the same key concurrently (which most PSPs do handle correctly by serializing on the key server-side, but you shouldn't rely on that as your only safety net). The fix is concurrency control on your side: a DB-level lock or conditional update (`UPDATE ... SET status='in_flight' WHERE status='pending'`, checking rows-affected) so only one instance actually proceeds to call the PSP, with the other backing off. This is a distributed-locking problem, not an idempotency-key problem.

Example

UPDATE payment_attempts
SET status = 'in_flight', locked_by = 'worker-7', locked_at = NOW()
WHERE id = 38213 AND status = 'pending';
-- if rows-affected = 0, another worker already claimed it; back off

Follow-ups

  • Would a distributed lock (Redis/etcd) work instead of a DB conditional update? Yes, functionally equivalent for this purpose — the DB approach is often preferred simply because the payment-attempt record already lives there and you avoid a second moving part.
  • What if the "winning" worker then crashes mid-call, holding the lock? Locks need a lease/timeout so a crashed holder's claim eventually expires and another worker can retry — an un-expiring lock turns one crash into a permanently stuck payment.
  • Does this change if the PSP called is proven to serialize correctly on identical idempotency keys? It reduces the blast radius of the race (both might legitimately land on the same key and get deduped correctly) but doesn't eliminate the risk of two different keys being minted, so the application-side lock is still the right primary defense.

Common wrong answer: "We use idempotency keys, so concurrent workers picking up the same job is already safe." Idempotency keys guard the PSP boundary; they say nothing about two of your own processes racing to decide what key to use or whether to act at all — that race has to be closed independently.

Is retrying a payment request always safe just because you have an idempotency key? Name a case where it isn't.senior

Answer. No — idempotency keys protect against duplicate *side effects for identical requests*, not against every consequence of retrying. Two concrete failure modes: first, if the underlying resource has expired or changed state between attempts (e.g. retrying a capture against a PaymentIntent whose authorization already lapsed), the key doesn't resurrect a dead authorization — you'll get a legitimate new error, and blindly retrying more just wastes calls. Second, and subtler: retrying too aggressively against a PSP that's degraded (not down, but slow/error-prone) can look like abusive traffic or contribute to cascading overload, and some PSPs will rate-limit or flag an account for it — the safety of a retry is about correctness of the single call, not about whether *retrying at all*, at whatever rate, is a good idea operationally.

Example

Attempt 1: authorize (hold created, 7-day validity)
[9 days pass, hold expired]
Attempt 2 (retry, same key): capture -> fails, "PaymentIntent no longer capturable"
-- idempotency key didn't help; the resource's own state moved on

Follow-ups

  • What should a retry policy do differently during a PSP-wide incident vs. an isolated timeout? Back off much more aggressively (circuit-breaker style) during a detected incident rather than retrying at the same cadence — hammering a degraded dependency with retries is a classic amplifier of an outage, covered further in the incident-response unit.
  • Can an idempotency key itself become stale? Yes — most PSPs cap how long a key is honored (Stripe: 24h); a "retry" sent after that window is treated as brand-new, silently losing the protection you thought you had.
  • Does this argue against retries generally? No — it argues for bounded, backed-off retries with circuit breakers and status-check fallbacks, not naive infinite retry loops.

Common wrong answer: "As long as I send the idempotency key, I can retry as many times, as fast as I want, with no downside." Idempotency solves the correctness half of retries; it says nothing about retry storms overwhelming a struggling dependency or about resource state moving on between attempts — both are real production incidents at payment-heavy companies.

How would you design idempotency for an operation that has no natural PSP-level idempotency-key support at all — say, an internal ledger-posting API you built yourself?principal

Answer. You build the same mechanism the PSPs build for you: a durable idempotency-key table scoped to your API, checked and written atomically with the actual side effect. Concretely — the endpoint accepts a client-supplied key; on receipt, it attempts an INSERT of (key, request-hash, status=processing) with a unique constraint on the key; if the insert succeeds, you're the first to see this key and proceed to do the real work, then update the row with the result; if the insert fails on the unique constraint, another request with this key already exists, so you look up its stored result and return that instead of redoing the work (blocking/polling if it's still in-flight). The request-hash matters too — storing a hash of the meaningful request parameters alongside the key lets you detect and reject the "same key, different payload" misuse case the same way Stripe does, rather than silently returning a mismatched cached result.

Example

-- atomic claim, race-safe by construction
INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (key, request_hash, status)
VALUES ('ledger_post_38213_1', 'sha256:...', 'processing')
ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING
RETURNING key;
-- 0 rows returned => already claimed; look up and return the stored result/hash-mismatch error

Follow-ups

  • What if the "real work" itself is a multi-step operation (post to ledger, then notify downstream)? The idempotency-key row should only be marked complete after the *entire* logical operation finishes, and each internal step needs its own idempotent design too — a half-finished multi-step operation retried from the top must not redo already-completed sub-steps, which usually means storing per-step progress, not just an overall status.
  • How do you avoid the idempotency table itself becoming a single point of contention under high write volume? Shard/partition it the same way you'd shard any high-write table (by key hash, or by tenant), and keep the claim-write as small and fast as possible — it should never itself become slower than the operation it's protecting.
  • Would you build this yourself, or reach for an existing pattern/library? This is a well-known pattern (often called the "inbox/outbox" pattern in distributed-systems literature) — worth naming the prior art rather than presenting it as a novel design, since interviewers are checking for pattern recognition as much as construction ability.

Common wrong answer: "Just check if a record with that ID already exists before inserting, in a normal SELECT-then-INSERT." That's a check-then-act race — two concurrent requests can both pass the SELECT before either INSERTs, producing exactly the duplicate the whole exercise was meant to prevent. The claim step must be atomic (a unique constraint, a conditional insert), not a read followed by a separate write.

03 Webhook handling

Webhooks are how a PSP tells you about state changes it discovered asynchronously — a delayed bank debit clearing, a dispute opening, a subscription renewal failing. Nearly everyone integrating payments ends up owning a webhook endpoint, and it's a rich area for exposing candidates who've never actually operated one in production: signature verification, at-least-once delivery, and out-of-order arrival are all things that look optional in a tutorial and are not optional in reality.

Docs: Stripe webhooks · Adyen webhooks

Why can't you just trust a webhook POST because it came in on the URL you registered for it?screener

Answer. An HTTP endpoint is public by definition — anyone who guesses or discovers the URL can POST to it, and a URL alone proves nothing about who sent the request. Without verification, an attacker could POST a fabricated payment_intent.succeeded event and get your system to ship an order or unlock content for a payment that never happened. PSPs solve this by signing each webhook payload with a secret only you and the PSP know, and you must verify that signature on every request before trusting the payload — the URL's obscurity is not a security control.

Example

POST /webhooks/stripe HTTP/1.1
Stripe-Signature: t=1725800000,v1=5257a869e7...
Content-Type: application/json

{"type":"payment_intent.succeeded","data":{...}}   <- unverified, this JSON could be anything

Follow-ups

  • Does HTTPS alone protect you here? No — TLS protects the transport from eavesdropping/tampering in transit, it says nothing about who the sender is; anyone can open a TLS connection to your public endpoint.
  • Is IP allowlisting to the PSP's published IP ranges a substitute for signature verification? It's a defense-in-depth layer at best — IP ranges change, get spoofed less reliably than you'd like, and this still doesn't verify the payload's integrity the way a signature does.
  • What should happen to a request that fails signature verification? Reject it with a 4xx and do not process the payload at all — not even for logging the "event" as if it were real.

Common wrong answer: "We keep the webhook URL secret/unguessable, so it's fine." Security through obscurity for a public HTTP endpoint isn't security at all — it can be found via logs, referrers, DNS enumeration, or simple brute force, and the fix (signature verification) is a solved, standard part of every PSP's API, so there's no excuse to skip it.

Walk through how signature verification actually works — what's being hashed, and why does the timestamp matter?mid

Answer. The PSP computes an HMAC (Stripe uses HMAC-SHA256) over a string built from a timestamp and the raw request body, using a webhook signing secret it generated when you registered the endpoint; that HMAC is sent in a header alongside the timestamp. On your side, you recompute the same HMAC over the same timestamp+body string using your copy of the signing secret, and compare it to the one in the header using a constant-time comparison. The timestamp is included and separately checked against a tolerance window (Stripe defaults to 5 minutes) to prevent replay attacks — without it, an attacker who captured a legitimately-signed request once could resend it indefinitely and it would still verify, since the signature itself never expires on its own.

Example

var stripeEvent = EventUtility.ConstructEvent(
    json, signatureHeader, webhookSecret,
    tolerance: 300 // seconds - rejects if |now - header timestamp| exceeds this
);

Follow-ups

  • Why HMAC and not just a plain hash of the body? A plain hash lets anyone recompute and match it without knowing any secret; HMAC requires the shared secret to produce a valid signature, which is what actually authenticates the sender.
  • Why compare signatures in constant time? To avoid a timing side-channel that could let an attacker incrementally guess the correct signature byte-by-byte via response-time differences — a real, if narrow, attack class against naive string equality checks.
  • What happens if your server clock drifts significantly? Legitimate webhooks can start failing the timestamp-tolerance check — this is a real, recurring on-call incident cause and argues for NTP-synced infrastructure as a hidden webhook-reliability dependency.

Common wrong answer: "Verify the signature by hashing the parsed JSON object and comparing." Verification must run against the exact raw request body bytes, before any JSON parsing or middleware reformatting — re-serializing parsed JSON can reorder keys or change whitespace, producing a different byte sequence than what was actually signed, and the check will fail unpredictably (or in the worst framework misconfigurations, be skipped instead of failing loudly).

Your webhook handler processed an event, but then the same event ID shows up again an hour later. Is this a bug on the PSP's side?mid

Answer. No — this is expected, documented behavior. Every major PSP webhook system guarantees at-least-once delivery, not exactly-once: if your endpoint doesn't respond with a 2xx quickly enough, times out, or the PSP's own delivery infrastructure has a transient issue, it will redeliver the same event, sometimes more than once, sometimes after a meaningful delay. Your handler is responsible for idempotency on receipt — checking the event's unique ID against a store of already-processed IDs before acting, and skipping (but still 2xx-acknowledging) a duplicate rather than reprocessing it.

Example

INSERT INTO processed_webhook_events (event_id, processed_at)
VALUES ('evt_1Nx...', NOW())
ON CONFLICT (event_id) DO NOTHING
RETURNING event_id;
-- 0 rows returned => already processed, skip side effects, still return 200

Follow-ups

  • How long should you retain processed-event IDs? Longer than the PSP's own maximum redelivery window (Stripe retries for up to 3 days by default) plus margin — short-lived dedup tables reopen the exact gap they're meant to close.
  • Should the dedup check happen before or after signature verification? After — you don't want to spend a DB round-trip deduping a payload you haven't yet confirmed is authentic.
  • Is "at-least-once" the same guarantee your own downstream systems get if you re-publish the event internally? Only if you deliberately design it that way — re-publishing to an internal queue introduces a second delivery hop with its own guarantees to reason about, not automatically inherited from the PSP's.

Common wrong answer: "Duplicate webhook deliveries mean something is wrong with our integration or the PSP is broken." At-least-once delivery with occasional duplicates is the normal, advertised contract — treating it as an anomaly to investigate (rather than a routine case to handle) means the handler was never built to be idempotent in the first place.

Can webhook events arrive out of order — and if so, how do you build a handler that doesn't corrupt state when a "payment succeeded" event arrives after a later "payment refunded" event?senior

Answer. Yes, out-of-order delivery is a documented possibility — retries, multiple delivery workers, and network variance mean you cannot assume events land in the order they were generated. The fix is to make your handler state-transition-aware rather than purely event-driven: instead of blindly applying whatever an event says, fetch the current authoritative state of the underlying object (re-query the PaymentIntent/charge by ID) and use the event only as a *signal to go check*, or, if acting directly on the event payload, compare event/object version numbers or timestamps and reject/ignore an event that's older than state you've already applied. Treating the webhook purely as a trigger to re-sync from the source of truth, rather than as the sole carrier of truth, sidesteps the ordering problem entirely.

Example

Event A: payment_intent.succeeded (created_at=T1)
Event B: charge.refunded          (created_at=T2, T2 > T1)
-- if A arrives AFTER B due to retry/redelivery:
-- naive handler: applies A last, incorrectly flips state back to "paid" over a refund
-- correct handler: re-fetches PaymentIntent by ID, sees it's actually refunded, no-ops on stale A

Follow-ups

  • Does Stripe guarantee ordering within a single object's event stream? It does not make a hard ordering guarantee across delivery attempts, which is precisely why handlers should re-fetch or version-check rather than trust arrival order.
  • Is this the same problem as the idempotency/dedup one from the previous question? Related but distinct — dedup handles the *same* event arriving twice; ordering handles *different* events for the same object arriving in an unexpected sequence.
  • What's a lighter-weight mitigation if a full re-fetch-on-every-event is too expensive? Store the last-applied event's timestamp/sequence per object and reject incoming events older than it — cheaper than a re-fetch, though it requires the PSP's event payload to carry a reliable ordering field.

Common wrong answer: "We process events in the order our queue delivers them, so ordering is guaranteed." Your queue can only guarantee the order it received events in, not the order they were generated in by the PSP, and even queue-level ordering guarantees typically don't survive redelivery/retry paths — the handler logic itself has to be order-tolerant.

Your webhook handler does three things synchronously inside the request: verifies the signature, updates your database, and sends a customer confirmation email. What's wrong with this design?senior

Answer. The handler is now only as reliable and fast as its slowest, flakiest dependency — if the email provider is slow or down, the whole request hangs or fails, and because PSPs expect a fast 2xx response (Stripe times out around 10-20 seconds and will treat a non-response as delivery failure, triggering a retry), a slow downstream side effect can cause the PSP to redeliver an event whose primary work (the DB update) already succeeded, adding unnecessary duplicate-handling load. The fix is to keep the webhook handler minimal and fast: verify the signature, durably record the event (or a "do this work" job), and return 2xx immediately; do the actual business logic (DB writes beyond the raw event log, emails, downstream notifications) asynchronously, off the request path, in a worker that can retry independently without holding the PSP's delivery mechanism hostage.

Example

Webhook handler (fast path):
  1. verify signature
  2. INSERT raw event (dedup on event_id) -> enqueue job
  3. return 200 immediately

Worker (async, own retry policy):
  4. process event: update DB state
  5. trigger email/notification

Follow-ups

  • What if the async worker fails after the webhook already returned 200? That's now a job-queue reliability problem (dead-letter queues, alerting, retry policy) entirely decoupled from the PSP's own redelivery mechanism — which is the point: two independent, appropriately-tuned retry systems instead of one overloaded one.
  • Does this add latency to when the customer actually sees their confirmation email? A little, but it's usually imperceptible (sub-second queue hop) and vastly preferable to coupling email deliverability to webhook reliability.
  • Should the raw event log ever be pruned? Keep it at least as long as useful for replay/debugging and reconciliation — it's often the cheapest source of truth for "what did the PSP actually tell us and when," valuable well beyond the processing itself.

Common wrong answer: "As long as we eventually return a 200, it doesn't matter what order or how long the work inside takes." A slow synchronous handler risks the PSP's own timeout firing before your 200 is sent, which triggers a redelivery for work that may have already completed — the *speed* of acknowledgment is itself part of correct webhook design, not just an optimization.

How do you test webhook handling in local development and in CI, given that the real event source is a third-party PSP?senior

Answer. For local development, PSP-provided CLI tools (Stripe CLI's stripe listen/stripe trigger) forward real or synthetic test-mode events to a local port via a tunnel, which is the closest thing to production behavior including real signatures signed with a CLI-provided secret. For CI, you generally don't want a network dependency on a third party at all — construct synthetic webhook payloads matching the PSP's documented schema, sign them yourself with a test signing secret using the same HMAC scheme the PSP uses, and POST them to your handler in an integration test; this exercises your actual verification and processing code paths without needing live PSP connectivity or non-deterministic test-mode event timing. Both layers matter: the CLI-forwarded events catch schema drift and real-world quirks; the synthetic CI tests catch regressions cheaply and deterministically on every commit.

Example

stripe listen --forward-to localhost:5000/webhooks/stripe
stripe trigger payment_intent.succeeded

Follow-ups

  • How do you keep synthetic CI payloads from drifting out of sync with the PSP's real schema over time? Periodically refresh fixture payloads from real test-mode events captured via the CLI, and treat a PSP API-version bump as a trigger to review webhook fixtures, not just request-side code.
  • Should you test the "signature verification fails" path too? Yes — deliberately send a payload with a bad/missing signature and assert a 4xx with no side effects, since that's a security-relevant code path easy to accidentally break during a refactor.
  • Can you replay production webhook events into a staging environment for testing? Some PSPs support this (Stripe: resending an event from the dashboard/API to a different endpoint) which is valuable for reproducing a specific production bug against a fix.

Common wrong answer: "We just point our test-mode webhook endpoint at a public staging URL and click around the checkout manually to see events arrive." That validates the happy path once, manually, but gives you no repeatable regression coverage, no test for malformed/duplicate/out-of-order events, and no CI signal — exactly the categories of bugs that reach production in webhook handlers.

You're migrating webhook signing secrets (rotating a leaked secret, or re-registering an endpoint). How do you do this without dropping events during the cutover?principal

Answer. Most PSPs support registering multiple signing secrets/endpoints simultaneously for exactly this reason — the safe sequence is: add the new secret/endpoint alongside the old one (don't remove the old one yet), update your verification code to accept signatures from *either* secret during the transition window, confirm via monitoring that events are verifying successfully under the new secret, and only then deregister the old secret/endpoint. Doing this as a single atomic swap (rotate the secret and remove the old one in the same deploy) creates a race window where in-flight or briefly-delayed events signed with the old secret arrive after your code only knows the new one, and get wrongly rejected as invalid signatures — a self-inflicted, entirely avoidable outage.

Example

1. Register new signing secret alongside old one (dual-active)
2. Deploy handler that tries verification against BOTH secrets
3. Monitor: confirm new-secret verifications succeeding, error rate flat
4. Deregister old secret only after a safety window with zero old-secret usage

Follow-ups

  • How would you detect that the old secret is now safe to remove? Log which secret successfully verified each event and watch that metric drop to zero for a sustained period before cutting over fully.
  • Does the same principle apply to rotating API keys used for outbound calls? Yes, structurally identical — dual-valid-secret windows during rotation are a general pattern for avoiding a hard cutover race, applicable to API keys, JWT signing keys, and webhook secrets alike.
  • What if the PSP only supports one active signing secret per endpoint, no dual-registration? Register a second endpoint URL with the new secret, run both endpoints in parallel pointed at the same handler logic, then delete the old endpoint registration once confident — achieving the same dual-window safety via a different mechanism.

Common wrong answer: "Rotate the secret in the PSP dashboard and update our environment variable in the same deploy." Without a dual-valid window, any event generated in the gap between the PSP-side rotation and your deploy finishing (or any event delayed/retried from before the rotation) will fail verification and be silently dropped or alerted on as an attack, when it's actually a legitimate event caught by a self-inflicted timing gap.

Design a webhook ingestion system that has to handle 50,000 events/day across a dozen event types, stay correct under PSP outages and redelivery storms, and give you a clean audit trail for a future compliance review. What are the components?principal

Answer. A durable, layered pipeline: (1) a thin, fast ingress handler that only verifies signature, checks for a dedup hit, persists the raw verified payload immutably (append-only, never mutated — this is your audit trail), and returns 2xx; (2) an async dispatcher that reads newly-ingested events and routes them by type to per-type processing logic, decoupled so a slow/broken handler for one event type doesn't back up ingestion of all the others; (3) per-event-type idempotent processors that apply business logic against current authoritative state (not blind event application, per the ordering discussion) and themselves retry independently with backoff and dead-lettering on repeated failure; (4) monitoring on ingestion lag, processing failure rate, and dead-letter queue depth, since a silent backlog is the actual production failure mode, not a crash. The audit trail requirement is mostly satisfied for free by step 1's immutable raw log — a compliance reviewer wants to see exactly what the PSP sent and when, separate from whatever your business logic later derived from it.

Example

PSP -> [ingress: verify+dedup+persist raw, 2xx fast] -> raw_events (append-only, audit trail)
                                                              |
                                                     [dispatcher: route by event.type]
                                                        /        |         \
                                              [processor A] [processor B] [processor C]
                                              (own retry/DLQ)  (own retry/DLQ) ...

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle a redelivery storm (PSP resends thousands of events after resolving its own incident)? The ingress layer's fast-2xx-and-queue design absorbs bursts naturally since it doesn't do synchronous processing; the dedup check keeps re-delivered duplicates cheap no-ops; backpressure on the dispatcher (not the ingress) prevents a burst from overwhelming downstream processors.
  • What would you monitor to know this system is healthy, beyond uptime? Ingestion-to-processing lag (are events piling up faster than they're processed), per-event-type error rates, and dead-letter queue growth — each catches a different failure mode a simple health check would miss.
  • Why separate ingestion from processing at all, rather than one handler that does everything carefully with try/catch? Because a single slow or buggy processor for one event type shouldn't be able to delay or fail acknowledgment of unrelated event types — coupling them means one bad deploy affects the PSP's view of your entire endpoint's health, not just one feature.

Common wrong answer: "Add a message queue in front of the webhook handler and call it done." A queue alone doesn't establish idempotency, ordering tolerance, per-type isolation, or an immutable audit log — the queue is one component of the pipeline, and treating it as the whole answer skips exactly the design decisions (dedup keying, dead-lettering, raw-vs-derived separation) that this question is actually probing for.

04 3-D Secure & Strong Customer Authentication

Anyone selling to European cardholders, or dealing with US issuers pushing back on CNP fraud, runs into 3DS/SCA. This unit checks whether a candidate understands it as a liability-and-conversion trade-off with real UX cost, not just "the popup that sometimes appears." EMVCo's spec is mid-revision right now — 2.3.1.1 is current production, and a 2.4.0.0 draft's comment period runs to July 2026 — which is worth knowing at senior+ level.

Docs: EMVCo 3-D Secure · Stripe 3DS

What problem does 3-D Secure solve, and what does SCA (Strong Customer Authentication) require concretely?screener

Answer. 3-D Secure ("three domain secure," referring to the acquirer, issuer, and interoperability domains it spans) is a protocol that lets the *issuer* authenticate the cardholder directly during an online purchase, rather than the merchant just trusting whatever card details were typed in. SCA is the regulatory requirement (originating in the EU's PSD2, now the standard expectation many issuers enforce globally for CNP transactions) that this authentication combine at least two independent factors from: something you know (password/PIN), something you have (a phone receiving an OTP, a banking app), and something you are (biometric). In practice, a 3DS challenge showing "approve this in your banking app" or "enter the code we texted you" *is* the SCA factor combination being satisfied live.

Example

Factor 1 (something you have): banking app push notification
Factor 2 (something you are):  fingerprint/face confirm inside that app
= two independent factors = SCA satisfied via a 3DS challenge

Follow-ups

  • Is 3DS the only way to satisfy SCA? No — it's the dominant mechanism for card payments, but other payment methods (bank redirects, wallets) satisfy SCA through their own native authentication (e.g. biometric wallet unlock).
  • Does SCA apply to every single transaction? No — there are documented exemptions (low-value, recurring, merchant-initiated, trusted beneficiaries) covered later in this unit.
  • Who decides whether a given transaction needs a challenge? Ultimately the issuer, informed by risk signals from both the merchant/acquirer and its own fraud models — the merchant can request/hint via the 3DS request but doesn't have final say.

Common wrong answer: "3DS is when you enter your card's CVV." CVV entry is a card-verification step orthogonal to 3DS entirely — conflating the two suggests the candidate hasn't distinguished issuer-side cardholder authentication from basic card-data-matching checks.

Frictionless flow vs. challenge flow in 3DS 2.x — what's actually different, and does liability shift apply to both?mid

Answer. In EMV 3DS 2.x, the merchant's 3DS SDK/integration silently sends a rich set of risk-relevant data (device fingerprint, browsing behavior, transaction history context) to the issuer's Access Control Server as part of every attempt. If the issuer's risk engine is confident enough from that data alone, it approves without ever presenting the cardholder anything — that's frictionless. If it isn't confident, it returns a challenge requirement and the cardholder sees an OTP/banking-app prompt — that's the challenge flow. Liability shift generally applies to both outcomes, provided the 3DS authentication process itself completed and returned a positive authentication result (an ECI/CAVV value indicating successful authentication) — frictionless isn't "unauthenticated," it's "authenticated without a visible UI step," and the liability protection follows the authentication result, not the presence of a challenge screen.

Example

Frictionless: rich data -> issuer ACS confident -> authenticated, no UI shown -> liability shift applies
Challenge:    rich data -> issuer ACS not confident -> OTP/app prompt shown -> if passed -> liability shift applies

Follow-ups

  • What data actually drives the frictionless decision? Device/browser fingerprint, IP geolocation consistency, prior transaction history with that merchant/cardholder pair, and dozens of other signals defined in the 3DS 2.x message spec — the "2" in 3DS2 is largely about carrying enough of this data to make frictionless viable at scale.
  • Can a merchant force a challenge even when the issuer would go frictionless? Yes, via a request indicator in the 3DS message (e.g. requesting a challenge for a high-risk SKU or first-time customer) — the issuer can still override, but the merchant has some influence.
  • Why did 3DS1 (the old redirect-heavy version) get replaced? Its full-page redirects and clunky static-password UX tanked conversion and were widely considered insecure/phishable — 3DS2's in-app/inline flows and richer risk data were designed specifically to fix both problems.

Common wrong answer: "Frictionless means no authentication happened, so there's no liability protection." This gets the mechanism backwards — frictionless is a *successful, silent* authentication outcome, not a skipped one; liability shift tracks the authentication result, not whether the cardholder saw a prompt.

Name the SCA exemptions that let a transaction skip a 3DS challenge under PSD2, and explain the trade-off each one makes.mid

Answer. The main PSD2-defined exemptions: low-value transactions (under roughly €30, with a cumulative counter resetting the exemption after a handful of consecutive uses or a cumulative amount, forcing periodic re-authentication); transaction risk analysis (TRA), where the acquirer/PSP's fraud rate is low enough to qualify for a risk-based exemption up to higher value thresholds; recurring/subscription payments after the first (initial) payment, which does require full authentication; merchant-initiated transactions (no cardholder present at all, e.g. a scheduled top-up); and trusted beneficiaries, where a cardholder has previously whitelisted a merchant with their issuer. Each trades authentication friction for conversion, but every exemption still leaves liability with whoever *requested* the exemption if the transaction turns out fraudulent — unlike a completed 3DS authentication, an exemption is a bet the merchant/issuer is choosing to take, not a liability shift.

Example

Low-value:        transaction < ~EUR 30, and running total since last SCA < ~EUR 100 (or 5 consecutive)
TRA:               acquirer fraud rate low enough -> exemption threshold up to EUR 500
Recurring (2nd+):  first payment authenticated; subsequent same-amount charges exempt
Merchant-initiated: no cardholder interaction at all (scheduled charge)

Follow-ups

  • Who actually applies for/requests an exemption, the merchant or the issuer? The acquirer/merchant requests it in the authorization message; the issuer can still decide to challenge anyway if its own risk assessment disagrees.
  • If a merchant requests a low-value exemption and the issuer grants it, and the transaction is later fraudulent, who eats the loss? Typically the merchant/acquirer that requested the exemption, since no full authentication with liability-shift protection occurred.
  • Does an exemption mean 0% chance of a challenge? No — it's a request the issuer can decline; a challenge can still be imposed if the issuer's own systems flag the transaction regardless of the exemption request.

Common wrong answer: "Exemptions mean the transaction is fully safe and processed exactly like an authenticated one." Exemptions are a deliberate friction-reduction trade that keeps fraud liability with the requester — treating them as equivalent to a completed authentication misunderstands the entire risk allocation the exemption represents.

Implement 3DS for a subscription business: the first charge needs a challenge, later renewals should generally not. How does this actually work end-to-end?senior

Answer. The initial subscription setup transaction is a genuine cardholder-initiated transaction (CIT) and needs full SCA — the customer is present, so 3DS runs normally, potentially with a challenge. Crucially, that initial authorization must be flagged/tagged as setting up a recurring/merchant-initiated series (Stripe's setup_future_usage or a dedicated MIT-setup flag, Adyen's recurringProcessingModel), which tells the issuer "expect future charges against this card with no cardholder present, tied to this authenticated setup." Subsequent renewal charges are then sent as merchant-initiated transactions (MITs) referencing that original CIT, which are exempt from SCA by design — there's no cardholder to challenge in a background billing job. If a renewal is nonetheless declined for a reason suggesting the issuer wants fresh authentication (some issuers do this opportunistically), you fall back to a cardholder-initiated retry flow that can trigger 3DS again.

Example

// initial setup: cardholder present, full SCA-capable flow
var intent = await service.CreateAsync(new PaymentIntentCreateOptions {
    SetupFutureUsage = "off_session", // marks this as establishing a future MIT series
    // ...
});
// later renewal: merchant-initiated, no cardholder present
var renewal = await service.CreateAsync(new PaymentIntentCreateOptions {
    Customer = customerId, PaymentMethod = savedMethodId,
    OffSession = true, Confirm = true, // MIT: SCA generally exempt
});

Follow-ups

  • What happens if the MIT renewal charge is declined for "authentication required"? You have to bring the cardholder back on-session (an email/in-app prompt to re-enter payment details or complete a fresh 3DS challenge) — this is a real part of dunning flow design, not a rare edge case.
  • Does a price change on renewal (a plan upgrade mid-cycle) affect this? Some issuers are stricter about MIT exemption when the amount varies from what was originally authorized — worth testing against your actual issuer mix rather than assuming exemption always holds.
  • Is the CIT/MIT distinction Stripe/Adyen-specific vocabulary or is it the actual EMVCo term? CIT/MIT is EMVCo/network-standard terminology; each PSP just exposes it through their own parameter names.

Common wrong answer: "Just save the card token after the first successful charge and reuse it for renewals like any saved payment method." Reusing a token without correctly flagging the original transaction as CIT-establishing-a-MIT-series can cause issuers to treat later renewals as fresh CITs needing authentication with no cardholder to authenticate — the flagging at setup time, not just the token itself, is what makes exemption-eligible renewals work.

A merchant complains that turning on 3DS tanked their checkout conversion rate. How do you diagnose and address this without just disabling 3DS?senior

Answer. First isolate whether the drop is from the *challenge* step specifically (customers abandoning at the OTP/app-approval screen) versus the 3DS *process* overall (added latency, a broken redirect, unsupported card types erroring out) — these need very different fixes. If it's genuinely challenge-rate driven, the lever is maximizing frictionless authentication: sending richer, higher-quality device/browsing data to raise the issuer's confidence, requesting exemptions where eligible (low-value, TRA) instead of always requesting full 3DS, and reviewing whether your own 3DS request parameters are inadvertently signalling higher risk than warranted. If it's a broken flow (certain card BINs failing 3DS entirely, mobile-web redirect UX being poor), that's an integration-quality bug, not an inherent 3DS cost, and fixable without touching authentication strength at all. Only after exhausting these would "reduce 3DS request rate for genuinely low-risk segments" be on the table — and that reintroduces the liability-shift trade-off explicitly, as a business decision, not a technical workaround.

Example

Diagnose: split funnel drop-off by 3DS outcome (frictionless-approved / challenge-passed / challenge-abandoned / hard-error)
-- 40% abandon at challenge screen -> investigate device-data richness, exemption eligibility
-- 15% hard-error on specific BIN range -> integration bug with that issuer's ACS, not a policy question

Follow-ups

  • Is there a way to see the challenge-abandonment rate specifically, not just overall conversion? Yes — most PSPs' dashboards break down 3DS outcomes (frictionless vs. challenged, passed vs. abandoned vs. failed) as a standard report; this should be the first place to look, not general conversion funnels.
  • Would routing more transactions through an exemption request always help conversion? Only within eligibility limits, and it moves fraud liability back onto the merchant — a legitimate lever, but a business risk trade, not a free win.
  • Could the checkout's own UX (not 3DS itself) be amplifying the drop? Very possibly — a full-page redirect for the challenge (older integration patterns) is much more abandon-prone than an inline modal/iframe challenge; upgrading the integration pattern itself is often the actual fix.

Common wrong answer: "Turn off 3DS entirely to recover the lost conversion." This throws away the liability shift for every transaction, trading a measured conversion cost for unbounded fraud-loss and chargeback liability exposure — a decision that should never be made without quantifying the fraud-cost side, which this answer skips entirely.

What actually changed in EMV 3-D Secure between 2.1/2.2 and the current 2.3.x line, and why would a merchant care about the version their PSP/ACS supports?senior

Answer. Successive EMV 3DS 2.x point releases have primarily added new message data fields (richer device/context signals to improve frictionless-authentication accuracy), extended support for new use cases (e.g. decoupled authentication, where the challenge happens on a separate channel/device than the one initiating the purchase — useful for smart-TV or voice-commerce style purchases with no natural screen for an OTP), and tightened specification bulletins addressing real-world interoperability issues found in the field. The current production spec, 2.3.1.1, reflects two specification bulletins (279/280, published August 2025) correcting issues discovered post-release. A merchant cares because the version an issuer's Access Control Server and their own PSP/3DS-server both support caps which use cases and which frictionless-improving data fields are actually available to them — requesting a 2.3-only feature against an ACS that only speaks 2.1 simply doesn't work, so integration version alignment directly affects achievable frictionless rates.

Example

3DS 2.1: baseline 2.x, core frictionless/challenge flow
3DS 2.2: added decoupled authentication, more risk data fields
3DS 2.3.x: spec bulletin corrections (279/280, 2025-08) refining message-field behavior
3DS 2.4.0.0 (draft, comment period closing 2026-07-01): next revision, not yet production

Follow-ups

  • Does a merchant need to do anything to "upgrade" their 3DS version? Usually not directly — version support is largely a PSP/3DS-server and issuer-ACS capability negotiated automatically per transaction; the merchant benefits passively as their PSP rolls out support for newer protocol versions.
  • What's decoupled authentication useful for concretely? Scenarios where the purchasing device can't reasonably present a challenge — a customer buying via a smart speaker or an unattended kiosk, authenticated instead through their phone's banking app asynchronously.
  • Should a candidate be expected to know the exact bulletin numbers? No — knowing that the spec evolves via numbered bulletins/point releases and that a 2.4 draft is in flight signals someone who tracks the ecosystem, which matters more than memorizing bulletin IDs.

Common wrong answer: "3DS2 is one static spec that hasn't meaningfully changed since it replaced 3DS1." Treating the 2.x line as frozen misses that it's an actively maintained, incrementally revised specification with real operational consequences (new fields, new use cases, bug-fix bulletins) — a senior candidate should at least know it's a moving target, even without memorizing every version delta.

How would you design a global checkout that serves both EU cardholders (SCA-mandated) and US cardholders (3DS optional, issuer-dependent) without either over-friction-ing US customers or under-protecting EU ones?principal

Answer. Don't hardcode a binary "3DS on/off" switch by merchant region — make the 3DS request decision per-transaction, driven by cardholder/issuer signals (BIN-range country lookup, or simply always attempting 3DS and letting frictionless absorb the low-risk cases) rather than merchant geography, since a US-issued card can be used by a traveler in the EU and vice versa, and EU regulation cares about the *issuer's* jurisdiction, not the merchant's storefront locale. The pragmatic default most PSPs converge on: always request 3DS ("3DS-preferred" mode) for every transaction and let the issuer's own risk engine decide frictionless-vs-challenge — US issuers overwhelmingly return frictionless approval for low-risk transactions (since SCA isn't mandated there, they rarely challenge), so the friction cost for US customers ends up close to zero in practice, while EU issuers correctly enforce SCA where required. This avoids building and maintaining fragile geo-based branching logic that has to track regulatory changes (like PSD3/PSR) in every jurisdiction you serve.

Example

Naive: if (issuerCountry in EU) requestChallenge=true else requestChallenge=false
Better: always requestThreeDSecure=true (let issuer's own ACS decide frictionless vs challenge per its own regulatory obligations)

Follow-ups

  • Does "always request 3DS" have any downside for US-heavy merchants? A small added latency/integration complexity for every transaction even when the issuer always frictionlessly approves — usually negligible next to the operational cost of maintaining geo-branching logic.
  • How does this interact with liability shift decisions for non-mandated markets? Merchants in non-SCA-mandated markets can still choose to always request 3DS purely for the liability shift benefit on fraud-prone transactions, independent of any regulatory requirement — a purely commercial decision.
  • What happens when PSD3/PSR changes SCA requirements (e.g. new exemption categories, or extending scope)? An issuer-driven, always-request design absorbs regulatory changes on the issuer's side automatically; a merchant-side geo-branching design would need a code change and redeploy every time the regulation shifts.

Common wrong answer: "Detect the customer's browser locale or shipping address and turn 3DS on only for EU addresses." Shipping/billing address and browser locale are weak, spoofable proxies for issuer jurisdiction, and this approach still requires ongoing maintenance as regulations change across markets — it optimizes for the wrong signal when the issuer's own ACS is already the authoritative decision-maker if you just ask it every time.

PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation (PSR) reached provisional political agreement in the EU in late November 2025. What's actually changing for SCA/3DS specifically, versus what's changing about payments regulation more broadly?principal

Answer. The SCA/3DS mechanics themselves aren't being replaced — PSD3/PSR builds on PSD2's authentication framework rather than tearing it up. The concrete new obligations that intersect payments engineering: mandatory payee-name verification before a transfer completes (confirming the account holder's name matches what the payer expects, closing a common social-engineering fraud vector, with roughly 24 months to implement after entry into force versus ~18 months for most other provisions), expanded and clarified fraud-prevention duties on payment service providers with corresponding reimbursement obligations for certain impersonation-fraud victims, and a structural simplification folding e-money institutions into the same payment-institution licensing category PSPs already sit in. As a regulation (PSR) rather than a directive, the payments-specific rules become directly applicable across member states without each country transposing it into national law separately, which is a meaningful change from PSD2's directive-based, country-by-country implementation variance. Since this is provisional agreement, not yet in force, OJ publication is projected around Q2 2026 with the actual compliance deadlines landing roughly 18-24 months after that — there's no immediate engineering action required today, but it's squarely on the "reads before finalizing this year's payments compliance roadmap" list.

Example

28 Jun 2023: PSD3/PSR proposed
27 Nov 2025: provisional political agreement reached
~Q2 2026 (projected): OJ publication, PSR enters into force
~18 months later: most provisions apply
~24 months later: payee-name verification specifically applies

Follow-ups

  • Does payee-name verification replace 3DS/SCA for a given transaction? No — it's an additional check specific to credit-transfer-style payments (bank transfers), distinct from the card-authentication flow 3DS covers.
  • Why does the directive-vs-regulation distinction matter operationally? A directive (PSD2) leaves implementation detail and timing to each member state, producing real cross-border inconsistency payments engineers have had to work around for years; a regulation (PSR) applies uniformly and directly, which should reduce that fragmentation once in force.
  • What should a payments engineering team do about this today, in September 2026? Track the OJ publication date once it lands and start scoping payee-name-verification work against the resulting deadline — there's no code to write yet, but the compliance roadmap should already have a placeholder milestone.

Common wrong answer: "PSD3 is already in force and requires new authentication flows we should be implementing now." As of today (September 2026) it's still provisional agreement, not enacted law with a live compliance deadline — claiming an immediate implementation requirement overstates where the legislative process actually is, and a principal-level candidate should be precise about that distinction rather than treating "agreed" as "in effect."

05 PCI DSS scope & tokenization

Every engineer who touches a checkout flow eventually has to reason about PCI DSS scope, even if a compliance team owns the actual assessment. The current standard is v4.0.1 (published 2024-06-11); its future-dated requirements became mandatory 2025-03-31 and are now simply "the standard," not a transition item. This unit checks whether scope-reduction is understood as an architecture decision, not paperwork.

Docs: PCI SSC document library · Stripe security & PCI

What is PCI DSS actually trying to protect, and what triggers a system being "in scope" for it?screener

Answer. PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) protects cardholder data — primarily the PAN (the card number itself), plus sensitive authentication data like the full magnetic-stripe/chip track data and CVV, which additionally can never be stored post-authorization at all under any circumstance. A system is in scope if it stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data, or if it's connected to/could impact the security of a system that does — scope isn't limited to the one server that touches a PAN; it extends to anything on the same network segment that isn't properly isolated from it. This is why network segmentation is one of the most powerful scope-reduction tools available: isolating the systems that actually handle card data shrinks the boundary of what needs the full DSS assessment.

Example

In scope:      checkout server receiving raw PAN, its DB, its logs, anything on its flat network segment
Reduced scope: same checkout server, but PAN never lands there (client-side tokenization) + segmented network

Follow-ups

  • Can CVV ever be stored, even encrypted, after authorization? No — this is one of the few absolute prohibitions in the standard, regardless of encryption or access controls.
  • Does PCI DSS apply only to merchants? No — it applies to any entity that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data, which includes PSPs, gateways, and service providers themselves.
  • Is "in scope" binary, or a matter of degree? Functionally binary for the assessment itself, but the *volume* of requirements you must satisfy scales with how much of the data flow and how many systems fall inside that boundary — which is exactly what SAQ types (next question) capture.

Common wrong answer: "PCI compliance is the payment provider's problem, not ours, since they process the card." Any part of your own stack that touches, stores, or is network-adjacent to cardholder data is in scope regardless of who ultimately processes the payment — using a PSP reduces scope, it doesn't eliminate your own compliance obligation entirely.

What are SAQ A, SAQ A-EP, and SAQ D, and what determines which one a merchant has to complete?screener

Answer. Self-Assessment Questionnaires are tiered by how much of the cardholder-data path a merchant's own systems touch. SAQ A is the lightest tier: card data is fully outsourced to a redirect or an iframe/SDK the PSP controls end-to-end (Stripe Elements, Adyen Components, hosted checkout) — the merchant's server never receives PAN, so the requirement list is short. SAQ A-EP sits between: the merchant's page hosts the payment iframe itself (rather than a full redirect) and thus has some responsibility for the page's own integrity and script security, even though the card data still flows directly to the PSP's iframe, not through the merchant's server. SAQ D is the heavy tier: any merchant whose systems directly store, process, or transmit PAN (server-side card handling, or storing card data at all) must complete the full, long questionnaire covering the entire DSS requirement set.

Example

SAQ A:    hosted checkout / fully-hosted iframe SDK, no server-side card touch -> ~20 requirements
SAQ A-EP: merchant page embeds the payment iframe itself, but PAN still flows direct to PSP -> ~130 requirements
SAQ D:    server-side card handling / storage -> ~300+ requirements, the full standard

Follow-ups

  • Why does SAQ A-EP have so many more requirements than SAQ A if the PAN never touches the server either way? Because the merchant's own page is now part of the security perimeter that could be compromised to intercept or redirect that iframe (a script-injection or dependency-compromise attack), so subresource integrity, vulnerability scanning, and web-app security requirements apply that a fully-redirected SAQ A merchant doesn't need.
  • Does using Stripe.js/Elements always guarantee SAQ A? Typically yes if implemented correctly (card fields genuinely isolated in the PSP's iframe with no server-side card handling), but the merchant still has to actually verify their implementation matches the assumptions, not just assume the SDK's existence is sufficient.
  • Who determines which SAQ type applies — the merchant, the PSP, or the acquirer? The acquirer/card networks ultimately require a specific SAQ type based on the merchant's validated payment channel and volume tier, though the merchant self-selects the SAQ matching their actual data flow as a starting point.

Common wrong answer: "We use Stripe, so we're automatically PCI compliant and don't need to do anything." Using a compliant PSP reduces your scope and requirement count, but you still have to complete the applicable SAQ, implement the specific requirements it lists (TLS config, dependency patching, access controls on whatever infrastructure remains in scope), and attest to it — there's no such thing as automatic compliance by association.

What's the difference between tokenization and encryption of card data, and why does a PSP-issued token typically get you further out of PCI scope than encrypting the PAN yourself?mid

Answer. Encryption is reversible by design given the right key — an encrypted PAN is still, structurally, the PAN, and if your systems hold both ciphertext and any path to the decryption key, that combination is still in PCI scope, since a compromise of the key recovers real cardholder data. Tokenization replaces the PAN with a surrogate value (a token) that has no mathematical relationship to the original number and cannot be reversed without access to the tokenization vault that issued it — a token stored in your database is, by itself, useless to an attacker and generally not considered cardholder data at all for scope purposes, precisely because reversing it requires compromising the PSP's vault, not anything in your possession. This is why PSP-hosted tokenization (Stripe's PaymentMethod ID, a network token) gets you further out of scope than "we encrypt the PAN with AES before storing it" — the latter still leaves you holding cardholder data, just encrypted.

Example

Encryption: PAN "4242...4242" --AES+key--> ciphertext (reversible, still cardholder data if key is reachable)
Tokenization: PAN "4242...4242" --PSP vault--> "pm_1Nx7f2..." (opaque, meaningless outside PSP's vault)

Follow-ups

  • Is a "network token" (from Visa/Mastercard's own tokenization service) different from a PSP's own token? Yes — network tokens are portable across PSPs/merchants under the same network scheme and survive card reissuance (a lost/stolen card's replacement number updates transparently), whereas a PSP's own token is generally vendor-locked to that PSP's vault.
  • Can you build your own tokenization vault instead of using a PSP's? Yes, but the vault itself then becomes the system that touches raw PAN and is squarely in the heaviest PCI scope — you've relocated the problem, not eliminated it, and building this correctly is a significant undertaking most companies shouldn't take on themselves.
  • Does storing a token mean you can never get the original card details back? Correct, by design, from your side — only the tokenizing vault (the PSP) can resolve a token back to usable card details, and only for its own authorized operations (like using it to charge again).

Common wrong answer: "We encrypt card numbers before storing them, so we're not really storing cardholder data." Encrypted PAN is still cardholder data under PCI DSS as long as decryption is feasible for anyone with appropriate access — the standard has specific, extensive requirements for exactly this case (key management, split knowledge, dual control) precisely because "we encrypted it" is not an exemption.

A startup wants to store raw card numbers themselves "to build a better checkout experience" instead of using tokenization. What would you tell them, and is there ever a legitimate reason to do this?mid

Answer. Nearly always, don't — the "better checkout experience" argument almost never survives contact with what SAQ D actually requires: network segmentation, quarterly vulnerability scans, annual penetration testing, formal key-management procedures, and (above a transaction-volume threshold) an on-site QSA audit rather than a self-assessment, all of which cost far more in ongoing engineering and compliance overhead than the marginal UX gain of, say, skipping a tokenized-field round trip. Tokenized flows (Elements/Components) can already deliver a fully custom-styled, single-page checkout with no redirect — the UX ceiling tokenization imposes is much higher than people assume going in. The legitimate exceptions are narrow: a company that *is* the payments infrastructure (a PSP, an acquirer processor) has to touch raw PAN somewhere in its stack by definition, and a very small number of specialized use cases (certain card-issuing/BIN-sponsorship products) genuinely require it — but "we want a nicer checkout" is essentially never a valid reason.

Example

"Better checkout" via raw PAN storage: SAQ D, QSA audit, segmentation, key management, ongoing audit cost
"Better checkout" via Elements/Components: SAQ A/A-EP, still fully custom-styled, no server-side card handling

Follow-ups

  • What if the business case is "we want to let customers re-use a card across multiple unrelated merchant accounts we operate"? A network token or a PSP's cross-account tokenization feature (several PSPs support platform-level saved cards reusable across connected accounts) usually solves this without needing raw PAN storage.
  • Is there a cost difference in processing fees between tokenized and raw-PAN flows? Not meaningfully — interchange/processing fees are driven by card type, transaction risk, and channel (CP/CNP), not by whether tokenization was used to reach the PSP.
  • How would you push back on this in a design review without just saying "compliance says no"? Quantify the actual engineering/audit cost of SAQ D against the UX gain, and demonstrate the equivalent UX is achievable with tokenized fields — a concrete cost comparison persuades better than a policy citation.

Common wrong answer: "It's fine as long as we get a QSA to sign off once a year." Passing an annual audit doesn't retroactively make the ongoing operational and breach-liability cost of holding raw PAN cheap or low-risk — the audit is a compliance gate, not a substitute for the actual security and cost trade-off being evaluated here.

PCI DSS v4.0's "future-dated requirements" became mandatory on 2025-03-31. What kind of requirement was in that bucket, and why give a full year-plus transition window at all?senior

Answer. V4.0 (published 2022, superseded by the 4.0.1 clarification-only update in June 2024) introduced a set of new or substantially strengthened requirements — things like expanded multi-factor authentication coverage, more rigorous password/passphrase strength, targeted risk analyses for certain controls, and stronger e-commerce/phishing-related script and payment-page-integrity monitoring for scripts loaded in the payment page (a direct response to real-world web-skimming/Magecart-style attacks). These were marked "future-dated" rather than immediately mandatory because they require real infrastructure and process changes — new authentication systems, new monitoring tooling, new documented risk-analysis procedures — that organizations of every size across a global card ecosystem couldn't reasonably stand up overnight. The transition window (v4.0 effective 2024-03-31 for baseline requirements, future-dated ones mandatory a year later on 2025-03-31) gave assessed entities a real runway; as of today, that runway has closed and they're simply part of the standard.

Example

2022:        PCI DSS v4.0 published
2024-03-31:  v4.0 becomes the active standard (v3.2.1 retired); baseline new requirements mandatory
2024-06-11:  v4.0.1 published (clarifications, no new requirements)
2025-03-31:  future-dated new requirements (MFA expansion, page-integrity monitoring, etc.) become mandatory
2026-09-08 (today): fully in force, no transition period remaining

Follow-ups

  • What's a concrete example of a "future-dated" requirement relevant to a typical SAQ A-EP e-commerce team? Payment-page script/content monitoring — detecting and alerting on unauthorized changes to scripts loaded on the checkout page, directly targeting the Magecart-style skimming attack pattern where a compromised third-party script exfiltrates card data client-side.
  • Is v4.0.1 a new version with new requirements, or something else? A clarification-only update to v4.0 — wording fixes and clarity improvements, not new controls; anyone compliant with v4.0's actual requirements is functionally aligned with v4.0.1.
  • What happens if an organization still isn't compliant with the now-mandatory requirements today? They're out of compliance with the standard their acquirer/card brands require — consequences range from fines to increased transaction fees to, in serious/repeated cases, loss of card-acceptance privileges, enforced by the acquirer rather than PCI SSC directly.

Common wrong answer: "PCI DSS v4.0.1 is the newest version and it's still being phased in." As of today, 2026-09-08, the phase-in period ended over a year ago (2025-03-31) — describing the future-dated requirements as still transitioning is out of date and would be a real gap for a senior candidate expected to track compliance timelines relevant to their own systems.

You inherit a legacy system where card numbers are stored in plaintext in an old orders table that nobody's touched in years, alongside a modern tokenized checkout for new orders. How do you remediate this safely?senior

Answer. Treat this as an active incident-adjacent risk, not routine tech debt — every day that data sits there is exposure with no compensating control most likely, and the remediation plan needs urgency proportional to that. First, stop the bleeding: confirm no active code path still writes plaintext PAN (audit and fix the write path immediately, even before touching historical data). Then assess whether the historical data is even needed: most of it likely serves no ongoing business purpose (old orders don't need a live, chargeable card number) and can simply be deleted or replaced with a truncated/masked reference (last 4 digits, which is explicitly allowed) sufficient for support/lookup purposes. Where a genuine ongoing need exists (a subscription still billing off that old data), migrate those specific records through the PSP's card-import/tokenization API to get a proper token, then delete the plaintext source once migration is verified. Document the whole exercise, since a QSA/assessor will want evidence discovery, remediation timeline, and verification of complete removal, not just a "we fixed it, trust us."

Example

1. Audit: confirm no live write path still stores plaintext PAN (fix first, even before touching history)
2. Classify old rows: needed for active billing? vs. dead/historical-only?
3. Dead rows -> truncate to last4 or delete entirely
4. Active-billing rows -> migrate via PSP's tokenization/card-import API, verify, THEN delete plaintext
5. Document discovery + remediation for the next assessment

Follow-ups

  • Is this something you'd disclose, and to whom? Depends on scope/jurisdiction and whether there's evidence of actual compromise (not just non-compliant storage) — this is exactly the point to loop in compliance/legal rather than deciding unilaterally as an engineer, since disclosure obligations are a legal, not purely technical, question.
  • Can you keep the last 4 digits of a card number without it being in PCI scope? Yes — truncated PAN (showing only the first six and/or last four digits, per the standard's masking rules) is explicitly permitted and commonly used for customer-facing "card ending in 4242" displays and support lookups.
  • What's the risk of just leaving it and "getting to it eventually"? Beyond the compliance exposure, it's a live breach-impact multiplier — if any adjacent system is ever compromised, this table turns a contained incident into a full cardholder-data breach with mandatory notification obligations.

Common wrong answer: "Just add access controls and encrypt the existing table in place, leave the data where it is." This treats the symptom (access control) rather than the actual goal (get raw PAN out of your possession) — encrypting data you don't need to keep at all is strictly worse than simply removing/tokenizing it, since encrypted-but-present cardholder data is still full PCI scope and still a breach liability if the key is ever exposed.

Design the smallest possible PCI-scope footprint for a marketplace platform that needs to both accept payments from buyers and eventually pay out to third-party sellers, where sellers' own card/bank details also need to be on file.principal

Answer. Separate the two data flows deliberately, since they have different scope profiles. Buyer-side: route entirely through a hosted/tokenized checkout (Elements/Components or full redirect) so the platform itself only ever holds PSP tokens for buyers — SAQ A/A-EP territory, no raw PAN in the platform's own systems at any point. Seller payout details are a different animal: sellers typically provide *bank account* details (for ACH/SEPA payout) rather than card numbers, which fall under different regulatory regimes (banking KYC, not PCI DSS, since PCI DSS specifically covers card data) — using a PSP's built-in marketplace/platform product (Stripe Connect, Adyen for Platforms) offloads seller onboarding, identity verification, and payout-instrument storage to the PSP's own compliance boundary entirely, so the platform never directly holds seller bank credentials either. The resulting architecture: the platform's own infrastructure never stores raw PAN or raw bank account numbers for either side — only references (tokens, connected-account IDs) to data the PSP holds under its own compliance scope, keeping the platform itself in the lightest applicable tier for both flows.

Example

Buyer flow:  buyer -> tokenized checkout (Elements) -> platform holds PSP token only -> SAQ A/A-EP
Seller flow: seller -> PSP's platform onboarding (Connect/for Platforms) -> platform holds connected-account ID only
                                                                          -> seller bank details live in PSP's own compliance boundary

Follow-ups

  • Does this fully remove the platform from any compliance obligation at all? No — the platform still has its own SAQ to complete (however light) and separate obligations under KYC/AML for the seller-onboarding side, which platform products help satisfy but don't eliminate the platform's responsibility for.
  • What if the platform wants to show sellers their own masked bank details in a dashboard? The PSP's platform product typically exposes a masked/last-4 view via API specifically for this, so the dashboard never needs the platform to hold or even see the full account number.
  • Why not just build seller payout infrastructure in-house for more control? That reintroduces full KYC/AML, sanctions-screening, and banking-relationship obligations the platform would otherwise offload — a legitimate choice only at a scale/control requirement that justifies owning a genuinely regulated function, mirroring the earlier build-vs-orchestration trade-off from unit 01.

Common wrong answer: "Use tokenization for the buyer side, and just store seller bank account numbers directly since PCI DSS doesn't cover bank accounts." Technically true that PCI DSS's scope is card data specifically, but this answer stops at the letter of one standard and misses that bank-account/payout data carries its own serious regulatory and security obligations (KYC, fraud, breach-notification law) that a platform-product PSP is generally far better positioned to carry than a marketplace re-inventing seller-payout infrastructure from scratch.

06 Refunds, disputes & chargebacks

Refunds are the easy half of this unit; chargebacks are where candidates who've only ever built the "happy path" checkout fall apart. Expect screeners on basic refund mechanics and terminology, and mid/senior questions on representment strategy, reason codes, and designing systems that keep evidence ready before a dispute ever lands.

Docs: Stripe disputes · Adyen disputes

What's the difference between a refund and a chargeback, and who initiates each?screener

Answer. A refund is voluntary: the merchant (or a system acting on the merchant's behalf) decides to return funds to the customer, typically through the same PSP API used to charge, and it's a cooperative, low-friction process. A chargeback is adversarial by comparison: the cardholder disputes a transaction directly with their *issuer*, the issuer reverses the funds unilaterally (often before the merchant even knows a dispute exists) and provisionally debits the merchant's account, and the merchant must actively contest it (representment) if they believe the dispute is invalid — the burden of proof effectively sits with the merchant to justify why the charge should stand. Chargebacks also carry real cost beyond the disputed amount itself: most PSPs/acquirers charge a dispute fee regardless of outcome, and a merchant's chargeback *rate* (disputes as a percentage of transaction volume) is monitored by the card networks, with excessive rates triggering monitoring programs or even account termination.

Example

Refund:     merchant -> PSP API -> funds returned, cooperative, no dispute fee, merchant controls timing
Chargeback: cardholder -> issuer -> funds reversed unilaterally -> merchant notified after the fact, must contest via evidence

Follow-ups

  • Can a merchant refund a transaction that's already been charged back? Generally no in the sense that matters — once a chargeback is opened, the dispute process supersedes a simple refund; you respond via representment or accept the loss, rather than issuing a refund on top of a reversal.
  • Does issuing a refund quickly prevent a chargeback? Often yes in practice — if a customer's underlying complaint is resolved by a prompt refund before they escalate to their bank, no dispute is opened at all, which is why fast, generous refund policies double as chargeback-prevention.
  • Is a chargeback the same as "friendly fraud"? No — friendly fraud is a specific *category* of illegitimate chargeback (a cardholder disputing a transaction they actually authorized, often just not recognizing the charge or wanting a refund without going through the merchant), whereas chargebacks broadly also include genuine fraud and legitimate service disputes.

Common wrong answer: "A chargeback is just a refund the bank processes instead of us." This misses the adversarial structure entirely — a chargeback is unilateral, carries fees, damages a merchant's standing with the card networks, and requires active evidence-based contestation, none of which apply to a normal refund.

What's a dispute "reason code," and why does it matter which one a chargeback comes in under?screener

Answer. Each card network defines a standardized set of reason codes categorizing why a chargeback was filed — common buckets include fraud (cardholder claims they didn't authorize the transaction), product/service not received, product not as described, duplicate processing, and credit not processed (a promised refund never appeared). The reason code matters because it dictates exactly what evidence is relevant and persuasive in representment: a "product not received" dispute is won or lost on delivery/tracking proof, while a "not as described" dispute needs proof of what was actually promised (listing, terms) versus delivered — submitting delivery tracking to contest a fraud claim, or vice versa, wastes the response and nearly guarantees losing regardless of the underlying facts.

Example

Reason: "Product not received"       -> Evidence: shipping/tracking confirmation, delivery signature
Reason: "Fraudulent - card not authorized" -> Evidence: 3DS authentication result, device/IP match, AVS/CVV match, order history
Reason: "Credit not processed"        -> Evidence: proof a refund was actually issued (ID, timestamp, amount)

Follow-ups

  • Are reason codes standardized across Visa, Mastercard, Amex? Each network maintains its own code set with roughly analogous categories, so a PSP typically normalizes them into a smaller set of network-agnostic categories for merchant-facing reporting.
  • Can a reason code be wrong/mislabeled by the issuer? It happens, and part of a strong representment strategy includes recognizing when the stated reason doesn't match the actual dispute pattern (e.g. a "fraud" code on what's clearly a legitimate, 3DS-authenticated repeat customer) and addressing that mismatch directly in the response.
  • Does the reason code affect the dispute fee? Not typically — fees are usually flat per-dispute regardless of category, though some PSPs waive fees for certain outcomes or on the first dispute in a period.

Common wrong answer: "Just submit all available evidence (receipts, shipping, correspondence) for every dispute regardless of the code." Evidence packages have length/relevance limits reviewers actually read, and irrelevant evidence for the stated reason code dilutes the persuasive material that matters — a targeted, reason-code-specific response measurably outperforms a kitchen-sink one.

Walk through what "representment" actually is and roughly how the timeline works after a chargeback lands.mid

Answer. Representment is the formal process of contesting a chargeback: the merchant submits evidence to the acquirer, who forwards it to the issuer, arguing the original transaction was legitimate and the dispute should be reversed back in the merchant's favor. The clock starts the moment the dispute notification arrives, with a response window that's tight and network-dependent (commonly on the order of 7-21 days depending on network and reason code) — missing it is an automatic loss regardless of evidence quality. If representment succeeds, funds and the dispute fee status are reversed back to the merchant; if the issuer isn't persuaded, some networks allow a second round (pre-arbitration/arbitration) at additional cost and with diminishing odds, while others treat the first representment decision as final. Because the window is short and evidence often needs to be assembled from multiple systems (order records, shipping data, communication logs, authentication results), the practical bottleneck is almost always internal evidence-gathering speed, not the representment submission itself.

Example

Day 0:  chargeback notification received, funds already provisionally reversed
Day 0-N: gather evidence (order record, shipping proof, 3DS result, correspondence)
Day N (deadline, network-dependent): submit representment via PSP/acquirer
Day N+~30-45: issuer decision -> won (funds restored) or lost (final, or eligible for arbitration on some networks)

Follow-ups

  • Is the response deadline the same across all card networks? No — it varies by network and sometimes by reason code, which is why a dispute-management system needs to track the actual deadline per case rather than assuming a single fixed number.
  • Who actually decides the representment outcome — the PSP, the acquirer, or the issuer? The issuer makes the final call; the PSP and acquirer are conduits forwarding evidence and returning the decision, though a good PSP's dispute tooling materially affects how well-formatted and complete your submission is.
  • Is it ever rational to not contest a chargeback even if you believe it's illegitimate? Yes — for very small transaction amounts, the engineering/ops time to assemble a strong response can exceed the disputed amount, making "accept the loss" the economically rational choice, a genuine judgment call covered later in this bank.

Common wrong answer: "You have a month or two to respond to any chargeback." Response windows are considerably tighter and network/reason-code dependent — treating this as a leisurely process is exactly how legitimate, winnable disputes get lost to a missed deadline rather than weak evidence.

Design a system that makes representment fast and high-quality by default, rather than a scramble every time a dispute notification lands.senior

Answer. The core idea is to capture dispute-relevant evidence *at transaction time*, before any dispute exists, rather than trying to reconstruct it retroactively under a tight deadline. Concretely: persist the full authentication context of every transaction (3DS result/ECI value, AVS/CVV match results, device fingerprint, IP), snapshot the order contents and any customer-facing terms shown at checkout time (not the current version of your terms page, which may have since changed), and log delivery/fulfillment confirmation as it happens rather than deriving it later from a shipping provider's possibly-purged records. When a dispute notification arrives, an automated first pass assembles the relevant evidence bundle for that specific reason code from already-persisted data, needing human review only for genuinely ambiguous or high-value cases rather than for evidence-gathering itself. This turns representment from a scramble into a mostly-automated, reason-code-driven lookup.

Example

At checkout time (persist, don't reconstruct later):
  - 3DS/SCA result + ECI value
  - AVS/CVV match results
  - snapshot of order contents + terms-as-shown
At fulfillment time:
  - delivery confirmation / tracking, logged as it happens
At dispute time:
  - auto-assemble evidence bundle keyed by reason code from already-persisted data
  - human review only for ambiguous/high-value cases

Follow-ups

  • How long should this evidence be retained? At least as long as the maximum chargeback window a card network allows (which can be considerably longer than the initial representment deadline — some categories permit disputes many months after the transaction), plus the arbitration window if pursuing that path.
  • Does this evidence-capture design have PCI or privacy implications? Yes — device fingerprints and IP data are personal data under privacy regulation (GDPR etc.), so retention policy needs a legitimate-interest/legal basis and a defined deletion schedule, not indefinite storage.
  • What's the highest-leverage single piece of evidence to automate capture for? Usually the 3DS/SCA authentication result — it directly determines liability-shift eligibility for a huge share of fraud-category disputes and is often the single deciding factor in those cases.

Common wrong answer: "Build a good ticketing workflow so customer support can quickly gather evidence when a dispute comes in." A fast ticketing workflow still depends on the underlying evidence existing and being retrievable — if 3DS results, order snapshots, and delivery confirmations weren't captured at the time, no amount of workflow speed recovers information that was never persisted.

Your dashboard shows a chargeback rate climbing toward a card network's monitoring threshold. What's actually at stake, and how do you investigate root cause?senior

Answer. Card networks run merchant-monitoring programs (Visa's and Mastercard's dispute-monitoring programs, under various names) that flag merchants whose chargeback-to-transaction ratio crosses a threshold (commonly discussed around the 0.65%-1%+ range depending on program tier, though exact figures and definitions are network- and program-specific and worth verifying against current published thresholds rather than assuming a fixed number); crossing it can trigger increased fees, mandatory remediation plans, or in sustained/severe cases, loss of card-acceptance ability entirely — a genuinely existential risk for a payments-dependent business. Root-cause investigation means segmenting disputes by reason code and looking for a concentrated pattern rather than treating "chargebacks are up" as one undifferentiated problem: a spike in "fraud" reason codes points to a fraud-detection gap (worth cross-referencing with unit 10); a spike in "product not received"/"not as described" points to a fulfillment or listing-accuracy problem entirely outside payments engineering; a spike in "credit not processed" points to a broken refund process. The fix lives wherever the concentrated pattern actually points, which is very often not a payments-team problem at all.

Example

Segment disputes by reason code over the trailing window:
  Fraud: 15% of disputes  -> check fraud-scoring/3DS coverage gaps
  Not received: 55%       -> fulfillment/shipping-provider issue, not a payments bug
  Not as described: 20%   -> listing accuracy / customer expectation mismatch
  Credit not processed: 10% -> refund pipeline bug (should be near zero if refunds work correctly)

Follow-ups

  • Should you always fight every winnable dispute to keep the rate down? Not necessarily — some networks count disputes toward the monitoring ratio regardless of representment outcome, in which case *prevention* (fixing the underlying cause) reduces the rate more than winning contested disputes does.
  • Does a high refund rate also count against you the same way? No — refunds and chargebacks are tracked separately, and a healthy voluntary-refund rate that pre-empts disputes is generally viewed favorably, not penalized the way chargebacks are.
  • Who typically owns fixing a "not as described" spike — payments engineering or someone else? Someone else, almost always — product/listings/customer support; the payments team's role here is usually limited to surfacing the pattern clearly through good dispute analytics, not fixing the root cause directly.

Common wrong answer: "Just make our refund policy stricter so fewer disputes are filed in the first place." Tightening refunds usually *increases* chargebacks, not decreases them — a customer denied a reasonable refund through normal channels goes to their bank instead, converting what would've been a cheap voluntary refund into an expensive, network-visible chargeback.

What is "friendly fraud" and why is it one of the hardest categories of dispute to actually solve with engineering alone?senior

Answer. Friendly fraud is a chargeback filed by the legitimate cardholder against a transaction they did, in fact, authorize — they simply don't recognize the charge on their statement, forgot they made a purchase, want a refund faster than the merchant's stated policy allows, or in the more cynical/deliberate cases, are knowingly exploiting the dispute process as a free-refund shortcut. It's hard to solve technically because the transaction data itself often looks completely legitimate — correct 3DS authentication, matching AVS/CVV, a real delivered order — since nothing about the *authorization* was fraudulent; the problem is entirely in the cardholder's dispute behavior after the fact, which no amount of stronger authentication at checkout time prevents. Mitigations are mostly non-technical or only partially technical: clear, recognizable billing descriptors (so "XYZ Corp" on a statement doesn't look unfamiliar), proactive order confirmation and shipping-notification emails that jog memory before a dispute is even considered, and for repeat/pattern offenders, a customer-level risk flag informing (not necessarily blocking) future transactions.

Example

Legitimate fraud:  cardholder never authorized -> stolen card/credentials -> caught by fraud scoring/3DS
Friendly fraud:    cardholder DID authorize -> disputes anyway (forgot / impatient / exploiting process)
                   -> looks identical to a clean transaction in the auth data itself

Follow-ups

  • Does 3DS liability shift protect against friendly fraud the same way it does against stolen-card fraud? Only partially — some networks' friendly-fraud-specific chargeback categories still permit disputes even over a 3DS-authenticated transaction in certain circumstances, though a clean authentication record is still your single strongest piece of representment evidence.
  • Is Visa's Compelling Evidence framework relevant here? Conceptually yes — frameworks of this kind exist specifically to give merchants a stronger evidentiary path against exactly this category (repeat legitimate usage patterns as evidence the disputed transaction was consistent with genuine account activity), though a candidate should verify current specifics against the network's live documentation rather than quoting exact rules from memory, since these evidentiary frameworks are actively revised.
  • What's a non-technical lever that measurably reduces friendly fraud? Clear, brand-recognizable statement descriptors are consistently cited as one of the highest-leverage, lowest-effort reductions — a huge share of "I don't recognize this charge" disputes trace back to a cryptic descriptor, not actual fraud or forgetfulness.

Common wrong answer: "Add stronger fraud detection and 3DS to reduce friendly fraud." Friendly fraud isn't a fraud-detection problem — the transaction genuinely was authorized by the genuine cardholder; stronger authentication at checkout time addresses account-takeover and stolen-card fraud, not a legitimate cardholder disputing their own purchase after the fact.

A partial refund is issued on an order, and three weeks later the same order is charged back for the full original amount. How should your ledger and dispute-response logic handle this?principal

Answer. The ledger needs to already reflect that a partial refund reduced the merchant's actual exposure on that order, and the chargeback amount received from the issuer must be reconciled against the *remaining* captured amount, not the original full transaction amount — if the chargeback notification's stated amount doesn't match your own records (some networks charge back the originally authorized amount even after a partial refund, requiring you to net it out yourself), your representment response needs to explicitly present the partial-refund evidence to argue the disputed exposure is smaller than the notification implies, or in some cases that the entire remaining balance dispute is invalid because the refund already addressed the underlying complaint. This requires the ledger to model refunds and disputes as related-but-distinct entries against the same originating payment, not as independent transactions, so any query for "what's actually at stake for order X" correctly nets partial refunds against a dispute amount rather than double-counting the loss.

Example

Original charge:      $100.00
Partial refund:        -$30.00  (already returned to customer, ledger reflects $70 net exposure)
Chargeback filed for:  $100.00 (issuer's notification, gross original amount)
Correct representment: dispute the $100 notification, present the $30 refund as evidence remaining exposure is $70,
                        not $100 -- avoids the merchant effectively losing $130 total (refund + full chargeback)

Follow-ups

  • Why would an issuer charge back the full original amount instead of just the un-refunded remainder? Some network/issuer processes trigger off the original authorization record and aren't automatically aware of a subsequent partial refund unless the merchant's response surfaces it — it's on the merchant to make that connection explicit in representment.
  • Should the ledger auto-flag this "double jeopardy" risk before a human even looks at the dispute? Yes — this is exactly the kind of check that should run automatically the moment a dispute notification is ingested (cross-reference against refund history for the same payment ID) rather than relying on a reviewer to remember to check.
  • Does this change how you'd design the "amount" field on a dispute-tracking model? Yes — store both the network-reported dispute amount and a computed "net exposure after prior refunds" field, since reporting/representment logic needs the latter but reconciliation against the issuer's own numbers needs the former preserved unmodified.

Common wrong answer: "Just pay out or accept whatever amount the chargeback notification states, since that's what the issuer says is owed." Blindly accepting the issuer's stated amount without netting against your own refund history can mean paying twice for the same underlying complaint — once via the voluntary refund, again via an unadjusted full chargeback — a real, avoidable loss a well-designed ledger and representment process should catch automatically.

Design the dispute-handling architecture for a platform processing payments on behalf of many sub-merchants (a marketplace or platform-as-payment-facilitator model). Who's actually liable for a given chargeback, and how does that shape the system?principal

Answer. In a platform/marketplace model (Stripe Connect, Adyen for Platforms), the platform itself is typically the merchant of record with the acquirer, meaning chargeback notifications land on the platform's account first, even though the underlying transaction and the goods/service came from a connected sub-merchant — the platform has to decide, contractually and technically, how liability flows down. The common pattern: the platform's terms of service with sub-merchants specify that disputes are the sub-merchant's financial responsibility (their payout is debited or held to cover the disputed amount), while the platform retains operational responsibility for actually receiving the notification, enforcing the response deadline, and providing tooling for the sub-merchant to supply evidence, since the sub-merchant often has no direct dispute-management relationship with the acquirer at all. The system therefore needs: a routing layer that maps each incoming dispute notification to the correct sub-merchant account, a reserve/holdback mechanism (withholding a percentage of a sub-merchant's payouts specifically to cover future dispute liability, especially for higher-risk sub-merchant categories), and an evidence-submission UI/API surfaced to sub-merchants with the platform enforcing the actual network deadline on their behalf.

Example

Chargeback notification -> lands on PLATFORM's acquirer account (platform = merchant of record)
                         -> routing layer maps to sub-merchant (connected account) by original payment ID
                         -> sub-merchant's payout reserve debited for disputed amount
                         -> sub-merchant submits evidence via platform's tooling; platform relays to acquirer before deadline

Follow-ups

  • What happens if a sub-merchant's reserve balance is insufficient to cover a chargeback? The platform is still liable to the acquirer regardless, so it needs its own risk model for negative-balance/insufficient-reserve sub-merchants — often addressed by requiring a minimum reserve percentage upfront, tiered by the sub-merchant's risk category, or by the platform itself absorbing losses beyond the reserve as a cost of doing business in that segment.
  • Does the sub-merchant ever deal with the acquirer or issuer directly? Generally no in a payment-facilitator model — that's precisely the abstraction the platform is selling; all dispute communication flows through the platform, which is also why the platform's own dispute-response quality directly affects every sub-merchant's outcomes.
  • How does this interact with the platform's own chargeback-rate monitoring exposure from unit 06's earlier question? The platform's *aggregate* rate across all sub-merchants is what the card networks monitor, meaning a handful of high-dispute sub-merchants can push the entire platform toward a monitoring threshold — which is why platforms actively risk-score and sometimes offboard chronically high-dispute sub-merchants.

Common wrong answer: "Since the sub-merchant made the sale, the chargeback goes directly to them and the platform isn't involved." In a payment-facilitator model the platform is the merchant of record and is the party actually liable to the acquirer — sub-merchant liability is a downstream contractual arrangement the platform has to build and enforce itself, not something that happens automatically at the network level.

07 Ledgers, double-entry & reconciliation

Once money is actually moving, someone has to be able to answer "where is it, exactly, right now, and can you prove that." This is the unit that separates candidates who've only called a charge API from candidates who've owned the record-keeping behind it — double-entry design, settlement reconciliation, and handling the PSP's numbers disagreeing with your own.

Docs: Stripe reconciliation · Double-entry pattern overview

Why do payments systems use double-entry ledgers instead of just a single "balance" column that gets incremented/decremented?screener

Answer. A single mutable balance column can only tell you the current number — it can't tell you how it got there, and any bug or race condition that corrupts it leaves no trail to detect or repair the corruption from. Double-entry bookkeeping records every movement as two balanced entries (a debit and a credit of equal magnitude, across two different accounts) so the ledger is self-verifying: at any point, the sum of all debits must equal the sum of all credits, and if it doesn't, you know immediately something is wrong, and roughly where. This gives you an immutable, appendable audit trail (never update or delete a posted entry; correct mistakes with an offsetting reversal entry) that's exactly what a support/compliance/dispute investigation needs months later — "what happened to this specific dollar" is answerable by reading history, not by trusting a mutable current-state number.

Example

Single balance:  UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 100 WHERE id = 'merchant_1';
                 -- no record of WHY, no way to detect corruption, no audit trail

Double-entry:    DEBIT  merchant_1_payable   100
                 CREDIT customer_1_receivable 100
                 -- self-balancing, immutable, fully explains the movement

Follow-ups

  • Does double-entry design cost you anything (performance, complexity) compared to a simple balance column? Yes — more storage, more write volume (two rows per movement minimum), and query complexity for "what's my current balance" (usually a materialized/cached sum rather than a live full-history scan) — a real trade-off, not a free upgrade.
  • Is double-entry only relevant at large scale? No — the audit-trail and self-verification benefits matter from day one for any system moving other people's money; it's one of the few places where "we'll add proper accounting later" is a genuinely expensive thing to retrofit onto an existing balance-column system.
  • What's the "chart of accounts" in this context? The set of named ledger accounts your system tracks (merchant payable, customer receivable, PSP clearing, fee expense, etc.) — designing it well up front is most of the actual design work in a payments ledger.

Common wrong answer: "Double-entry is just an accounting formality required for the finance team's books, not something the engineering system itself needs." The self-verifying, immutable-audit-trail property is an engineering correctness tool first — it's how you catch bugs, races, and data corruption in the system that's actually moving money, independent of whatever the finance team separately does with the numbers.

What does "settlement" mean from the ledger's point of view, and why is there almost always a lag between "payment succeeded" and "money actually in our bank account"?screener

Answer. A successful charge means the issuer has authorized and (post-capture) committed to paying, but the actual movement of funds from issuer to acquirer to the PSP to your bank account happens through batch settlement cycles, not instantly — commonly T+1 to T+several business days depending on the PSP, card network, and country. From the ledger's perspective, this means "payment succeeded" and "cash landed in our account" are two distinct events that need two distinct ledger states: a receivable/in-transit account representing money you're owed but don't yet physically have, and a cash account representing money actually settled into your bank. Conflating these — treating a successful charge as immediately spendable cash — is a common and costly mistake, especially for businesses paying out to others (marketplaces, platforms) based on funds they don't actually hold yet.

Example

Day 0: charge succeeds -> DEBIT accounts_receivable_psp 100 / CREDIT revenue 100  (owed, not yet cash)
Day 2: settlement batch lands in bank -> DEBIT cash 100 / CREDIT accounts_receivable_psp 100 (now cash)

Follow-ups

  • Does every payment method settle on the same schedule? No — cards typically settle faster than certain bank-transfer/ACH methods, and this variance is itself something a ledger needs to model per payment method, not assume uniformly.
  • What happens if the PSP's settlement batch amount doesn't match what your ledger expected? That's a reconciliation break (covered next) — the whole reason settlement and your internal ledger are tracked as separate concerns is so a mismatch is detectable rather than silently absorbed.
  • Can you offer instant payouts to sellers before settlement actually completes? Yes, several PSPs offer this as a paid feature — but it means the platform is extending short-term credit against not-yet-settled funds, a real risk decision, not a free convenience.

Common wrong answer: "Once the PaymentIntent shows succeeded, that money is in our account and available." Success at the authorization/capture level and settlement into your actual bank account are different events on different timelines — a ledger and any payout logic built on top of it has to track both, not conflate them into one state.

Design the core chart of accounts for a simple e-commerce ledger: a customer pays $100, your PSP takes a $3.20 fee, and you eventually get paid out. Walk through the entries.mid

Answer. A minimal but correct chart needs at least: a PSP clearing/receivable account (funds owed by the PSP before settlement), a revenue account, a fee-expense account, and a cash account. The charge itself posts revenue against the PSP receivable at the gross amount; the fee is a separate posting that most PSPs actually net out at settlement time rather than charging separately, so the fee entry typically appears alongside the settlement posting, not the original charge. At settlement, the PSP receivable is cleared: cash increases by the net amount actually deposited, and the fee-expense account absorbs the difference — the key invariant being that gross revenue minus fee expense equals the net cash that lands, always reconcilable line by line.

Example

At charge time:
  DEBIT  psp_receivable        100.00
  CREDIT revenue               100.00

At settlement (PSP pays out net of fee):
  DEBIT  cash                   96.80
  DEBIT  fee_expense             3.20
  CREDIT psp_receivable        100.00

Follow-ups

  • Where does a refund fit into this chart? A reversing entry against revenue and psp_receivable (or cash, if post-settlement) for the refunded amount — never editing the original entries, always an additional offsetting posting.
  • What if the PSP charges the fee upfront rather than netting it at settlement? Then the fee posts at charge time instead, and the settlement entry is simpler (cash exactly equals the receivable) — the chart design has to match your specific PSP's actual fee-timing model, verified against their settlement reports, not assumed.
  • Why not just track "net revenue" directly and skip the separate fee-expense account? Because gross revenue and fee cost are different business questions (top-line sales vs. cost of payment acceptance) that finance, tax, and unit-economics analysis all need separately — collapsing them loses real information the ledger exists to preserve.

Common wrong answer: "Just record $96.80 as revenue since that's what we actually received." This collapses gross sales and payment-processing cost into one number, losing the ability to analyze fee cost, breaking any downstream reporting that expects gross revenue (tax reporting often needs the gross figure specifically), and making it much harder to reconcile against the PSP's own statements, which report gross amounts and fees as separate line items.

What does "reconciliation" mean in this context, and what's a concrete break you'd expect to find between your ledger and the PSP's settlement report?mid

Answer. Reconciliation is the process of comparing your internal ledger's expected state against an independent source of truth — the PSP's settlement/payout reports — and confirming every entry on one side has a matching, explained counterpart on the other, flagging anything that doesn't (a "break"). Concrete break types: a transaction your ledger shows as succeeded that never appears in a settlement batch (possibly a webhook you never received, or a payment that was later reversed without your system being notified); a settlement amount that doesn't match your expected net (a fee schedule change, an unexpected additional fee like a currency-conversion charge, or a dispute-related deduction you hadn't yet recorded); and timing breaks, where a transaction legitimately settles in a later batch than expected and just needs to be matched once that batch arrives rather than treated as missing. Reconciliation isn't a one-time report — it needs to run continuously (daily, ideally automated) precisely because breaks are where real money-losing bugs and fraud get caught early instead of months later.

Example

Ledger expects: payment_intent pi_123, net settlement $96.80, batch date 2026-09-10
PSP report shows: pi_123, net settlement $94.10, batch date 2026-09-10
Break: $2.70 unexplained -- investigate: extra fee? partial dispute deduction? fee-schedule change?

Follow-ups

  • Should reconciliation be automated or is a manual spreadsheet review acceptable? At any real transaction volume, manual review misses breaks and doesn't scale — automated daily reconciliation with alerting on unmatched items is the standard expectation once volume exceeds trivial.
  • What's the cost of NOT reconciling regularly? Breaks compound silently — a small, consistent fee-calculation bug can bleed real money for months before anyone notices, precisely because nothing forces the comparison to happen.
  • Is reconciliation only about money, or does it also catch state bugs? Both — a payment your ledger thinks is "pending" that the PSP's report shows as settled weeks ago is a state-sync bug, not a money-amount bug, and reconciliation catches both categories the same way.

Common wrong answer: "We trust our own webhook-driven ledger updates, so reconciliation against the PSP's reports is redundant." Webhooks can be missed, mis-processed, or arrive with race conditions (per unit 03); reconciliation against an independent report is precisely the safety net that catches exactly those failures, which is why it's standard practice even for teams confident in their webhook handling.

Design an automated daily reconciliation job. What does it actually compare, and what should happen when it finds a break?senior

Answer. The job pulls the PSP's settlement/payout report for the period (via API or scheduled file export) and your own ledger's expected-settlement records for the same period, matches them by a stable shared key (the PSP's own transaction/payout reference, never your own internal ID alone, since that's not guaranteed to appear verbatim in their report), and for each PSP-side record either finds a matching ledger entry with the same amount (auto-confirmed, no action), finds a matching entry with a different amount (a "amount break," needs investigation), or finds no matching ledger entry at all (a "missing" break, often signaling a missed webhook or an out-of-band PSP-initiated adjustment). Separately, it should check the reverse direction too — ledger entries expecting settlement that never appeared in any PSP report by their expected date, which surfaces stuck/delayed payments proactively. Breaks should never auto-resolve silently; they go into a review queue with enough context (both sides' records, the specific field that mismatched) for a human to close out, and the job's own health (break count trend, oldest unresolved break) should itself be monitored, since a growing backlog of unresolved breaks is a leading indicator of a systemic bug.

Example

For each PSP settlement record:
  match by psp_reference_id against ledger
  if no match -> break: "missing_in_ledger"
  elif amount differs -> break: "amount_mismatch"
  else -> mark reconciled

For each ledger record expecting settlement by today, with no PSP match yet:
  if past expected settlement date -> break: "missing_in_psp_report" (possible stuck payment)

Follow-ups

  • What key should you match on if the PSP's report uses a different ID scheme than your internal records? The PSP's own transaction/charge reference, always stored on your ledger entry at creation time specifically so this later join is possible — retrofitting a missing foreign key after the fact is painful and is a common real-world gap.
  • Should currency differences be handled here too? Yes, if you operate multi-currency — matching needs to compare in the settlement currency the PSP report uses, not silently convert, since FX-rate assumptions are themselves a common source of false-positive breaks.
  • What's a reasonable SLA for resolving a break once found? Varies by materiality, but "missing" breaks (possible lost money or missed webhook) should get same-day attention; small amount-mismatch breaks from known, expected fee variance can batch into a weekly review.

Common wrong answer: "Sum both sides' totals for the day and compare the aggregate numbers." Aggregate-only comparison can mask two offsetting breaks (one transaction $5 too high, another $5 too low) that net to a matching total while both individual records are actually wrong — reconciliation has to match at the individual-transaction level to be trustworthy.

Your reconciliation job flags a $4,000 "missing in ledger" break — the PSP's report shows a settlement your system has no record of at all. Walk through how you'd investigate it.senior

Answer. First check whether this is a webhook gap: query the PSP's own event log/API directly for the transaction referenced in the settlement report (bypassing your own webhook-derived state entirely) to confirm the underlying payment did happen and get its full details — this tells you whether your system ever knew about this transaction at all. If it never appears in your own systems anywhere, the most common causes are a webhook that was never delivered or was delivered and silently failed processing (check webhook delivery logs / dead-letter queues from unit 03's pipeline), or a transaction that originated outside your normal flow entirely (a manual dashboard action, a different integration hitting the same PSP account, a test-mode leak into production reporting). Once root-caused, backfill the missing ledger entries from the PSP's authoritative data (not guessed), and separately fix whatever caused the gap so it doesn't recur — a one-off backfill without a root-cause fix just guarantees the next occurrence.

Example

1. Query PSP directly for the transaction referenced in the settlement report (ground truth)
2. Check webhook delivery logs for that event ID -- delivered? processed? dead-lettered?
3. If never delivered: check PSP dashboard for endpoint health/recent failures
4. If delivered but not processed: check application logs/DLQ for a processing error at that time
5. Backfill ledger entry from PSP's authoritative data; fix root cause; add a regression check

Follow-ups

  • Could this be a fraud indicator rather than a bug? Worth ruling out — a transaction your system has zero record of, moving real money, is exactly the pattern an account-takeover of your PSP dashboard (someone issuing manual actions outside your normal application flow) would produce, so if the "manual dashboard action" explanation checks out, verify who performed it and why.
  • How would you prevent this exact category of break from recurring? Alert directly on webhook-delivery failures/dead-letters in near-real-time (rather than discovering the gap a day later via reconciliation) — reconciliation should be a backstop that rarely fires, not the primary detection mechanism for a broken webhook pipeline.
  • Should you backfill by re-deriving the entry from your own business logic, or copy the PSP's numbers directly? Copy the PSP's authoritative numbers directly for the ledger entry itself — re-deriving risks reproducing whatever assumption caused the original gap; the PSP's data is ground truth here by definition.

Common wrong answer: "Just add a manual journal entry for $4,000 to make the reconciliation balance again." Force-balancing the numbers without identifying what transaction actually happened and why your system never recorded it fixes the symptom the reconciliation report shows while leaving the actual gap (a webhook pipeline problem, or worse, unauthorized account activity) completely uninvestigated.

How would you extend a single-currency double-entry ledger to correctly support multi-currency settlement, where a EUR sale settles into a USD bank account?principal

Answer. Every ledger entry needs to carry its own currency explicitly, and the classic double-entry balance invariant (debits equal credits) only holds *within* a single currency — you cannot sum a EUR debit against a USD credit and expect zero. The clean design keeps per-currency sub-ledgers that each independently balance, plus an explicit FX-conversion entry pair whenever currency actually converts: a EUR receivable is cleared and a USD cash account is credited, with the FX rate used and any resulting gain/loss versus the transaction-time rate posted to a dedicated FX gain/loss account, not silently absorbed into revenue. This makes the rate used at every conversion step auditable and explains exactly why $103.20 landed in the bank for a transaction originally recorded as €100 — the answer isn't "rounding," it's a specific, logged FX conversion at a specific rate with a specific gain/loss delta.

Example

At charge time (EUR):
  DEBIT  psp_receivable_EUR   100.00 EUR
  CREDIT revenue_EUR          100.00 EUR

At settlement, converted to USD at rate 1.032:
  DEBIT  cash_USD             103.20 USD
  CREDIT psp_receivable_EUR   100.00 EUR  (cleared)
  [FX gain/loss posted separately if the rate differs from any earlier internal estimate]

Follow-ups

  • Where does the "current balance" concept get complicated by this? A merchant's "total balance" across currencies isn't a single number you can sum directly — it's either reported per-currency, or converted to a reporting currency at a specific point-in-time rate purely for display, which must never be confused with the underlying per-currency ledger truth.
  • Does this design need to track the FX rate at multiple points (authorization time vs. settlement time)? Yes, if your system shows the customer an estimated converted amount before settlement — the gap between an estimated rate and the actual settlement rate is exactly the FX gain/loss that needs its own auditable entry, not a shrug.
  • Is this the PSP's problem to solve, or does the merchant's own ledger need to model it regardless? The PSP handles the actual currency conversion mechanics, but the merchant's own books still need to record the transaction accurately in whatever currency their financial reporting requires — this ledger design work doesn't go away just because a PSP is doing the conversion itself.

Common wrong answer: "Just convert every amount to USD at the time of recording and store everything in one currency." Converting at record-time and discarding the original currency and rate loses the information needed to reconcile against the PSP's own multi-currency settlement reports (which report in the original transaction currency) and makes any FX gain/loss on later settlement unexplainable — the original currency and the specific rate used at each conversion step both need to be preserved, not collapsed away early.

A payout batch job that pays out to 10,000 connected sellers on a marketplace platform crashes halfway through. How do you design the ledger and the job so this doesn't produce duplicate or missing payouts on retry?principal

Answer. Each individual payout within the batch needs its own idempotency key (per unit 02's pattern, here keyed by seller ID + payout period) and its own durable status row, written before the payout call is issued — the batch job itself should be a driver that iterates over "payouts still pending for this period," not a stateless loop that re-processes the whole seller list blindly on every run. On crash and restart, the job re-queries for pending payouts (those not yet marked succeeded/failed), which naturally skips everyone already paid and resumes exactly where it left off; the per-payout idempotency key additionally protects against the edge case where a payout call actually succeeded at the PSP right before the crash, even though your own status write for it never completed. The ledger posts each payout as its own double-entry pair (debit the seller's payable, credit cash-out) at the moment of confirmed success, so a partial batch failure leaves the ledger in a perfectly consistent state reflecting exactly who was and wasn't paid — there's no "half-done batch" state to reconcile, only individually resolved or still-pending payout rows.

Example

-- before the batch run: one row per seller for this period, status='pending'
-- job processes rows where status='pending', in any order, safely resumable:
UPDATE payouts SET status='in_flight' WHERE id = ? AND status = 'pending'; -- claim
-- call PSP with Idempotency-Key = 'payout_' || seller_id || '_' || period
-- on confirmed success: UPDATE payouts SET status='succeeded' ...; post ledger entries
-- on crash: next run simply re-queries WHERE status IN ('pending','in_flight') and resumes

Follow-ups

  • What if a payout row is stuck in in_flight because a worker claimed it and then crashed? Needs a lease/timeout the same way the concurrent-worker idempotency question in unit 02 did — an in_flight row older than a reasonable threshold with no corresponding PSP-side confirmation should be reclaimable by a subsequent run, after checking PSP status first.
  • Should the whole batch be all-or-nothing (a single transaction), or independently resolved per seller? Independently resolved — an all-or-nothing batch of 10,000 payout calls to an external API is neither practically transactional (you can't roll back money already sent) nor desirable (one seller's failure shouldn't delay everyone else's payout).
  • How would you monitor this system's health beyond "did the job finish"? Track pending/in_flight row age distribution and count — a growing tail of old pending rows after the job's expected completion window is the actual health signal, more informative than a binary job-succeeded/failed status.

Common wrong answer: "Wrap the whole batch in a database transaction so it either fully commits or fully rolls back on crash." A single DB transaction can't make external PSP payout API calls transactional — money that's already been sent to sellers 1 through 4,000 doesn't roll back just because your database transaction does, and this design would either double-pay on retry (no idempotency) or lose track of who was actually paid (no per-row persisted state) depending on exactly where the crash occurred.

08 Payment methods & rails beyond cards

Cards dominate interview questions but not global payment volume — ACH/SEPA bank debits, wallets, BNPL, and open-banking-driven account-to-account payments each have distinct settlement timing, reversal risk, and failure modes card-only engineers routinely get wrong. Expect this unit to test whether a candidate over-generalizes card assumptions onto rails that don't share them.

Docs: Stripe ACH direct debit · Adyen payment methods

ACH direct debit vs. a card charge — what's fundamentally different about the risk and timing profile?screener

Answer. A card charge gets a near-real-time authorization response (approved/declined in seconds) because the issuer checks it live; ACH debit has no equivalent real-time check — you initiate a debit against a bank account and typically don't find out it failed (insufficient funds, closed account, or an unauthorized-debit claim) until one to several business days later, well after you may have already shipped goods or granted access on the assumption it succeeded. ACH also carries a materially longer reversal window than most card disputes — a consumer can dispute an unauthorized ACH debit for a period measured in weeks under US banking rules (NACHA), not the days-to-see-a-decline card charges give you. This combination (delayed failure, delayed reversal risk) means ACH is cheaper per-transaction than cards but structurally riskier for anything where you'd extend value before confirming the debit actually cleared.

Example

Card:  charge -> approved/declined in seconds -> settle T+1-3
ACH:   debit initiated -> "accepted" (not yet failed) -> may bounce 1-5+ business days later -> longer dispute window

Follow-ups

  • So is ACH ever a good choice for a business needing fast confidence a payment cleared? Not without additional risk controls (waiting periods before fulfillment, account-verification services at setup time) — ACH's low cost is a trade against exactly this certainty, which is why it's common for recurring/subscription billing (where a bounce just means retry-the-charge, not "we already shipped the product") more than one-off instant-fulfillment purchases.
  • What is "micro-deposit verification" and why does it exist? A setup-time check where tiny deposits (or a real-time bank-login-based instant-verification flow) confirm the bank account is real and owned by the person authorizing the debit, reducing (not eliminating) the risk of debiting an account without genuine authorization.
  • Is SEPA (EU bank debit) the same risk profile as US ACH? Similar in kind — both are bank-to-bank debit schemes with delayed-failure characteristics — but specific timelines, dispute windows, and mandate requirements differ by scheme and are worth checking against current scheme rules rather than assuming ACH's numbers apply directly.

Common wrong answer: "ACH is basically a cheaper card charge." Cost is the only axis where that comparison holds — the risk timing, failure visibility, and dispute-window differences are substantial enough that treating ACH as a drop-in cheaper card replacement, without adjusting fulfillment/risk logic, is a common and costly mistake.

How does a digital wallet (Apple Pay, Google Pay) transaction actually flow differently from typing in a card number, from the merchant's integration point of view?screener

Answer. The wallet doesn't introduce a new payment rail underneath — it's still a card network transaction at the settlement layer — but the merchant integration and the data presented change meaningfully. The wallet authenticates the user locally (biometric/device passcode) and returns a payment credential that's typically a network token (a device-specific, cryptographically-bound token, not the actual PAN) plus a cryptogram proving the specific device and transaction, which the merchant passes to their PSP largely as they would a regular card payment method. From the merchant's side, integration is usually just adding the wallet's JS/SDK button to the checkout page and letting the PSP handle the token-to-authorization translation — the meaningful engineering difference is UX (one-tap, biometric-authenticated) and the improved fraud/liability profile from device-level authentication, not a different settlement mechanism.

Example

// Apple Pay via Stripe: still ends up as a PaymentMethod, same downstream PaymentIntent flow
const paymentRequest = stripe.paymentRequest({ country: 'US', currency: 'usd', total: { label: 'Order', amount: 5000 } });
// device handles biometric auth; merchant receives a PaymentMethod token, not raw card data

Follow-ups

  • Does using a wallet change PCI scope? Favorably — the merchant never sees any card data at all, wallet or otherwise, which is generally at least as good as SAQ A treatment.
  • Why do wallet transactions often see higher approval rates than manually-entered cards? The device-bound token and cryptogram give issuers stronger authentication signal than a typed card number, similar in spirit to the CP/CNP liability discussion in unit 01.
  • Is a wallet transaction CP or CNP? Typically treated as CNP for the purchase channel, though with favorable liability treatment given the device-level authentication, as noted in unit 01.

Common wrong answer: "Wallets are a completely separate payment rail from cards, like ACH is." Apple Pay/Google Pay transactions still ride the existing card networks underneath — they're a UX and tokenization layer on top of card rails, not an independent settlement system the way bank-debit or open-banking rails are.

Explain "Buy Now, Pay Later" (BNPL) from an integration and risk perspective — who's actually extending credit, and what does the merchant's system need to handle differently?mid

Answer. The BNPL provider (Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay, and similar) is the one extending consumer credit and bearing the underwriting/default risk — the merchant is typically paid the full amount upfront (minus the provider's fee) at the time of purchase, functionally similar to accepting any other payment method, while the BNPL provider then collects installments from the consumer over time and absorbs the risk if the consumer defaults. From an integration standpoint this means BNPL behaves more like a card charge (near-immediate merchant settlement, standard capture/refund API patterns) than like the merchant extending credit itself, but the approval decision now depends on the *provider's* underwriting of that specific consumer for that specific amount, which can decline for reasons entirely orthogonal to card-level fraud checks (credit risk, not card validity). Refund handling needs care: refunding a BNPL purchase typically also needs to correctly unwind the consumer's installment schedule on the provider's side, which the PSP/BNPL API handles, but a merchant's refund logic built assuming card-style full-reversal semantics can miss provider-specific partial-refund/installment-adjustment nuances.

Example

Merchant:   paid ~full amount upfront (minus provider fee), similar timing to a card settlement
BNPL provider: extends credit to consumer, collects installments, bears default risk
Consumer:   pays provider in installments, not the merchant

Follow-ups

  • Does the merchant bear any risk at all in a standard BNPL integration? Minimal direct credit risk, but real reputational/dispute risk if the provider's decline or installment-collection issues create a poor customer experience the merchant is blamed for.
  • Why would a merchant pay a higher fee for BNPL than a card? BNPL providers typically charge higher merchant fees than card interchange, justified by demonstrated conversion and average-order-value lifts — a commercial trade-off, not a payments-engineering one, though engineering often has to model and report on it.
  • Is BNPL underwriting done per-transaction or per-customer? Per-transaction/per-purchase typically, though providers do build ongoing risk profiles per consumer across merchants using their network.

Common wrong answer: "BNPL is just a checkout button; the merchant finances the installments themselves like a store credit card." The merchant is not extending credit or collecting installments in the standard BNPL model — the provider is; conflating the two misunderstands where the risk, underwriting, and cash-flow timing actually sit, which materially affects how a merchant should model BNPL in their own risk and revenue reporting.

What is open banking / account-to-account (A2A) payment, and why do proponents argue it could meaningfully undercut card-network economics?mid

Answer. Open banking payments let a merchant initiate a payment directly from a customer's bank account to the merchant's, authenticated through the customer's own bank (via regulated APIs, in the EU/UK under PSD2's Account Information/Payment Initiation Service Provider framework), bypassing card networks and their interchange/scheme fees entirely. Because there's no card network or issuer interchange fee in the chain, per-transaction cost can be substantially lower than card acceptance, which is the core commercial argument for it; the trade-offs are UX (a bank-authentication redirect flow, historically clunkier than a one-tap wallet, though improving), and residual reliance on each individual bank's own API reliability and UX, which varies far more than the standardized card-network experience does. Adoption has been steadily growing in markets with strong open-banking regulatory mandates (UK, EU) but remains far from card-level ubiquity as of today.

Example

Card:        customer -> card network -> issuer -> acquirer -> merchant (interchange + scheme fees at each hop)
Open banking: customer -> customer's own bank -> merchant's bank (direct, no card network fees)

Follow-ups

  • Does open banking have the same chargeback/dispute mechanism as cards? No — dispute rights and reversal mechanics differ substantially from the card-network chargeback system, and merchants need to understand the actual regulatory/contractual dispute framework for A2A specifically rather than assuming card-like protections or exposure.
  • Is PSD3/PSR relevant here? Directly — the payee-name verification requirement discussed in unit 04 applies specifically to this class of credit-transfer payment, and PSD3/PSR's broader aim includes strengthening the open-banking framework PSD2 originally established.
  • Why hasn't open banking displaced cards faster given the cost advantage? Consumer habit/trust, inconsistent UX across different banks' authentication flows, and the deeply entrenched merchant tooling/loyalty-program ecosystem built around cards are all cited as friction, alongside genuinely earlier-stage dispute/reversal frameworks.

Common wrong answer: "Open banking payments are just another name for ACH/bank transfer." They share the "no card network" characteristic, but open banking's regulated, API-driven, bank-authenticated initiation flow (with strong customer consent and near-real-time confirmation in many implementations) is a meaningfully different mechanism and regulatory framework from a traditional ACH batch debit, not merely a rebrand.

A merchant wants to offer 8 different payment methods (cards, wallets, several BNPL providers, a regional bank-transfer method) at checkout. What does this actually cost the engineering team beyond "add more buttons"?senior

Answer. Each payment method typically has its own status-machine quirks, settlement timing, refund semantics, and failure modes that a checkout built card-first will get subtly wrong if generalized naively — a refund API that assumes instant reversal breaks against a BNPL provider's installment-unwind timing; a "payment succeeded" webhook handler tuned for cards' fast confirmation mishandles a bank-transfer method's multi-day pending state; a dispute/chargeback pipeline built around card reason codes doesn't map cleanly onto open-banking or BNPL dispute mechanics. The real engineering cost is designing an abstraction layer that captures genuine cross-method commonality (a payment has an amount, a status, eventually settles or fails) without pretending away the real differences (settlement timing, dispute mechanism, refund constraints) that each method's specific implementation still needs to expose and handle correctly. Underestimating this is exactly how "we added Klarna in a sprint" turns into months of edge-case bug reports around refunds and reconciliation.

Example

Common abstraction: PaymentAttempt { amount, status: pending|succeeded|failed, method_type }
Per-method specifics still needed:
  card:          fast auth/capture, chargeback dispute flow, 3DS
  ACH:           delayed failure, NACHA dispute window, no instant confirmation
  BNPL:          provider underwriting decline reasons, installment-aware refunds
  bank transfer: multi-day pending, different reconciliation cadence

Follow-ups

  • Does adding payment methods increase or decrease overall conversion? Generally increases it (customers use their preferred method), which is the commercial driver — but it's a real trade against the engineering cost above, not a free win, and should be prioritized by actual observed demand, not added speculatively.
  • Should the reconciliation job from unit 07 change per payment method? Yes — different settlement timing and reporting formats per method mean reconciliation logic often needs per-method adapters even if the overall job structure stays common.
  • How would a payment orchestration layer (from unit 01's build-vs-buy discussion) help here specifically? It's exactly this kind of method-proliferation cost that orchestration platforms are built to absorb — a second concrete trigger condition (beyond multi-PSP routing) that can justify adopting one.

Common wrong answer: "It's just adding a few more entries to the payment-method dropdown and a few more PSP API calls." This treats a genuine cross-cutting design problem (differing settlement timing, dispute mechanics, and refund semantics across methods) as a UI change, which is exactly the underestimation that produces broken reconciliation and refund bugs once real volume flows through the newly-added methods.

Design a checkout that intelligently offers different payment methods to different customers/regions rather than showing every method to everyone. What signals would you use, and what's the risk of getting this wrong?senior

Answer. The signals that actually matter: customer billing/shipping country or detected locale (a bank-transfer method popular in one country is often meaningless or unavailable in another), device type (some wallets are effectively unusable outside their native app/browser context), cart amount (some BNPL providers have minimum/maximum thresholds), and currency (a payment method may only settle in specific currencies). The risk of getting this wrong runs both directions: showing irrelevant methods clutters the checkout and can genuinely hurt conversion (decision paralysis, or worse, a customer picking a method that then fails for a reason invisible to them, like a BNPL provider declining their specific credit profile with no clear message), while being too aggressive about filtering can hide a method a specific customer would have preferred and successfully used, silently costing conversion in the other direction. Most PSPs' own "dynamic payment methods" features (Stripe's automatic payment-method configuration, Adyen's similar capability) already implement much of this logic server-side based on the transaction context, which is usually more current and better-tuned than a bespoke rules engine a merchant maintains themselves.

Example

Signals: billing_country, currency, cart_amount, device_type
Rule sketch:
  if currency == EUR and country in SEPA_zone -> show SEPA debit
  if cart_amount within [provider_min, provider_max] -> show that BNPL provider
  if device_type == mobile_safari -> show Apple Pay prominently

Follow-ups

  • Should this logic live in the merchant's own code or be delegated to the PSP? Delegating to the PSP's dynamic-payment-method feature is usually the pragmatic default — it stays current with each method's eligibility rules (which change) without the merchant needing to track every provider's threshold and geography rules themselves.
  • How would you measure whether the filtering logic is actually helping or hurting conversion? A/B test showing a broader vs. narrower method set for a comparable segment, tracking both overall conversion and per-method failure rate — "fewer options" and "more options" both have plausible upside, so this needs real measurement, not intuition.
  • What happens if the eligibility signal is wrong (e.g. a VPN masks true customer location)? The customer sees a method that ultimately fails at the provider level, which needs a graceful, clear fallback UX (not just an opaque error) since the underlying eligibility mismatch will occasionally happen no matter how good the signals are.

Common wrong answer: "Just show all available payment methods to everyone and let the customer pick." Beyond the clutter/decision-paralysis cost, several methods (BNPL amount thresholds, region-specific bank debit schemes, currency-restricted wallets) will simply fail for a meaningful share of customers who pick them, producing avoidable checkout failures that better-targeted method display would have prevented entirely.

What is Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC), and why is it controversial from a consumer-value standpoint?screener

Answer. DCC lets a merchant (or more precisely the acquirer/terminal on the merchant's behalf) offer a cardholder the choice to pay in their home currency instead of the merchant's local currency, converting at a rate set by the DCC provider rather than the card network. It's controversial because DCC exchange rates and markups are typically worse for the consumer than the card network's own conversion rate (which the consumer would get by declining DCC and letting their issuer convert), yet DCC is often presented as a convenience ("know exactly what you're paying in your home currency") that obscures the worse rate; consumers who don't understand this frequently accept it because it looks like transparency. Regulators and consumer-protection advocates in multiple markets have pushed for clearer DCC disclosure specifically because of this gap between how it's marketed and the actual value delivered.

Example

Card network rate:  100 EUR -> ~108 USD (interbank-linked rate + issuer's own markup)
DCC offered rate:    100 EUR -> ~112 USD (DCC provider's markup, often worse, presented as "your choice")

Follow-ups

  • Who decides whether DCC is offered at a given terminal or checkout? The acquirer or DCC provider integrated into that terminal/checkout, not the card network or issuer — card networks generally require DCC be clearly disclosed and optional, not defaulted.
  • Should a merchant enable DCC on their checkout? It can generate incremental margin/commission for the merchant, but at real reputational risk if customers feel misled — a trade-off worth stating explicitly rather than defaulting to "yes" or "no" without the business weighing in.
  • Is DCC the same thing as pricing your product in the customer's local currency (unit 09's later question)? No — that's the merchant pricing in the customer's currency directly (no consumer choice at the point of sale); DCC is a currency-conversion *option* layered onto a transaction otherwise priced in the merchant's currency.

Common wrong answer: "DCC is the same as showing prices in multiple currencies for convenience." DCC is specifically a point-of-sale currency-conversion offer with its own (often unfavorable) exchange rate and markup, presented as consumer choice — conflating it with simple multi-currency price display misses exactly the consumer-protection concern that makes it a notable topic.

Explain "settle in merchant's currency" vs. "merchant prices and settles directly in the customer's local currency" — what changes for the business?mid

Answer. If a merchant always prices and settles in one home currency (say USD) and lets the PSP/acquirer handle conversion when a foreign customer pays, the merchant's revenue reporting and accounting stay simple (everything in USD) but the *customer* sees a foreign-currency conversion happen somewhere in the chain (their issuer or a DCC offer), which can create price-perception friction and occasionally surprise FX fees for the customer. If instead the merchant enables local pricing (say, listing EUR prices for EU customers, settling that revenue back into USD themselves), conversion risk and the FX spread shift to the merchant's own treasury operations, but customers get a cleaner "no surprise foreign transaction fee" experience that measurably improves conversion in many markets. The engineering implication is that local-currency pricing needs a maintained price list or a live-rate-with-markup pricing engine per currency (not just "same USD price, converted at checkout"), and needs FX risk to be actively managed (hedging, or accepting the exposure) rather than passed through.

Example

Model A: price $100 USD always -> customer in EU pays ~92 EUR at their issuer's rate (issuer/DCC controls the rate customer sees)
Model B: merchant prices at 95 EUR directly for EU -> merchant settles EUR revenue, converts to USD on their own schedule/hedge

Follow-ups

  • Does Stripe/Adyen support settling in multiple currencies? Yes — both support holding balances and settling payouts in multiple currencies, which is the infrastructure piece that makes Model B practical without the merchant building their own multi-currency treasury from scratch.
  • How often should local prices be refreshed against a floating exchange rate? Depends on risk appetite and margin — some businesses update daily against a live rate with a fixed markup, others peg prices for weeks/months and absorb short-term FX movement, since re-pricing too often creates its own customer-facing confusion.
  • Does this interact with the multi-currency ledger design from unit 07? Directly — local pricing is exactly the scenario that motivates per-currency sub-ledgers and an explicit FX gain/loss account, since revenue now genuinely arrives in multiple currencies rather than being converted transparently upstream.

Common wrong answer: "It doesn't matter, the PSP converts the money either way." The *economic* conversion happens either way, but *who* bears the FX spread/risk and what the customer perceives at checkout differ substantially between the two models — treating them as equivalent misses a real product and finance decision.

A subscription business bills customers monthly in their local currency, but the exchange rate moves noticeably between billing cycles. What are the design options for handling this, and what are the trade-offs?senior

Answer. Option one: re-price at the current exchange rate every billing cycle, keeping the merchant's home-currency revenue per customer roughly stable but producing a locally-visible price that fluctuates month to month, which can confuse or frustrate subscribers ("why did my bill change with no plan change?"). Option two: fix the local-currency price for an extended period (e.g. a year) and re-evaluate periodically, giving customers price stability but leaving the merchant fully exposed to FX movement over that period, which for a large subscriber base in a volatile currency can be a material, hard-to-forecast cost or windfall. Option three: pass a currency-adjustment surcharge/note only when the rate moves beyond some threshold, which balances customer-facing stability with bounding the merchant's worst-case exposure, at the cost of extra billing-logic complexity and now needing customer communication for the adjustment. Most engineering-mature subscription platforms choose a hybrid: fixed local price for a defined term, algorithmically re-priced at renewal boundaries, exactly mirroring how they'd handle a domestic price increase.

Example

Option 2 (fixed for term) sketch:
  subscription.local_price = snapshot_rate * home_price   // locked at subscription start or last renewal
  on renewal_boundary: recompute local_price from current rate, notify customer, apply going forward

Follow-ups

  • Does this interact with dunning/failed-payment retry logic? Yes — a re-priced renewal is a plausible reason for an otherwise-healthy card to decline (spend limit, unexpected-amount fraud flags on the issuer side), so re-pricing events benefit from being flagged distinctly in retry/dunning logic rather than treated as a generic decline.
  • Should the threshold for a mid-term adjustment be a percentage or an absolute amount? Percentage is more common (proportional to the underlying price), but very low-priced subscriptions sometimes need an absolute floor so a tiny percentage move doesn't trigger churn-inducing billing noise for pennies.
  • Who inside the business should own the re-pricing policy? Finance/pricing strategy, not engineering — engineering's job is to build the mechanism (snapshot, renewal-boundary recompute, notification) configurable enough that the actual threshold and cadence can be a business decision, not a hardcoded constant.

Common wrong answer: "Just always charge at today's live exchange rate, it's the most accurate." "Accurate" isn't the only or even primary consideration here — from the customer's perspective a fluctuating subscription bill for a service whose usage didn't change reads as unpredictable and erodes trust, which is precisely why nearly every subscription business in practice trades some FX accuracy for local-price stability.

What's the difference between "cross-border" and "cross-currency" in a payment, and why do interchange/scheme fee schedules often treat them as separate cost triggers?mid

Answer. Cross-border refers to the issuer and acquirer being in different countries (regardless of currency — a US-issued card charged at a US-merchant's UK-domiciled acquiring entity could be cross-border even if both quote USD); cross-currency refers to the transaction currency differing from the cardholder's billing currency (requiring an actual conversion, regardless of whether issuer and acquirer are in the same country). Card networks and many acquirers apply separate additional fees for each condition independently, because each represents a genuinely distinct cost/risk driver: cross-border transactions carry different regulatory/interchange-cap regimes and higher historical fraud rates, while cross-currency transactions carry an actual FX conversion cost the entity performing it needs to recover. A transaction can be one, both, or neither, and a merchant analyzing their effective payment cost needs to separate these two dimensions rather than assume "international fee" is one undifferentiated line item.

Example

US card, US merchant, USD           -> neither -> baseline domestic interchange
US card, UK merchant, USD           -> cross-border only -> cross-border fee, no FX
US card, UK merchant, GBP           -> cross-border AND cross-currency -> both fees
EU card, EU merchant (diff country), EUR -> cross-border only (shared currency, SEPA/EU rules may cap this differently)

Follow-ups

  • Does this affect the smart-routing logic from unit 03? Yes — a routing engine optimizing cost should account for both dimensions per candidate route, since routing through a local acquiring entity in the customer's country can avoid the cross-border fee even when currency conversion still applies (or vice versa).
  • Are these fees regulated anywhere? Yes in some jurisdictions (e.g. EU interchange fee caps for certain transaction categories) but the specifics and current caps should be checked against current scheme/regulatory publications rather than assumed to be stable over time.
  • Can a merchant avoid cross-border fees by having local acquiring entities in each market? That's exactly the commercial logic behind "local acquiring" arrangements many large multinational merchants set up, though it comes with its own operational and integration overhead (unit 03's multi-PSP discussion again becomes relevant).

Common wrong answer: "They're the same thing — an international transaction gets an international fee." Treating cross-border and cross-currency as one undifferentiated category misses cost-optimization opportunities (e.g. local acquiring reduces cross-border fees even when currency conversion is unavoidable) that a candidate analyzing real payment economics should be able to identify separately.

How would you design idempotent, auditable handling of FX rate application in a payment/ledger system, given that rates change continuously and a given transaction needs one fixed, provable rate?principal

Answer. The core design need is to snapshot the exact rate, its source, and its timestamp at the moment it's applied to a specific transaction, and persist that snapshot immutably alongside the transaction record — not just the converted amount, but enough to reconstruct and defend the conversion later (rate provider, rate value, timestamp, any markup applied on top). This matters for reconciliation (unit 07's break-investigation logic needs to distinguish "this ledger entry is wrong" from "this entry correctly reflects a rate that has since moved"), for dispute defense (proving to a regulator, auditor, or customer exactly what rate and markup were applied and when), and for idempotent retries (if a payment attempt is retried, it must reuse the *original* rate snapshot associated with that idempotency key, not silently re-fetch a new rate under the same logical transaction, exactly analogous to unit 05's idempotency-key discussion but for a "what value was recorded" concern rather than "was the side effect performed"). Practically, this looks like a `fx_snapshot` record referenced by ID from the transaction, with the rate source's own timestamp and any internal markup stored as separate, auditable fields rather than folded into just a final converted number.

Example

CREATE TABLE fx_snapshots (
  id UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  from_currency CHAR(3), to_currency CHAR(3),
  base_rate NUMERIC(18,8), markup_bps INT, effective_rate NUMERIC(18,8),
  rate_source TEXT, source_timestamp TIMESTAMPTZ, captured_at TIMESTAMPTZ
);
-- transactions.fx_snapshot_id references this row; never recomputed on retry

Follow-ups

  • Should the rate be re-fetched if a retry happens hours later? No for the same logical transaction/idempotency key — reusing the original snapshot is what makes the retry produce an identical, auditable result; a genuinely new transaction (new idempotency key) fetches a fresh rate.
  • Where does markup (the merchant's or PSP's margin on conversion) get recorded? As an explicit separate field/line item from the base market rate, both for internal margin reporting and because some jurisdictions require disclosing the markup distinctly from the underlying rate.
  • How does this interact with the multi-currency ledger's FX gain/loss account from unit 07? The gain/loss account captures the *difference* between the rate at transaction time and the rate at eventual settlement/conversion-to-home-currency — the immutable snapshot here is exactly what lets that later calculation be precise and auditable rather than approximate.

Common wrong answer: "Just store the converted amount; the rate is implied by the ratio of the two amounts." That reconstruction loses the markup breakdown, the rate source, and the exact timestamp needed for audit and dispute defense, and it does nothing to solve the idempotent-retry problem — the actual requirement is to persist the rate application as a first-class, referenced record, not to leave it recoverable-in-theory from downstream numbers.

What is AVS (Address Verification Service) and CVV/CVC checking, and why are they weak signals on their own?screener

Answer. AVS checks whether the billing address/zip the customer entered matches what the issuer has on file for the card; CVV checking confirms the 3-4 digit security code printed on the card was provided (proving the customer likely has the physical card or its printed number, not that the transaction is legitimate). Both are weak signals in isolation because they're binary match/no-match checks easily satisfied by a fraudster who has stolen full card details including the billing address and CVV (common in large data breaches), and because AVS in particular has notoriously inconsistent support and matching behavior across different issuers and countries, producing partial-match results that are hard to interpret confidently. They remain useful as one input among many in a broader risk score rather than as standalone accept/decline gates, and issuers/networks treat them as advisory signals the merchant can choose how strictly to enforce.

Example

AVS result codes (typical): Y=full match, A=address only, Z=zip only, N=no match, U=unavailable
CVV result: M=match, N=no match, P=not processed
Neither alone should gate accept/decline for anything but the lowest-risk transactions.

Follow-ups

  • Are AVS/CVV checks part of PCI scope concerns? CVV specifically has strict storage rules under PCI DSS (never store it after authorization, discussed in unit 06) precisely because it's meant to prove physical possession at the moment of the transaction, not be retained.
  • Do AVS/CVV mismatches always cause a decline? Depends on the issuer's and merchant's own risk rules — a mismatch is often just one weighted input to the issuer's authorization decision and the merchant's own fraud score, not an automatic hard decline.
  • How does 3DS (unit 04) relate to AVS/CVV? 3DS provides a fundamentally stronger authentication signal (proving control of the cardholder's own device/bank credentials) than either AVS or CVV, which is why liability shift attaches to 3DS and not to AVS/CVV matches.

Common wrong answer: "A matching AVS and CVV means the transaction is legitimate." Both can be satisfied by a fraudster with stolen full card data, which is common enough after major breaches that treating a match as proof of legitimacy is a real and exploitable mistake, not just a theoretical edge case.

Explain the false-positive vs. false-negative trade-off in fraud rule design, with a concrete cost model.mid

Answer. A false positive blocks or challenges a legitimate customer (lost sale, customer friction/churn, and potentially reputational cost from wrongly flagging a genuine customer as fraudulent); a false negative lets a fraudulent transaction through (direct loss of goods/funds, a chargeback, and the chargeback-rate impact discussed in unit 06 including scheme monitoring program risk). These pull in opposite directions: tightening rules to catch more fraud (reducing false negatives) mechanically increases false positives, and loosening them does the reverse — there's no rule change that improves both simultaneously; the actual work is finding the operating point that minimizes total expected cost given the specific business's margins, average order value, and fraud base rate. A low-margin, high-volume business is typically more sensitive to false positives (losing legitimate low-margin sales stacks up fast); a high-value, lower-volume business with expensive individual goods can often tolerate more friction/false-positives to avoid a single large fraud loss.

Example

Cost model sketch:
  expected_cost = (false_positive_rate * legit_volume * lost_margin_per_order)
                + (false_negative_rate * fraud_volume * (loss_per_order + chargeback_fee))
  Tune threshold to minimize expected_cost, not to minimize either rate alone.

Follow-ups

  • How would you measure false positives if you never find out the customer was actually legitimate? This is a genuinely hard measurement problem — common approaches include manual review sampling of blocked transactions, comparing conversion-rate drops against a control group with looser rules, and tracking customer complaints/support tickets tied to blocks.
  • Should the threshold be static or dynamic? Dynamic in most mature systems — risk scoring models continuously retrain and thresholds get tuned per segment (geography, product category, customer history) rather than one static global cutoff.
  • Does 3DS change this trade-off? Yes — routing borderline-risk transactions to a 3DS challenge (unit 04) rather than an outright block converts some false-positive-risk into added friction with a chance of legitimate completion, which is often a better point on the trade-off curve than a hard block.

Common wrong answer: "The goal should be to minimize fraud losses as much as possible." Minimizing fraud losses in isolation, without weighing the false-positive cost of lost legitimate revenue, is how a naive risk team can quietly destroy more value in blocked good customers than they save in blocked fraud — the correct framing is minimizing total expected cost, not either error type alone.

What is velocity checking in fraud detection, and what are its failure modes?mid

Answer. Velocity checks flag or block based on the rate of activity from a given signal (same card, same device fingerprint, same IP, same billing address) exceeding a threshold within a time window — e.g. more than N transaction attempts from one card in an hour, or more than M distinct cards attempted from one device in a day — on the theory that legitimate customers rarely transact at that rate while card-testing fraud (attackers validating stolen card numbers by attempting many small transactions) does. The main failure modes: shared infrastructure (a large company's NAT'd office IP, a popular co-working space, a shared family device) can trigger false positives for a cluster of unrelated legitimate customers; legitimately bursty legitimate behavior (a customer retrying a declined card several times while troubleshooting, a gift-buying spree around a holiday) looks statistically similar to abuse; and sophisticated fraud specifically paces attempts below known thresholds once they're aware defenses exist, so static thresholds decay in effectiveness over time and need ongoing tuning.

Example

Rule: block if attempts_from_card(1h) > 5 OR distinct_cards_from_device(24h) > 10
Failure mode: shared office IP -> many legitimate employees flagged together
Failure mode: attacker paces at 4 attempts/hour per card across many cards -> stays under threshold

Follow-ups

  • How would you reduce the shared-IP false-positive problem? Weight IP-based velocity signals lower than device-fingerprint or card-based signals, and combine multiple weak signals into a composite score rather than gating on any single one, which is the general pattern risk engines use to reduce any one signal's individual failure mode.
  • What's "card testing" and how does velocity checking specifically target it? Attackers running small-value authorizations (often $0-1 or auth-only, never captured) across a list of stolen card numbers to identify which are still valid before committing to larger fraud — a burst of small, rapid authorization attempts against many distinct cards from one source is the classic velocity-detectable pattern.
  • Should velocity thresholds differ by product/merchant category? Yes — a digital-goods/gift-card merchant (attractive for card testing and fast monetization) typically needs tighter velocity rules than, say, a B2B invoicing platform with naturally low transaction frequency per customer.

Common wrong answer: "Set a strict velocity limit and it'll catch card testing without hurting real customers." Static thresholds both miss adaptive attackers who pace below them and catch legitimate bursty behavior (retries, shared IPs, gift-buying) — velocity is a useful signal, but treating it as a clean, self-sufficient rule ignores its well-known failure modes.

Design a real-time fraud-scoring pipeline that needs to make an accept/challenge/decline decision within the latency budget of a checkout flow (typically under a few hundred milliseconds). What are the architectural constraints?senior

Answer. The pipeline needs to gather signals (device fingerprint, velocity counters, historical customer behavior, third-party risk-data lookups, the transaction's own attributes) and score them within a tight latency budget, which rules out anything requiring synchronous calls to slow external services on the hot path — instead, most signals need to be precomputed/cached (velocity counters maintained incrementally in a fast key-value store, device/customer risk profiles refreshed asynchronously in the background rather than recomputed per-request) so the real-time call is mostly reads plus a lightweight model inference, not a fan-out of slow lookups. The score then maps to a decision: below a low-risk threshold, accept immediately; above a high-risk threshold, decline immediately; in between, route to a 3DS challenge (unit 04) or manual review queue, deferring the final decision without blocking the checkout indefinitely. Any component on this path needs a fallback behavior for when it's unavailable or too slow (fail open to a conservative default, typically routing to challenge rather than either extreme) rather than timing out the entire checkout.

Example

Checkout request
  -> fetch precomputed signals (device risk, velocity counters, customer history) from fast cache, parallel reads
  -> lightweight model inference (few ms) using those signals + transaction attributes
  -> score < T_low  -> accept
     score > T_high -> decline
     else           -> 3DS challenge or manual review queue
  -> any signal source down/slow -> fail open to "challenge", never block indefinitely

Follow-ups

  • Where does manual review fit if the decision has to be real-time? Manual review typically happens after acceptance for borderline cases (post-authorization, pre-capture or pre-fulfillment), or the "accept" is provisional with fulfillment gated on review completing within an SLA — genuinely synchronous human review inside a checkout's latency budget isn't realistic.
  • How do you avoid the model itself becoming a latency bottleneck as it grows more complex? Model complexity budgets (inference-time SLAs) as a hard constraint during model development, feature-store precomputation instead of live feature engineering, and possibly a fast simple pre-filter model ahead of a slower more thorough one only for borderline cases.
  • Should scoring happen before or after the PSP's own authorization call? Often both — a merchant-side pre-screen can decline or challenge before even attempting authorization (saving processing costs on clearly-fraudulent attempts), while the PSP/issuer's own risk systems run their own scoring during the authorization itself, layered rather than duplicative.

Common wrong answer: "Call out to a comprehensive third-party fraud-data API synchronously for every transaction to get the most accurate score." A synchronous call to a slow external dependency on the checkout's critical path is exactly the kind of design that blows the latency budget and creates a new single point of failure — the real design principle is precomputing and caching what you can, keeping the hot path to fast local reads plus lightweight inference.

What is "friendly fraud" (referenced in unit 06) from a detection standpoint, and why is it structurally harder to fight with the same tools used for stolen-card fraud?senior

Answer. Friendly fraud is a genuine cardholder disputing a transaction they actually authorized — the payment credential, device, and behavioral signals all look completely legitimate because the customer *is* legitimate; they're either misusing the dispute process opportunistically ("get the item free by disputing after receiving it"), genuinely confused about a charge (unrecognized merchant name, forgotten subscription), or a household member made the purchase without the cardholder's knowledge. Because velocity checks, device fingerprinting, AVS/CVV, and even 3DS all validate "is this the real cardholder," none of them detect friendly fraud at all — the fraud signal here isn't in the transaction, it's in post-transaction behavior (dispute patterns, delivery-confirmation mismatches, customer history of disputes) which requires an entirely different detection approach than pre-authorization risk scoring.

Example

Stolen-card fraud detection: device fingerprint, velocity, AVS/CVV, 3DS -- all target "is this really the cardholder?"
Friendly fraud detection: delivery confirmation + signature capture, dispute-history-per-customer scoring,
                          clear billing-descriptor naming (reduce "I don't recognize this charge" disputes),
                          evidence packages assembled proactively (unit 06's "capture at transaction time")

Follow-ups

  • Does 3DS/SCA help against friendly fraud at all? Marginally — strong authentication at least establishes the cardholder actively participated in the transaction, which can help with representment evidence, but it does nothing to prevent a genuine cardholder from later disputing anyway.
  • How would you quantify how much of a merchant's dispute volume is friendly fraud vs. stolen-card fraud? Represented-and-won disputes are a reasonable proxy for friendly fraud that evidence successfully rebutted; disputes tied to transactions that also triggered pre-authorization risk flags are more likely genuine third-party fraud — imperfect signals, but directionally useful for prioritizing where to invest.
  • What's a concrete, low-friction mitigation a merchant can deploy? A clear, recognizable billing descriptor (the text that appears on the customer's statement) is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost mitigations, since a large share of friendly-fraud disputes start as "I don't recognize this charge" rather than deliberate abuse.

Common wrong answer: "Better fraud-scoring models will catch friendly fraud too." Friendly fraud transactions are, by construction, indistinguishable from legitimate ones at the point of sale — no amount of pre-authorization risk-model improvement addresses it, because the fraud signal only exists in post-transaction dispute behavior, requiring a fundamentally different detection strategy.

A merchant's fraud-decline rate has spiked from 1% to 8% of transactions overnight with no obvious deploy or config change on their end. Walk through how you'd investigate.principal

Answer. First isolate whether declines are happening at the merchant's own risk layer (their rules, their model) or upstream at the issuer/PSP level — segment declines by decline reason code and by which system produced the decision (merchant's own pre-screen vs. PSP's Radar/RevenueProtect-equivalent vs. issuer decline codes coming back on the authorization response) since these have completely different causes and fixes. If it's the merchant's own model/rules: check for a recent (even indirect) change — a feature-store data source going stale or returning malformed values can silently degrade a model's inputs without any code deploy; check the training-data assumptions haven't been invalidated by a real shift in traffic mix (a new marketing campaign driving a different, riskier-looking customer segment that a static threshold now misclassifies). If it's upstream: check whether a specific card BIN range, issuer, geography, or payment method is disproportionately represented in the spike (an issuer-side policy change or a genuinely coordinated fraud attack targeting this merchant can both look like this), and cross-reference against public status pages or PSP support channels for known issues. The investigation should produce a decline-reason-code breakdown and a before/after segment comparison before proposing any fix, exactly because the fix for a stale feature-store looks nothing like the fix for a real fraud attack or an issuer-side policy shift.

Example

Investigation checklist:
1. Segment declines by decision source (merchant rules / PSP risk engine / issuer decline code)
2. Diff decline-reason-code distribution: today vs. 7-day baseline
3. Check feature-store / risk-signal data freshness (silent staleness is common and code-change-free)
4. Segment by BIN range, issuer, geography, payment method for concentration
5. Check for a genuine traffic-mix shift (new campaign, new market) vs. a genuine coordinated attack

Follow-ups

  • What if the spike correlates with a specific BIN range? That's a strong signal of either an issuer-side policy change (worth reaching out to the PSP/issuer about) or a coordinated fraud attack using cards from a specific breach, and the appropriate response differs (adjust routing/escalate vs. tighten rules for that BIN specifically).
  • Should the merchant immediately loosen their rules to recover the decline rate? Not before understanding the cause — loosening rules in the middle of a genuine attack would let real fraud through, while loosening in response to a stale-data bug just masks the underlying issue without fixing it.
  • How does this connect to the "always test in an environment resembling production" theme from webhook testing in unit 05? Similarly, fraud-model/feature-pipeline changes deserve staged rollout and monitoring with automatic rollback triggers, rather than discovering a regression only after it's affected 8% of live transactions.

Common wrong answer: "Loosen the fraud rules until the decline rate goes back to normal." This treats the decline rate as the problem rather than a symptom, and could either mask a real attack (dangerous) or just as easily hide a legitimate data-quality bug that will resurface elsewhere — the correct first move is always diagnosis (segment and compare), not reflexively adjusting the threshold to hit a target number.

What is card-on-file tokenization, and why is it required for recurring billing rather than just storing the raw card number yourself?screener

Answer. Card-on-file tokenization has the PSP or network store the actual card details on the merchant's behalf and hand the merchant back an opaque token (or, at the network level, a "network token" bound to that specific card and merchant relationship) that can be used to initiate future charges without the merchant ever holding raw PAN data — directly enabling the SAQ A-level PCI scope discussed in unit 06 for the merchant's recurring-billing system. Storing the raw card number yourself would pull the entire storage system into full PCI DSS scope (SAQ D) and create a much larger breach liability, for essentially no engineering benefit over using the PSP's tokenization, which is why virtually no modern payments integration stores raw card numbers for recurring use.

Example

// Stripe: SetupIntent captures a card for future use, returns a PaymentMethod ID
const setupIntent = await stripe.setupIntents.create({ customer: custId, payment_method_types: ['card'] });
// later: stripe.paymentIntents.create({ amount, customer: custId, payment_method: pmId, off_session: true, confirm: true })

Follow-ups

  • What's a "network token" specifically, as distinct from a PSP's own internal token? A token issued by the card network itself (Visa/Mastercard token services) bound to the card and merchant, which networks increasingly push because it survives card-detail changes (see the automatic-updater follow-up below) and reduces fraud versus a PSP-only token.
  • Does tokenization eliminate the need for a card-verification step at signup? No — a $0 or small verification authorization (or 3DS challenge for setup, per unit 04's CIT/MIT distinction) is still typically run when a card is first saved, to confirm it's valid and reduce later declined-recurring-charge rates.
  • Is a token merchant-specific or portable across merchants? Merchant-specific in essentially all cases — a token issued to one merchant cannot be used by a different merchant, which is a deliberate security property, not a limitation to work around.

Common wrong answer: "You can store the card number yourself as long as it's encrypted in your database." Encryption alone doesn't remove the system from full PCI DSS scope the way tokenization does (see unit 06's encryption-vs-tokenization distinction) — the entire point of using the PSP's token is to avoid ever holding the raw PAN, encrypted or not.

A customer's card on file expires or is reissued (new number) mid-subscription. What happens, and how do "account updater" services help?mid

Answer. Without intervention, the next recurring charge attempt against the stale token/card details fails, kicking off dunning (retry logic) and, if unresolved, involuntary churn — a large and often underestimated source of subscription revenue loss given how routinely cards expire, get reissued after a breach, or get upgraded (chip reissue, new expiry date). Account updater services, offered by the card networks and integrated through most PSPs, proactively push updated card details (new expiry, new number after reissue) to merchants' stored tokens without requiring the customer to do anything, since the network/issuer already has the new details and the token is designed to reference "this card relationship" rather than a frozen snapshot of the old number. This substantially reduces involuntary churn from card-lifecycle events, though it isn't universal coverage (issuer participation varies) and doesn't help when a customer actively switches to a different card or closes the account.

Example

Without updater: card reissued -> next charge fails -> dunning retries against dead card -> churn
With updater:     card reissued -> network pushes new details to token -> next charge succeeds transparently

Follow-ups

  • Does account updater work for cards that were closed entirely, not just reissued? No — a genuinely closed account with no successor card has nothing for the updater to push; that case still needs the customer to add a new payment method, typically via a dunning email prompting them to do so.
  • Is account updater automatic or does the merchant need to enable it? Typically an opt-in feature at the PSP level that needs to be explicitly enabled and sometimes has its own fee, so it's worth confirming it's actually turned on rather than assumed.
  • How does this interact with the webhook-driven architecture from unit 05? PSPs typically emit a webhook event when a token's underlying card details are updated by the account updater, letting the merchant's system log/audit the change even though no action is required to keep charging.

Common wrong answer: "The customer needs to update their card details manually whenever it expires." That's the fallback experience without account updater, but it's exactly the friction-heavy, high-churn path that account updater services exist to avoid — a mature recurring-billing integration should have this enabled rather than relying on customer-initiated updates as the primary path.

Design "dunning" (failed-payment retry) logic for a subscription business. What variables should the retry schedule consider, and what's the risk of getting it wrong in either direction?senior

Answer. A good dunning schedule varies retry timing and channel by the decline reason (a "insufficient funds" decline benefits from waiting until a plausible payday/billing-cycle boundary rather than retrying immediately; a "card expired" or "do not honor" decline benefits from prompting the customer to update payment info rather than blindly retrying the same doomed charge), and escalates communication (in-app notice, then email, then a firmer "your subscription will be cancelled" warning) in parallel with payment retries rather than silently retrying with no customer visibility. Retrying too aggressively (or on every decline reason indiscriminately) risks tripping issuer-side velocity/fraud flags against the *merchant* (repeated rapid charge attempts against a declining card look like a testing pattern) and irritating customers who see repeated failed-charge notifications; retrying too rarely or giving up too fast increases avoidable involuntary churn on genuinely recoverable declines (temporary insufficient funds is often resolved within days). The schedule should be tuned against measured recovery-rate data per decline reason, not a single one-size-fits-all cadence.

Example

Decline reason -> retry strategy
insufficient_funds     -> retry in 3-5 days, up to 3-4 attempts, aligned near typical pay cycles
expired_card            -> no blind retry; prompt customer to update card immediately
do_not_honor / stolen   -> no retry; treat as a signal to re-verify, possibly risk-review
generic_decline         -> retry once after 24h, then escalate to customer contact

Follow-ups

  • How many total retry attempts is reasonable before cancelling a subscription? Commonly in the range of a few attempts over one to two weeks, but this is a business policy tuned against measured recovery curves, not a fixed universal number — most billing platforms (Stripe Billing's smart retries, for example) provide configurable, data-informed defaults as a starting point.
  • Should a failed recurring charge immediately cut off service access? Usually not immediately — a grace period balances recoverable-decline economics against the risk of extending free access indefinitely; the specific grace period length is again a business tuning question.
  • Does dunning interact with the MIT (merchant-initiated transaction) flagging from unit 04? Yes — a dunning retry is itself an MIT and needs correct flagging/exemption handling per that unit's discussion, since it's an unattended recurring charge, not a customer-present transaction.

Common wrong answer: "Retry the failed charge daily until it succeeds or a fixed number of attempts is reached." A single blind retry cadence applied to every decline reason wastes retries on unrecoverable declines (expired card, stolen card) while potentially under-serving recoverable ones (insufficient funds, better retried near a pay cycle) — and repeated rapid retries against a declining card can itself trigger issuer fraud/velocity flags against the merchant.

A customer upgrades from a $10/month plan to a $30/month plan halfway through their billing cycle. Walk through how proration should work, and where the tricky edge cases are.senior

Answer. Standard proration charges the customer for the unused remainder of the old plan as a credit and the new plan's cost for the remainder of the current period as a charge, netting to one adjustment (e.g. 15 days left on a 30-day cycle: credit $5 for unused half of the $10 plan, charge $15 for the new plan's half-period, net $10 charged immediately) so the customer's next full-cycle bill reflects the new $30 rate going forward. The tricky edges: what happens on a *downgrade* (should the customer get an immediate credit/refund, or just have it applied against the next invoice, since immediate refunds interact with the refund-vs-chargeback economics from unit 06); what happens with usage-based add-ons or metered components that don't prorate as cleanly as a flat monthly fee; and what happens if the plan change happens right at a billing-cycle boundary, where proration calculations can produce near-zero or negative adjustment amounts that need sane handling instead of an attempted $0.01 charge or a confusing negative-amount invoice line. Most billing platforms (Stripe Billing's proration engine, for example) implement this out of the box, but engineers integrating them still need to explicitly decide the downgrade-credit policy and test boundary-timing edge cases themselves.

Example

30-day cycle, upgrade at day 15 (15 days remaining):
  credit: -$5.00  (unused half of $10 plan)
  charge: +$15.00 (new $30 plan, remaining half)
  net immediate charge: $10.00
  next full cycle: $30.00 flat

Follow-ups

  • Should a downgrade prorate immediately or take effect at the next billing cycle? Both are legitimate product choices — immediate downgrade with a credit is more customer-friendly but adds refund-accounting complexity; deferring the downgrade to the next cycle avoids mid-cycle credits entirely at the cost of the customer paying the higher rate a bit longer, and is the simpler default many platforms use.
  • How does proration interact with taxes (VAT/sales tax)? Prorated amounts still need correct tax calculation on the adjusted amount, which is a genuinely fiddly detail many billing-tax integrations (Stripe Tax, Avalara) handle but engineers should verify explicitly rather than assume.
  • What if the plan change happens on the very last day of the cycle? Worth explicit test coverage — near-boundary proration math can produce negligible or even negative net amounts that need a defined floor/rounding policy rather than surfacing a nonsensical invoice line to the customer.

Common wrong answer: "Just charge the new plan's full price immediately and let the next cycle sort itself out." This double-charges the customer for the portion of the current cycle they already paid for under the old plan, which is both unfair to the customer and a predictable source of support tickets and disputes — proration exists specifically to avoid this.

Design the retry/webhook-handling logic for recurring billing so that a subscription's status in your system stays consistent with the PSP's view, even across dunning retries, plan changes, and eventual cancellation.principal

Answer. The PSP is the source of truth for subscription/invoice status (mirroring unit 05's webhook-ingestion design), so the local system should treat its own subscription-status field as a projection updated exclusively from PSP events (`invoice.paid`, `invoice.payment_failed`, `customer.subscription.updated`, `customer.subscription.deleted`, or Adyen's equivalent notifications) rather than from application-code assumptions about what "should" have happened after issuing an API call. Each event needs idempotent, order-tolerant handling (per unit 05) because dunning specifically produces a burst of related events over days-to-weeks for one subscription (multiple `payment_failed` events, then either a recovering `paid` or an eventual `subscription.deleted`), and a handler that doesn't defend against out-of-order or duplicate delivery can flip status incorrectly (e.g. an old failed-payment retry's webhook arriving after a subsequent successful payment's webhook, if delivery order isn't guaranteed). A practical implementation keys off each event's own state and timestamp, only applying a status transition if the incoming event is at least as recent as the last-applied one for that subscription, discarding (but logging) stale events rather than blindly applying whatever arrives.

Example

// simplified stale-event guard
if (event.created < subscription.last_applied_event_at) {
  log('stale event, skipping', event.id); return;
}
applyStatus(subscription, event); // idempotent by event.id, per unit 05 dedup pattern
subscription.last_applied_event_at = event.created;

Follow-ups

  • What if the local system needs to make an access-control decision (e.g. "cut off access now") faster than webhook delivery latency allows? A synchronous check against the PSP's API at the moment of the access decision can supplement the webhook-driven projection for latency-sensitive gates, accepting the extra API call cost for that specific check.
  • Should plan-change requests be applied optimistically in the local system or only after the PSP confirms? Optimistic local UI updates are fine for perceived responsiveness, but the durable status field should still only be considered confirmed once the corresponding webhook lands, exactly mirroring the general principle from unit 05 of the PSP being authoritative.
  • How would you test this without waiting for real dunning cycles to play out? Replay a scripted sequence of the PSP's test-mode webhook events (or the CLI-based event triggering from unit 05) covering the full dunning-to-cancellation lifecycle in a CI test, rather than relying on production timing to exercise these paths.

Common wrong answer: "Update the local subscription status directly whenever the application code makes a plan-change or cancellation API call, since we know what we just did." This ignores the fact that the *actual* outcome (did the charge succeed, did the PSP's own retry logic recover it) is only knowable from what the PSP subsequently reports — treating the local write as authoritative rather than the webhook-confirmed state is exactly how status drift between the merchant's system and the PSP's real subscription state creeps in.

12 Failure modes & incident response

Payments systems fail in ways that are unusually costly to get wrong — a naive retry can double-charge a customer, a naive timeout-handling can silently drop a payment. This unit is where interviewers test operational maturity: does the candidate reach for the safe default under uncertainty, and can they run an incident without making it worse.

Docs: Stripe error handling · Adyen error handling

Your payment API call to the PSP times out — you sent the request but never received a response. What's the correct default behavior, and why?screener

Answer. A timeout means the outcome is genuinely unknown — the PSP may have never received the request, may have processed it successfully with the response lost in transit, or may still be processing it — so the correct default is to treat it as "unknown, needs to be resolved," never as "failed, safe to retry blindly" and never as "succeeded, safe to proceed." The safe resolution path is to query the PSP for the actual status of that specific request using the idempotency key (unit 05) that was sent with the original attempt, which is exactly why idempotency keys are generated client-side before the call and why they matter even for reads-after-timeout, not just retry-safety. Only after confirming the true status (via a status lookup, or via the eventual webhook) should the system decide to retry, mark as failed, or proceed as succeeded — blindly retrying without checking status risks a double charge if the original request actually succeeded server-side.

Example

try {
  const pi = await stripe.paymentIntents.create({ amount, currency, idempotency_key: key });
} catch (err) {
  if (isTimeoutOrNetworkError(err)) {
    // do NOT blindly retry; look up status first
    const status = await stripe.paymentIntents.retrieve(key); // or list by idempotency key
    // decide next step based on actual status, not the fact that the call itself errored
  }
}

Follow-ups

  • Does retrying the exact same request with the same idempotency key ever help here? Yes — if the original request never reached the PSP, retrying with the same key is safe and will simply process it fresh; if it did reach the PSP and succeeded, retrying with the same key returns the original result rather than double-processing, which is the whole point of idempotency keys covering this exact scenario.
  • What if the status lookup itself also times out? This is where exponential backoff with a bounded retry count for the *status check* (not the original mutating call) becomes necessary, and eventually surfacing to manual/alerted intervention if status genuinely can't be determined within a reasonable window.
  • Should the customer see a spinner/pending state during this resolution? Yes, generally — showing a generic error and letting the customer retry an ambiguous-outcome payment risks a duplicate customer-initiated attempt on top of the ambiguous one, so a "processing, please wait" state while the backend resolves the ambiguity is safer UX.

Common wrong answer: "If the call times out, just retry it — if it's not idempotent, that's a bug in the integration." Retrying without first checking status assumes the retry itself is what's making it safe, but the real safety comes from confirming actual status (or relying on the idempotency key covering the retry) — and even with idempotency keys correctly used, jumping straight to retry without any status-check discipline is a worse default than resolving the ambiguity first.

A PSP has a partial outage: authorization succeeds but capture calls are failing. What should a well-designed payment system do, and what should it avoid doing?mid

Answer. The system should queue capture attempts for retry with backoff rather than failing the whole order outright, since the authorization already succeeded and most authorization holds remain valid for several days (the exact window depends on the card network and issuer per unit 02's discussion), giving genuine room to retry captures once the PSP recovers rather than needing to re-authorize from scratch. It should avoid re-running authorization again as a workaround (creating a duplicate hold on the customer's card, confusing them and needlessly consuming their available credit), avoid failing the order/cancelling it reflexively (losing a sale that's actually recoverable), and avoid a tight synchronous retry loop that hammers an already-degraded PSP. The retry queue should also have a defined ceiling tied to the authorization's actual expiry window, past which capture is no longer possible and the order genuinely needs to be treated as failed and the customer notified.

Example

auth succeeds (t=0)      -> hold placed, valid ~7 days (network/issuer dependent)
capture fails (t=0)      -> enqueue retry with backoff, do not re-authorize
capture retries succeed (t=+2h) -> order proceeds normally
if still failing near hold expiry -> escalate to manual/alerted handling, consider re-authorization as a deliberate, informed decision

Follow-ups

  • How would you detect a PSP-wide outage vs. an isolated capture failure for one order? A spike in capture failure rate across many unrelated orders in a short window is the signal — monitoring failure-rate-by-error-type at the aggregate level (not just per-order alerting) is what surfaces "this is systemic" quickly.
  • Should the multi-PSP failover from unit 03 apply here? Only carefully — failing over a *capture* for an already-authorized transaction to a different PSP doesn't work (the authorization hold lives with the original PSP/acquirer); failover is more applicable to routing *new* transactions away from the degraded PSP, not rescuing in-flight ones.
  • What's the customer-facing messaging while captures are queued? Something honest but not alarming ("payment confirmed, order processing") is usually appropriate since the authorization did succeed — the customer shouldn't be told the payment failed when it's genuinely just pending capture.

Common wrong answer: "Cancel the order and ask the customer to try again." This throws away a perfectly good authorization hold and pushes the burden onto the customer for a problem that's entirely on the PSP's side, when the actually recoverable path (queue-and-retry the capture within the hold's validity window) would complete the original order without customer involvement at all.

Design an incident-response runbook specifically for "payments are failing at an elevated rate" — what should the first 15 minutes look like?senior

Answer. First, confirm the signal is real and scope it: is the elevated failure rate across all payment methods/PSPs or isolated to one (pointing at a PSP-side issue vs. a merchant-side bug), all regions or one, all card networks/issuers or a concentrated subset (an issuer-side or BIN-range-specific issue looks very different from a systemic one). Second, check the PSP's own status page and any recent deploys on the merchant's own side within the failure window — a correlated deploy is the single highest-value clue and should be checked before anything else, since "we just shipped something" is disproportionately often the actual cause. Third, based on scope: if PSP-wide, this is a "monitor and wait" or "invoke failover" situation depending on unit 03's routing setup, not something the merchant's engineers can fix directly; if isolated to the merchant's own integration, this is a rollback-or-hotfix situation. Throughout, communicate status to stakeholders (support team fielding customer complaints, business stakeholders tracking revenue impact) on a regular cadence rather than going silent while investigating, and capture a timeline as you go for the postmortem rather than reconstructing it afterward from memory.

Example

First 15 minutes:
0-3 min:  confirm signal real; scope by PSP/method/region/BIN
3-7 min:  check PSP status page + merchant's own deploy log for correlated changes
7-12 min: decide path -- PSP-side (monitor/failover) vs. merchant-side (rollback/hotfix)
12-15 min: post initial status update to stakeholders; begin timeline log

Follow-ups

  • What's the single most valuable dashboard/metric to have pre-built for this scenario? A failure-rate breakdown by dimension (PSP, payment method, region, decline-reason code) over time, so scoping in step one is a lookup rather than an ad hoc query written under pressure.
  • Should a rollback happen before root cause is fully understood? Yes, generally — if a recent deploy correlates strongly with the failure spike, rolling back is usually lower-risk than continuing to investigate live in production, and root-causing can continue against the rolled-back state afterward.
  • How does this differ from a generic API-outage runbook? Payments failures have direct, immediate revenue and customer-trust impact that most other outages don't, which is why the stakeholder-communication cadence and the failover-vs-fix decision tree deserve to be specific to payments rather than reused wholesale from a generic incident template.

Common wrong answer: "Start debugging the code immediately to find the root cause." Jumping straight to root-cause debugging without first scoping the failure (which PSP, method, region) risks spending the first critical minutes chasing the wrong hypothesis entirely — scoping first is what tells you whether you're even looking in the right codebase.

What's the risk of a payment system that retries failed operations using an exponential backoff without jitter, specifically in a payments context?mid

Answer. Without jitter, many clients that failed at roughly the same time (say, during a brief PSP blip affecting a batch of concurrent requests) back off on synchronized schedules and then retry in synchronized waves, creating a "thundering herd" that can overwhelm the PSP right as it's recovering, extending the outage or triggering rate-limiting that then affects legitimate new traffic too. In a payments context this is especially costly because retry storms during a partial outage compound exactly the capture-retry and idempotency-key scenarios discussed earlier in this unit — a synchronized retry wave hitting rate limits doesn't just delay recovery, it can cause spurious idempotency-key collisions/timeouts that then need their own resolution. Adding randomized jitter to the backoff interval spreads retries out in time, which is standard practice for any distributed retry logic but particularly important where the downstream dependency (a PSP under partial outage) is shared across a very large number of concurrent callers.

Example

// without jitter: all clients failing at t=0 retry at t=1s, t=2s, t=4s... in lockstep
// with jitter:
const backoff = baseDelay * Math.pow(2, attempt);
const jittered = backoff * (0.5 + Math.random() * 0.5); // spread retries across a range

Follow-ups

  • Does this interact with the idempotency-key retry-safety-limit discussion from unit 05? Directly — the retry-count/backoff policy discussed there needs jitter as a standard component, not just a longer max-wait, to avoid the thundering-herd failure mode compounding a partial outage.
  • Is jitter relevant for the webhook-delivery side too, or just outbound API calls? Both — PSPs jittering their own webhook redelivery attempts (which they generally do) is exactly why a merchant's webhook handler needs to be idempotent and order-tolerant regardless of when redeliveries actually land.
  • How would you size the jitter range? Commonly a percentage of the base backoff interval (e.g. ±50%) rather than a fixed absolute value, so it scales appropriately as the backoff grows across retry attempts.

Common wrong answer: "Exponential backoff alone is sufficient to avoid overwhelming a recovering service." Exponential backoff without jitter still synchronizes retries across many clients that failed together, which is precisely the thundering-herd pattern jitter exists to break — backoff and jitter solve related but distinct problems and both are needed.

Design monitoring/alerting for a production payments system such that a real incident is caught within minutes, without so much alert noise that engineers start ignoring pages.principal

Answer. The key design move is alerting on customer-impact-correlated business metrics (overall payment success rate, success rate segmented by PSP/method/region, checkout-to-payment conversion) with statistically-informed thresholds (deviation from a rolling baseline accounting for normal daily/weekly seasonality, not a flat percentage), rather than alerting on every individual technical error or on raw counts that don't account for traffic volume changes. This should be layered: a small number of high-urgency pages for metrics that clearly indicate broad customer impact (e.g. overall success rate dropping more than N standard deviations below the seasonal baseline for M consecutive minutes), and a larger set of lower-urgency, non-paging dashboards/tickets for narrower anomalies (one specific decline-reason code trending up, one specific BIN range's success rate dipping) that a human reviews on a normal cadence rather than getting woken up for. Alert fatigue is a real reliability risk in payments specifically because a genuinely critical alert arriving in an inbox already full of low-signal noise is easy to miss or dismiss, which is worse than having fewer, well-calibrated alerts.

Example

Page (high urgency): overall payment success rate < baseline - 3*stddev for 5+ consecutive minutes
Page (high urgency): any single PSP's success rate drops >20 percentage points vs. its own 1h-ago baseline
Ticket (low urgency): one decline-reason code's share trending up over 24h, no acute drop yet
Dashboard-only: per-BIN-range, per-country granular breakdowns for investigation, not alerting

Follow-ups

  • How do you set a "seasonal baseline" for something like payment volume that varies by time of day and day of week? A rolling comparison against the same time-of-week window over recent weeks (e.g. "this Tuesday 2pm vs. the last 4 Tuesdays at 2pm") handles most seasonality better than a flat historical average across all time.
  • Should synthetic/canary transactions be part of this design? Yes — periodic small real (or sandboxed, where the PSP supports it) test transactions catch end-to-end breakage (including in paths with naturally low real traffic, like a rarely-used payment method) that pure metric-threshold alerting on live traffic alone might miss until enough real customers hit it.
  • How does this relate to the fraud-decline investigation scenario from unit 10? That scenario is exactly the kind of anomaly this monitoring design should surface quickly (an 8x decline-rate spike) rather than requiring a customer complaint or manual dashboard check to first notice it.

Common wrong answer: "Alert on every payment error and let engineers triage what matters." This produces alert fatigue almost immediately in any system with non-trivial traffic, since individual technical errors (a single timeout, a single card decline) are constant background noise in payments — the correct design alerts on aggregate, business-impact-correlated deviations from baseline, not on raw error occurrence.

13 Regulatory landscape & compliance

Beyond PCI DSS (unit 06) and PSD2/PSD3 (unit 04), payments engineers need working familiarity with AML/KYC obligations and the general shape of how payments regulation differs across jurisdictions — not to replace legal/compliance teams, but to recognize when a design decision has regulatory weight and needs their input.

Docs: Stripe global payments guide · FinCEN Bank Secrecy Act (US AML reference)

What are KYC (Know Your Customer) and AML (Anti-Money Laundering) obligations, and where do they typically intersect with a payments engineer's actual work?screener

Answer. KYC requires verifying the identity of customers (or, for a platform/marketplace, the identity of the sellers/sub-merchants onboarding to receive funds) before allowing them to transact meaningfully, typically collecting government ID, business registration documents, and beneficial-ownership information; AML requires monitoring transaction activity for patterns consistent with money laundering (structuring, rapid movement of funds through multiple accounts, transactions inconsistent with a customer's stated business) and filing reports with regulators when required. For most payments engineers, this shows up concretely as: building or integrating an onboarding flow that collects and verifies the required KYC documents (often via a dedicated KYC/identity-verification API rather than building verification logic in-house), and building transaction-monitoring hooks that flag suspicious patterns for compliance-team review, not as making the underlying legal judgment calls (those belong to compliance/legal, who define what "suspicious" means for the specific business and jurisdiction).

Example

Marketplace onboarding a new seller (unit 06's minimal-scope marketplace design):
  KYC: collect legal name, tax ID, government ID, business registration -> verify via provider (e.g. Stripe Identity)
  AML: ongoing monitoring of the seller's transaction volume/pattern against expected profile, flagged for compliance review

Follow-ups

  • Does a simple e-commerce merchant (not a marketplace) need to run KYC on their own customers? Generally not directly — that obligation typically sits with the merchant's acquirer/PSP, who KYC'd the *merchant* during onboarding; a marketplace/platform paying out to many sub-merchants takes on KYC obligations for those sub-merchants specifically because it's acting more like a payment facilitator.
  • How does this relate to the "merchant of record" concept from unit 06? Directly — who bears KYC/AML obligations for a given flow of funds tracks closely with who is legally the merchant of record for that transaction, which is exactly why that designation matters beyond just dispute liability.
  • What's a "PEP" or "sanctions screening" check? Screening against Politically Exposed Persons lists and government sanctions lists (e.g. OFAC in the US) is a standard component of KYC/AML programs, typically automated via the same identity-verification providers, flagging matches for compliance review rather than auto-blocking on every screen hit (which would produce significant false positives given common-name collisions).

Common wrong answer: "KYC/AML is a legal/compliance department problem, not something engineering needs to understand." Engineering builds the actual onboarding flows, verification integrations, and monitoring hooks that implement these obligations — not understanding the requirements risks building an onboarding flow or data model that can't actually satisfy what compliance needs, discovered only once regulators or auditors ask for something the system was never designed to produce.

Why do payment regulations differ so much by jurisdiction (EU's PSD2/PSD3, US's more fragmented state-and-federal approach, and others), and what does this mean practically for a global payments product?mid

Answer. The EU regulates payments through comprehensive, harmonized directives/regulations (PSD2, soon PSD3/PSR per unit 04) applied consistently across member states via a single licensing passport (an entity licensed in one EU state can generally operate across the bloc), reflecting the EU's broader single-market regulatory approach. The US instead layers federal law (Bank Secrecy Act/AML, Regulation E for consumer protections, card network rules) with state-level money-transmitter licensing requirements that differ state by state, producing a more fragmented compliance landscape where a US-focused payments business often needs separate licensing or partnership arrangements per state rather than one unified authorization. Practically, this means a "global payments" product can't have one compliance design — the SCA/3DS-mandatory posture that makes sense as a default for EU traffic (unit 04's discussion) would be commercially counterproductive if defaulted for US traffic where SCA isn't mandated, and a platform operating in both needs genuinely region-aware compliance logic, licensing arrangements, and often local banking/acquiring partnerships rather than one codebase with a single global ruleset.

Example

EU: PSD2/PSR, SCA broadly required, single passportable license across member states
US: BSA/AML (federal) + money-transmitter licensing (state-by-state) + card network rules, no SCA mandate
=> region-aware config, not one global default, for both risk posture and legal operating structure

Follow-ups

  • How do most payments products handle this without becoming licensed money transmitters themselves in every jurisdiction? By operating as an agent/user of an already-licensed PSP or banking-as-a-service provider that holds the relevant licenses, rather than seeking direct licensure themselves — this is a large part of what a PSP/BaaS relationship is actually providing beyond the technical API.
  • Does this affect where a company should incorporate or headquarter its payments operations? It's a genuine factor in that decision for payments-heavy businesses, though ultimately a legal/corporate-structure decision informed by, not made by, engineering.
  • Are there efforts to harmonize this globally? Some (e.g. open-banking-style frameworks spreading beyond the EU, ISO 20022 messaging standardization), but full harmonization remains far off, and a working payments engineer should expect meaningful jurisdictional divergence to persist for the foreseeable future.

Common wrong answer: "Compliance requirements are basically the same everywhere, just enforced by different regulators." The EU's harmonized-directive model and the US's fragmented federal/state model are structurally different, not just administratively different — a design (SCA defaults, licensing strategy, data-handling requirements) that's correct for one can be actively wrong or non-compliant for the other.

What is a "money transmitter license" and why does a platform that lets users hold balances and pay each other (not just pay a single merchant) often need one, or need to structure around not needing one?senior

Answer. A money transmitter license is a regulatory authorization (in the US, largely state-by-state, per the prior question) required of entities that hold or transmit funds on behalf of others as a core part of their business — a marketplace or platform that lets one user pay another, holds user balances, or enables peer-to-peer transfers is functionally acting as a money transmitter, distinct from a typical merchant simply accepting payment for its own goods/services. Because obtaining and maintaining money-transmitter licenses across every relevant jurisdiction is expensive and slow, most platforms instead structure their product on top of an already-licensed partner (a "payment facilitator" or "platform" arrangement with a PSP like Stripe Connect or Adyen for Platforms, per unit 06's marketplace discussion) that holds the licenses and lets the platform operate as its agent, rather than becoming directly licensed themselves. The engineering implication is that the specific arrangement (which entity is legally the money transmitter, what activities the platform itself is and isn't permitted to perform, like directly holding customer funds versus only initiating movements through the licensed partner) constrains what the platform's own systems are legally allowed to do with user balances, not just how they're technically implemented.

Example

Simple merchant: sells own goods, accepts payment for itself -> no money-transmitter license needed
P2P/marketplace platform: holds balances, moves funds between users -> money-transmitter activity
  -> typically structured via a licensed PSP's platform product (Stripe Connect, Adyen for Platforms)
     rather than the platform itself becoming directly licensed

Follow-ups

  • Does this affect the ledger design from unit 07? Yes — whether the platform's ledger represents genuinely held customer funds (requiring the money-transmitter-compliant structure) versus merely a receivable/payable record against a licensed partner who actually holds the funds is a real distinction the ledger's chart of accounts should reflect accurately, not gloss over.
  • What's "custodial" vs. "non-custodial" fund holding in this context? Custodial means the platform itself holds/controls customer funds directly (triggering the heaviest regulatory obligations); non-custodial arrangements route actual fund custody through the licensed partner while the platform only orchestrates movements — a meaningful distinction platform architects need to get right with legal input.
  • Is this the same issue as PCI scope from unit 06? Related in spirit (both are about minimizing what a platform directly handles/holds to avoid unnecessary regulatory burden) but legally distinct regimes — PCI is a card-network contractual security standard, money-transmitter licensing is government financial regulation.

Common wrong answer: "As long as the platform uses a PSP for the actual payment processing, it doesn't need to worry about money transmitter licensing." Using a PSP for processing individual charges doesn't automatically cover P2P/marketplace fund-holding and transfer activity — that requires the specific platform/payment-facilitator arrangement (like Stripe Connect) designed for this, and the platform's legal team needs to confirm the actual structure covers the actual activity, not just assume any PSP relationship is sufficient.

A payments platform is expanding into a new country. What regulatory and compliance questions should the engineering team expect to need answered before writing code, and why can't engineering just proceed with the existing global design?principal

Answer. Before building, engineering typically needs answers to: what licensing structure covers operating in this country (does the existing PSP/BaaS partner already have coverage there, or is a new partnership/license needed), what local payment methods are expected/required for competitive parity (per unit 08's method-proliferation discussion, now with a compliance dimension — some local methods have their own regulatory requirements), what data-residency or data-localization rules apply (some jurisdictions require certain customer/transaction data to be stored or processed within the country), what KYC/AML documentation requirements differ locally (acceptable ID types, business registration formats), and what consumer-protection/dispute-resolution rules apply (refund-timing requirements, mandatory disclosures) that may differ from the platform's existing defaults. Engineering can't just proceed with the existing global design because payments compliance is jurisdiction-specific by nature (per the earlier EU/US question in this unit) — a design that's fully compliant in the platform's home market can be silently non-compliant elsewhere in ways that don't fail loudly (no error, no test failure) until a regulator, auditor, or dispute reveals the gap, at which point remediation is far more expensive than building it correctly from the start.

Example

Pre-launch checklist for a new country:
1. Licensing coverage: does existing PSP/BaaS partner cover this jurisdiction?
2. Required local payment methods and their specific integration/compliance needs
3. Data residency/localization requirements
4. Local KYC/AML documentation and verification requirements
5. Local consumer-protection rules (refund timing, disclosure requirements)
6. Tax/reporting obligations (VAT, local transaction reporting)

Follow-ups

  • Who should own this checklist — engineering, legal, or product? Legal/compliance owns the actual determinations, but engineering needs to be a stakeholder early because several answers (data residency in particular) can require architectural changes that are far cheaper to design in from the start than to retrofit.
  • How does data residency specifically affect system design? It can require per-region data storage/processing boundaries (a region-partitioned database or infrastructure deployment) that a single-global-database architecture wasn't built for, echoing the general lesson that compliance requirements can be genuine architectural constraints, not just paperwork.
  • Should this checklist be run even for a country the platform expects very little volume in initially? Yes — regulatory obligations generally attach based on operating in a jurisdiction at all, not based on volume, so "we'll only have a few customers there at first" doesn't reduce the compliance requirement, even though it might reasonably affect the build's prioritization and polish.

Common wrong answer: "Since the platform already handles payments compliantly in several countries, expanding to one more is mostly a localization (language/currency) task." This treats compliance as if it generalizes across jurisdictions the way UI localization does, when in fact each jurisdiction can impose genuinely new legal requirements (licensing, data residency, local KYC rules) that have nothing to do with language or currency and everything to do with a distinct regulatory regime needing its own review.

14 Judgment calls

These don't have a single correct answer — they're the questions a principal-level loop uses to see how you weigh competing goods (conversion vs. risk, consistency vs. speed, build vs. buy) and whether you can articulate a defensible position rather than a reflexive one. Each item below sketches the tension and what a strong answer engages with, not a verdict.

Should a payments team default to always requiring 3DS/SCA globally (unit 04's "always-request" pattern), even in markets where it isn't mandated?

The case for: consistent fraud/liability posture, one code path instead of a region-forked one, and issuers increasingly reward always-authenticated traffic with better approval rates even outside PSD2 jurisdictions. The case against: added friction measurably reduces conversion in markets with no regulatory or liability upside to justify it, and a global default optimizes the EU case at a real cost everywhere else. A strong answer engages with the actual trade-off quantitatively (what's this business's conversion sensitivity, what's its fraud exposure) rather than picking a side on principle, and typically lands on a risk-based/region-aware default with an escape hatch, not a single global switch either way.

When a fraud model's false-positive rate rises, is it better to tune the model or to add friction (3DS challenge, manual review) for borderline cases?

Tuning the model addresses the root cause but takes time and carries the risk of overcorrecting into more false negatives; adding friction is fast to deploy and reversible but degrades the experience for exactly the legitimate customers already at risk of being misclassified. A strong answer recognizes these aren't mutually exclusive — friction is often the right short-term lever while a model retrain is validated offline against historical data before shipping, rather than either "just retrain" (too slow for an active problem) or "just add friction everywhere" (a permanent tax on conversion for what should be a temporary fix).

Is it acceptable for a payments system to occasionally double-process a webhook side effect (e.g. send a duplicate "payment received" email) as long as money movement itself stays exactly-once?

A strict interpretation says every side effect should be deduplicated, full stop. A pragmatic interpretation distinguishes side effects by consequence: a duplicate email is an annoyance; a duplicate ledger entry or duplicate fulfillment is a financial or operational loss. Engineering effort spent guaranteeing exactly-once delivery for every downstream effect (not just money-moving ones) has real cost and diminishing returns. A strong answer names this distinction explicitly and argues for prioritizing dedup effort by consequence severity, while still preferring dedup-by-default (per unit 03) wherever it's cheap, rather than treating "some duplication is tolerable" as license to skip idempotency work broadly.

Should a startup building its first payments integration reach for a full orchestration platform (unit 01) from day one, or start with a single PSP and migrate later if needed?

Starting with orchestration front-loads cost and complexity the startup may never need if it stays single-PSP-viable, and locks in architectural decisions before the actual failure-rate/routing-need data exists to justify them. Starting single-PSP is faster to ship but risks a costly mid-flight migration if multi-PSP need materializes under real production pressure (an outage, a geographic expansion) rather than on a planned timeline. A strong answer favors starting single-PSP with clean abstraction boundaries that don't preclude adding orchestration later, treating premature orchestration as a real anti-pattern (over-engineering for a scale problem that doesn't exist yet) while acknowledging the migration cost is real and worth minimizing through the initial abstraction design.

How much should a payments engineer trust a PSP's own documentation and SDK defaults versus independently verifying behavior against the actual API?

Documentation and SDKs are usually accurate and re-deriving everything independently wastes time better spent elsewhere. But payments-specific behavior (exact retry semantics, exact webhook ordering guarantees, exact idempotency-key scoping) has high enough consequence-of-being-wrong that blind trust is risky, and documentation does go stale or have edge cases undocumented. A strong answer calibrates by consequence: trust documented behavior for low-stakes integration details, but write an actual test against the sandbox/test-mode API for anything the ledger or a customer-facing money amount depends on (a live "does this idempotency key actually behave as documented under concurrent requests" test, for instance) rather than assuming.

Is a chargeback-rate monitoring threshold (unit 06) something engineering should optimize for directly, or is that solely a fraud/risk team's KPI?

Treating it as purely a risk-team KPI risks engineering shipping features (new payment methods, looser fraud rules for conversion) without accounting for chargeback-rate impact until the risk team escalates after the fact. Treating it as an engineering KPI to directly optimize risks over-indexing on a lagging, narrow metric at the expense of product goals engineering is actually accountable for. A strong answer argues for shared visibility (engineering sees the metric, understands roughly what drives it) without engineering owning the target the way the risk team does — similar to how a backend team might watch error-budget burn without owning the SLO itself.

When designing a ledger (unit 07), should correctness (never losing a cent) be pursued even at meaningful cost to system availability, or is eventual reconciliation an acceptable trade for keeping the system responsive under load?

Strict correctness (e.g. synchronous double-entry writes with strong consistency guarantees on every transaction) can become a genuine availability/latency bottleneck under load. Favoring availability with asynchronous, eventually-reconciled ledger writes risks a window where the ledger doesn't yet reflect reality, and if reconciliation logic has bugs, that window can hide real loss rather than just delay its visibility. A strong answer distinguishes the money-movement path (which usually should stay strongly consistent, since that's genuinely non-negotiable) from ancillary ledger views/reporting (which can tolerate eventual consistency more readily), rather than treating "the ledger" as one monolithic consistency requirement.

Should a merchant disclose to customers when Dynamic Currency Conversion (unit 09) or a similar potentially-unfavorable-to-the-customer feature is being offered, beyond the legal minimum required?

Legal minimum disclosure protects the business from regulatory risk at the lowest cost; going beyond it (clearer language, an explicit "you may get a better rate by declining this") sacrifices some of the feature's commercial upside for the sake of customer trust. A strong answer recognizes this is fundamentally a values/brand question the business needs to decide, not a purely legal or technical one, but that engineering building the disclosure UI has some agency in how clear (or how deliberately easy-to-miss) that disclosure ends up being, and that choice has real reputational tail risk if the pattern is later scrutinized publicly.

If a PSP's API and a card network's own published rules seem to disagree on a specific behavior (e.g. exact 3DS exemption eligibility), which do you build against?

The PSP's API is what your code actually calls and what determines your immediate integration behavior, so pragmatically you build against what the PSP's API actually does. But the card network's rules are the underlying authority the PSP is supposed to be implementing, and a PSP can have bugs or lag behind rule changes (as seen with EMV 3DS version transitions in unit 04). A strong answer builds against observed PSP behavior for day-to-day integration decisions, but flags the discrepancy to the PSP (and, for anything consequential, verifies independently against current scheme documentation) rather than silently assuming the PSP is always correctly implementing the underlying rule.

How much should a payments team invest in supporting payment methods (unit 08) with low current usage but plausible future demand, versus waiting for demonstrated demand before building?

Building ahead of demand risks wasted engineering effort on a method that never gains traction, plus ongoing maintenance cost for something barely used. Waiting for demonstrated demand risks losing customers who wanted that method during the window before it existed, and reacting late to a competitor who got there first. A strong answer looks for lower-cost ways to test demand signal before committing full engineering investment (a waitlist, a "notify me" option, or leveraging a PSP's dynamic-payment-method feature to add a method with minimal custom code first) rather than treating it as a binary build-now-or-never decision.

15 Design exercises

Whiteboard-style prompts a principal loop might spend 30-45 minutes on. Read the prompt, ask the clarifying questions you'd actually ask before designing, then open the solution outline to compare notes — not to check whether you matched it exactly, but whether you covered the same load-bearing concerns.

Design a payment-processing service for an e-commerce platform that needs to accept cards, handle webhooks, and support refunds — assume a single PSP (Stripe-shaped) for now, but design so a second PSP could plausibly be added later without a rewrite.

Clarifying questions to ask

  • What's the expected transaction volume and peak QPS, and does that change the idempotency/retry design?
  • Is this a marketplace (multiple sellers, split payments) or a single merchant selling its own goods?
  • What's the fulfillment model — does fulfillment wait for capture, or can it proceed on authorization alone?
  • Are there existing systems (order management, ledger) this needs to integrate with, or is this greenfield?
Solution outline & trade-offs

Core components: a payment-attempt API layer that generates and enforces idempotency keys client-side (unit 02) before calling the PSP; a webhook ingestion pipeline with fast-ack, signature verification, and async processing into a durable event log keyed by PSP event ID for dedup (unit 03); a payment/order status projection that's updated exclusively from confirmed webhook events, never from the synchronous API-call return value alone; and a refund API that records refund intent locally before calling the PSP, mirroring the same idempotency discipline as the original charge.

To keep a second PSP addable later without a rewrite: define an internal "PaymentProvider" interface (create charge, capture, refund, parse webhook) that the Stripe-specific code implements, and make sure the payment-status state machine and idempotency-key generation live above that interface, not inside the Stripe-specific implementation — so a second provider only needs a new adapter, not changes to the core status/idempotency logic. Explicitly avoid designing a full smart-routing/orchestration layer (unit 01's build-vs-buy discussion) up front; that's premature until there's an actual second PSP and observed routing need.

Trade-offs worth naming out loud: the abstraction layer adds indirection cost now for a benefit (easy second-PSP addition) that may never be needed — a reasonable, bounded amount of that cost is worth paying (a clean interface boundary), but a fully generic multi-PSP orchestration engine built speculatively is over-engineering for a one-PSP system.

Design the reconciliation system for a payments platform that needs to verify, daily, that every dollar the PSP says it moved matches what the internal ledger recorded — and alert a human when it doesn't.

Clarifying questions to ask

  • Does the platform operate in multiple currencies, and if so, is FX conversion involved in the reconciliation (unit 09)?
  • What's the PSP's reporting mechanism — a downloadable settlement file, an API, or both — and how fresh is it?
  • What's an acceptable alerting threshold — any discrepancy, or only ones above some materiality amount?
  • Should this system also handle payout reconciliation (unit 07's marketplace payout scenario), or just incoming payments?
Solution outline & trade-offs

Core design: a scheduled job (daily, or more frequent if volume/risk warrants) that pulls the PSP's settlement report for the period, matches each PSP-reported transaction against the internal ledger by a shared identifier (the PSP's own transaction/charge ID, stored on the ledger entry at transaction time, never re-derived after the fact), and classifies the result into matched, missing-in-ledger, missing-in-PSP-report, and amount-mismatch buckets (unit 07's break taxonomy). Matched entries need no action; every other bucket gets written to a review queue with enough context (both records side by side) for a human to investigate without re-querying multiple systems.

For multi-currency: reconciliation needs to compare within the settlement currency directly (not attempt to reconcile a converted amount against a differently-converted amount), using the persisted FX snapshot (unit 09) for any comparison that does need to cross currencies, rather than re-fetching a current rate that would produce a spurious mismatch purely from rate drift.

Trade-offs: running reconciliation more frequently (hourly vs. daily) surfaces breaks faster but multiplies job complexity and cost for marginal benefit if breaks are rare; alerting on every discrepancy including sub-cent rounding differences creates noise that trains people to ignore the alert (unit 12's alert-fatigue theme) — a materiality threshold for paging, with all discrepancies still logged and reviewable, is usually the right balance.

Design a subscription billing system that needs to support plan upgrades/downgrades with proration, handle failed-payment dunning, and stay consistent with the PSP's view of each subscription's status — for a B2B SaaS product with monthly and annual plans.

Clarifying questions to ask

  • Should downgrades take effect immediately (with a credit) or at the next billing cycle (unit 11)?
  • Does the product have usage-based/metered components in addition to flat plan fees?
  • What access-control latency is acceptable when a subscription lapses — does it need to gate access in real time, or is eventual (webhook-driven) consistency acceptable?
  • Are there existing customers being migrated onto this system, or is this greenfield for new signups only?
Solution outline & trade-offs

Core design: use the PSP's subscription/billing primitives (Stripe Billing or equivalent) rather than hand-rolling recurring-charge scheduling, since proration math, invoice generation, and dunning retry scheduling are exactly the kind of well-trodden, easy-to-get-subtly-wrong logic worth not reimplementing (echoing unit 01's build-vs-buy theme). The local system's subscription-status field is a projection updated exclusively from webhook events (unit 11's consistency design), with a stale-event guard keyed by event timestamp to handle dunning's characteristic burst of out-of-order-risk events over days-to-weeks.

For access control: default to webhook-driven eventual consistency (typically sub-minute in practice) for cutting off access on cancellation/final dunning failure, since a short grace window is usually acceptable for a B2B SaaS context; if the specific business genuinely needs real-time gating (unlikely but worth confirming per the clarifying question), supplement with a synchronous status check at the point of the access decision rather than redesigning the whole system around low-latency consistency.

Trade-offs: leaning entirely on the PSP's billing primitives reduces custom code but couples the product's plan/pricing model tightly to what the PSP's billing API can express — a sufficiently unusual pricing model (complex usage tiers, unusual proration rules) might force either awkward workarounds against the PSP's model or a partial custom-billing-engine build, which is a real cost worth surfacing explicitly rather than assuming the PSP's primitives always fit.

16 Night-before cheat sheet

One sentence each — the things to have loaded in working memory walking into the room, not a study plan.

  • Idempotency keys must be generated client-side before the first attempt, scoped to one logical operation, and reused verbatim on every retry — never regenerated on retry, and never assume a request that timed out failed (look up its status before retrying).
  • Webhooks are at-least-once and out-of-order: verify the signature against the raw body before parsing, dedupe by the PSP's event ID, fast-ack within seconds and process asynchronously, and never trust a webhook whose signature you haven't checked.
  • 3DS/SCA liability shift attaches specifically to authenticated (frictionless or challenge-completed) transactions; PSD2 exemptions (low-value, TRA, recurring/MIT, trusted beneficiary) reduce friction but at the cost of giving up that liability shift.
  • PCI DSS scope is something you engineer down (tokenization, hosted fields/redirect, never touching raw PAN) rather than something you audit your way out of after the fact; SAQ A beats SAQ D by architecture, not by paperwork.
  • Encryption and tokenization are not interchangeable for PCI purposes: encrypted PAN in your database still puts that system in scope; a PSP-issued token does not.
  • A chargeback and a refund are different mechanisms with different economics — a refund is voluntary and closes cleanly; a dispute has a reason code, a representment deadline, and a chargeback-rate consequence if you lose too many.
  • The ledger should be double-entry and driven by confirmed PSP events, not by what your application code assumes happened after issuing an API call — the PSP's webhook-confirmed state is the source of truth, your local status field is a projection of it.
  • Settlement lag means "money received" (webhook-confirmed) and "cash in the bank" (actual settlement) are different moments and need different ledger accounts (receivable vs. cash) to reconcile correctly.
  • Retry logic on the hot path needs exponential backoff with jitter, not just backoff — synchronized retries from many clients failing together can thundering-herd a recovering PSP.
  • When diagnosing a spike (decline rate, failure rate, chargeback rate), scope by dimension (PSP, method, region, BIN, decline-reason code) and check for a correlated deploy before touching any threshold or rule — diagnosis before adjustment, always.
