System design (.NET full-stack)

study · .NET 10.0 LTS · released 2025-11-11

System design has no version of its own, so this guide pins itself to the platform a .NET full-stack engineer actually designs on today: .NET 10 LTS (11 Nov 2025, supported to Nov 2028) with C# 14 and EF Core 10, Aspire 13.5 (Aug 2026) for local composition and deployment, PostgreSQL 18 and SQL Server 2025 as the two relational defaults, Kafka 4.x (KRaft-only since 4.0, Mar 2025) for streaming, Kubernetes 1.37 underneath, and React 19.2 with a stable React Compiler 1.0 in the browser. The durable half of the topic — queueing theory, replication, isolation levels, idempotency, failure modes — has not moved in a decade and is what interviews actually test; the fast-moving half is only which primitive you now get for free instead of building. Two changes are recent enough to be worth naming out loud: PostgreSQL 18 shipped native uuidv7() and an asynchronous I/O subsystem (25 Sep 2025), quietly retiring a decade of "never use GUID primary keys" advice, and .NET 11 (Preview 7 on 11 Aug 2026, GA 10 Nov 2026) makes async a runtime concept rather than a compiler rewrite while turning on the NativeAOT CLI and the MSBuild server by default. Everything here is written for a lead/principal candidate: the reasoning you narrate at the whiteboard, not just the conclusion. Assumption: "system design" means the interview discipline and the judgment behind it — greenfield rounds, deep dives on an existing system, and the operational follow-ups — scoped to a full-stack .NET engineer rather than to any one employer's stack.

01How to run the design round

A system design round is not a knowledge test, it is a simulated design meeting with a peer, and it is scored on the meeting, not the diagram. The interviewer has 45–60 minutes and a rubric that almost always contains the same five lines: did the candidate establish requirements before designing, did they scope to something buildable, did they justify choices with numbers or explicit trade-offs, did they find the failure modes themselves, and could they go deep on at least one component. Candidates who fail rarely fail on knowledge; they fail by drawing boxes for forty minutes and never saying why, or by solving a much harder problem than the one asked.

The mental model that fixes this: a design is a sequence of decisions, each of which closes off alternatives, and your job is to make the decision sequence audible. Every time you draw something, say what you rejected and what would change your mind. "Postgres, single primary, because at 500 writes/second we are three orders of magnitude from needing to shard; the thing that would change my mind is a tenant that needs data residency in another region." That one sentence demonstrates capacity thinking, restraint, and a migration trigger — the three things lead-level rubrics are actually looking for.

The frame: six phases, timeboxed

Use a fixed frame so you never have to decide what to do next under pressure. Say it out loud at the start ("I will spend five minutes on requirements, then sketch the API, then the data model, then scale it, then break it") — that single sentence buys you credibility and control of the clock.

Requirements: the questions that actually change the design

Bad requirement-gathering is a list of trivia ("do we support dark mode?"). Good requirement-gathering asks only questions whose answers move a box on the diagram. There are about eight of them, and they are the same in every round:

  • Who writes and who reads? Read:write ratio decides whether you cache, replicate or shard first. 100:1 means caching and read replicas; 1:1 means the write path is the system.
  • How fresh must reads be? "Seconds is fine" unlocks replicas, caches, and async projections. "Must reflect my own write immediately" forces read-your-writes handling (unit 10).
  • What is the latency budget, at which percentile? p50 is a vanity number; p99 is where retries, GC pauses and cold caches live.
  • What must never be lost, and what may be? This is the durability question. Money and audit logs are different from view counts, and the answer decides sync vs async replication and at-least-once vs at-most-once delivery.
  • How many tenants, and how isolated? Decides schema shape, key design, noisy-neighbour controls (unit 13).
  • What is the peak-to-average ratio? A 10x spike at 09:00 is a completely different system from a flat load with the same daily total.
  • What is the compliance envelope? Data residency, retention, right-to-erasure, PCI/PHI scope. Each one is a hard constraint that beats any elegant design.
  • Is this greenfield or a change to something running? If it is running, the design must include a migration path, and that is usually where the real interview is.

Narrating trade-offs like a lead, not a candidate

The difference between a senior and a principal answer is rarely the choice; it is the framing around the choice. Three verbal patterns carry most of the signal:

  1. Name the axis before the option. "The trade here is consistency against availability during a partition" is stronger than "I would use Postgres."
  2. Give the reversal condition. "I would start with a modular monolith; I would split out the ingest service when its deploy cadence or its scaling shape genuinely diverges from the rest." Now you have shown you will not need a rewrite to change your mind.
  3. Cost the option in something real. Engineer-weeks, on-call pages, dollars per month, or p99 milliseconds. "Sharding buys us headroom we do not need and costs us cross-shard queries and a year of operational learning" is a lead sentence.

A useful running example for this whole guide, because it exercises every layer without being exotic: a multi-tenant B2B document-collaboration product — users upload and edit documents, others comment in near-real-time, everything is searchable, and admins export audit trails. It has a read-heavy API, a write-heavy collaboration channel, blob storage, full-text search, background processing (virus scan, thumbnails, OCR), a React front end, and hard tenancy isolation. Whenever this guide needs a concrete anchor, it uses that system.

Interview trap

Designing for the scale you were told, not the scale you were asked about. When the interviewer says "10 million users", candidates immediately reach for sharding and multi-region. But 10M registered users at 2% daily-active and 20 requests/session is roughly 4,600 requests/minute — around 80 requests/second, which a single well-indexed Postgres and three API pods will serve with room to spare. The strong move is to do the arithmetic out loud, show that the naive design fits, and then ask which dimension is expected to grow 100x. Candidates who skip straight to a sharded, multi-region, event-sourced design are marked down for over-engineering and for not knowing the numbers.

Gotcha

Silence while thinking reads as not knowing. The interviewer cannot see your reasoning. If you need to think, think out loud in options: "Two ways to do the fan-out — write-time fan-out into per-user inboxes, or read-time merge. Write-time is better for read-heavy, worse for celebrity accounts. Let me check the numbers." That sentence takes eight seconds and converts silence into signal.

Try it

1. Run a timed round against yourself. Pick "design a URL shortener" — deliberately easy — and give yourself exactly 40 minutes with a timer, out loud, recording it. Then listen back with the six-phase table open and mark where each phase started. Expected outcome: most people discover they spent 20 of the 40 minutes on phase 4 and never reached failure modes. The fix is mechanical — set a phone timer for the phase boundaries.

2. Convert three of your own past decisions into reversal conditions. Take three technical choices from a system you have worked on and write, for each, one sentence of the form "we chose X over Y because Z; we would revisit if W." Expected outcome: you will find at least one where you cannot name W, which means the decision was taste rather than engineering — that is exactly the one an interviewer will probe.

Solution sketch: the URL shortener in six phases

Requirements: create short link, resolve short link, optional custom alias, click analytics; out of scope: user accounts, link expiry UI. Envelope: 100M links total, 10:1 read:write is wrong here — it is more like 1000:1; 500M redirects/day is ~5,800/sec average, ~20,000/sec peak; 100M rows at ~100 bytes is 10 GB, which fits in RAM. API: POST /links, GET /{code} returning 301/302. Key insight: the read path is a pure key-value lookup, so it belongs in a cache or an edge KV store, not in a relational query; the write path is tiny. Code generation: base62 of a monotonic counter (or a Snowflake-style id) rather than a hash, so you avoid collision handling entirely. Failure: cache miss storm on deploy, hot links, and the analytics write path — which you make async through a queue so a redirect never waits on an analytics insert. The scored moment is noticing that 302 vs 301 is a design decision: 301 is cached by browsers forever and destroys your analytics.

Interview Q&A

How do you open a system design interview?

I restate the problem in one sentence to check we agree on it, then spend five to eight minutes on requirements before drawing anything. I separate functional requirements (what a user can do) from non-functional ones (scale, latency percentile, durability, consistency, tenancy, compliance) and I explicitly write down what is out of scope, because scope creep is what makes candidates run out of time. Then I state the plan for the hour so the interviewer knows I will get to failure modes, and I ask which parts they most want depth on — that turns the round into a collaboration rather than a performance. The thing I am really buying with those eight minutes is the right to say "no" later: if I have agreed that mobile push is out of scope, I do not have to design it at minute 35.

The interviewer says "10 million users". What do you do with that number?

I convert it into requests per second and bytes, out loud, because the raw user count means nothing. I ask for or assume a daily-active ratio and a sessions-per-day figure, multiply through, and divide by 86,400 for the average, then apply a peak factor of three to ten depending on whether the product is global or has a business-hours shape. Ten million registered users typically lands somewhere between 50 and 500 requests per second, which is not a scaling problem at all — so the useful next question is which dimension is expected to grow, and whether there is a skew such as one tenant being a thousand times bigger than the median. Doing this early is also protective: it stops me designing a sharded system for a workload that fits on one box, which is the most common way strong engineers fail this round.

How much of the hour should be the diagram?

Less than most people think — roughly ten minutes for the high-level boxes, and the diagram should stay at about nine boxes. The value is concentrated in the deep dive and the failure discussion, because that is where the interviewer can tell the difference between someone who has read a book and someone who has operated a system. I keep the diagram deliberately coarse and annotate it as I go with the numbers from the envelope: requests per second on the edges, storage size on the stores, and the consistency model on the replication arrows. A diagram with numbers on the arrows is worth three diagrams without them.

What separates a senior answer from a principal answer in this round?

A senior answer picks a good design and defends it. A principal answer makes the decision framework visible: it names the axis of the trade-off, states the condition under which the choice would be reversed, and costs the alternatives in engineer-time and operational load rather than only in latency. Principal answers also tend to bring in the organisation — who owns this service, how many teams touch it, what the on-call rotation looks like — because at that level the architecture and the team topology are the same decision. The last marker is restraint: being able to say "we do not need this yet, and here is the trigger that tells us we do" is harder and more valuable than proposing the sophisticated thing.

You are asked to design something you have never built. How do you handle it?

I say so, briefly and without drama, and then reason from primitives instead of pattern-matching. Almost every system decomposes into the same handful of problems: a write path with a durability requirement, a read path with a latency requirement, some fan-out, some ordering, and some background work. I map the unfamiliar domain onto those, ask two or three questions to pin down the parts that are genuinely domain-specific, and reason from there. Interviewers weight this heavily, because it is the actual job — nobody has built the thing the company is about to build, and what they want to see is whether you derive or whether you recall.

How do you handle an interviewer who keeps pushing you toward their preferred design?

I treat it as a signal rather than a fight. Usually it means either they have a specific follow-up they want to reach, or they are testing whether I hold a position under pressure. I ask what they are optimising for — "are you worried about the write amplification, or about the operational cost?" — and then either adopt their direction while narrating what it costs, or hold mine with one concrete reason and offer to explore theirs. What loses points is capitulating instantly with no reasoning, or digging in without engaging. Being able to say "you are right, that is better, here is why I did not see it" is a strong signal, not a weak one.

What do you do in the last ten minutes?

Failure modes and operations, unprompted. I walk each dependency and say what happens when it is slow, when it is down, and when it comes back — the recovery case is the one people forget, because a cold cache or a queue backlog on recovery is often worse than the outage. Then I name the three or four signals I would put on a dashboard, the SLO I would defend, and one thing the system degrades to rather than failing. If there is time left I say what I would build first and what I would deliberately defer, which shows I can sequence work and not just draw an end state.

How do you scope when the problem is genuinely huge, like "design Slack"?

I decompose it into the three or four subsystems out loud, then explicitly pick one to design deeply and get agreement on that choice. For Slack that is roughly: message ingest and fan-out, presence, search, and file handling. I say "message fan-out is the interesting core; presence is a well-understood ephemeral-state problem and search is a standard index-behind-a-queue; I propose we go deep on fan-out unless you want otherwise." That single move converts an unanswerable prompt into a tractable one and demonstrates decomposition, which is what the question was really testing. Trying to cover all four superficially is the failure mode.

How do you use back-of-envelope numbers without being pedantic about them?

I round hard and say I am rounding: 86,400 seconds a day becomes 100,000, a million becomes ten to the sixth, and I work in orders of magnitude. The purpose is never precision, it is to establish which regime we are in — does this fit in memory, on one disk, on one machine, or does it not? A calculation that is wrong by 30% but right about the regime is entirely useful; a calculation carried to three decimals is a waste of interview minutes. I also state the assumption alongside every number so the interviewer can correct the input rather than the arithmetic.

The design round is on an existing system rather than a greenfield one. What changes?

The centre of gravity moves from "what is the right end state" to "what is the safe path from here to there", and that is usually the harder question. I spend the requirements phase understanding the current shape, the pain that triggered the change, and the constraints that cannot move — data that cannot be migrated in a maintenance window, clients that cannot be upgraded, a compliance boundary. Then I design in increments where every step is independently shippable and reversible: expand-and-contract for schema changes, a strangler facade at the edge, dual-write with a reconciliation job and a read-side cutover flag. I also say explicitly how I would prove each step worked before the next one, because migrations fail on verification, not on design.

02Napkin math: capacity, latency and cost

Every design decision downstream of this section is really an arithmetic decision, and the arithmetic is the part most candidates cannot do under pressure. There are only three quantities: how many things per second, how many bytes, and how long each thing takes. Everything else — how many pods, whether it fits in memory, whether you need a queue, whether you need to shard — falls out of those three plus one law.

Little's law is the whole of capacity planning

Little's law says L = λ × W: the number of items concurrently in a system equals the arrival rate times the time each spends inside. It is unconditionally true for any stable system, which makes it the single most useful formula in this interview. Read it three ways:

  • Sizing concurrency: 2,000 requests/second at 50 ms each means 100 requests in flight at any moment. That is your minimum thread/connection/pod concurrency, before headroom.
  • Sizing a connection pool: if a request holds a database connection for 8 ms and you serve 2,000 rps, you need 16 concurrent connections — not the 100 that people configure by reflex. Oversized pools do not add throughput; they move queueing into the database where you cannot see it.
  • Detecting a lie: if someone claims 10,000 rps through a service with 200 ms latency and a 100-thread pool, the numbers say 2,000 concurrent requests are needed and only 100 exist. One of the three numbers is wrong.

The companion fact is queueing behaviour near saturation. For an M/M/1 queue, average wait time scales as 1/(1−ρ) where ρ is utilisation. At 50% utilisation you wait 1 service time; at 90% you wait 9; at 99% you wait 99. This is why "our CPU is only at 80%, we have headroom" is wrong, and why capacity targets in practice sit at 50–70% for latency-sensitive services. It is also why variance matters more than the mean: one slow dependency at p99 turns into a queue that inflates everyone's latency.

Cost as a first-class design axis

At lead level you are expected to have a rough cost model, because "it works" and "it works at a price the business will pay" are different designs. Orders of magnitude in 2026 cloud pricing, useful for comparison rather than quoting: compute is roughly $25–60 per vCPU-month on demand; managed relational storage is ~$0.10–0.25 per GB-month plus IOPS; object storage is ~$0.02 per GB-month; and cross-region and internet egress is the line item that surprises people, at roughly $0.05–0.09/GB. That last one is a design constraint: a chatty multi-region architecture can cost more in egress than in compute, which is one of the strongest arguments for keeping a service and its database in the same region and replicating asynchronously.

Interview trap

Confusing average and peak, and then confusing peak with p99 latency. Three different numbers get conflated constantly. Average rps sizes your monthly bill; peak rps sizes your pod count; and neither says anything about p99 latency, which is driven by variance — GC pauses, cold caches, connection establishment, a slow dependency, one hot partition. A system provisioned for peak throughput can still miss its p99 target badly. When you state a latency requirement, always state the percentile, and when you state throughput, always state whether it is average or peak and what the peak factor is.

Gotcha

Ignoring the "and then it retries" multiplier. A dependency that starts failing does not reduce load, it multiplies it: with three retries you turn 360 rps into 1,440 rps aimed at a component that is already unhealthy. Every capacity estimate for a downstream should include the retry amplification factor, and every retry policy should be paired with a budget or a circuit breaker (unit 11). This is the single most common way a small incident becomes an outage.

Try it

1. Measure Little's law on something you own. Take any service with metrics: read requests/second and mean latency off the dashboard, multiply them, and compare with the actual observed concurrency (in-flight requests, or active threads). Expected outcome: they agree within noise. If they do not, you have found either a metric that is lying or a queue you did not know about — both are worth finding.

2. Break saturation on purpose. Run a local ASP.NET Core endpoint that sleeps 50 ms, put a load generator on it, and walk the arrival rate up in steps toward the concurrency limit while plotting p50 and p99. Expected outcome: p50 stays flat and then knees sharply; p99 knees much earlier. You will see the 1/(1−ρ) curve with your own eyes, and you will never again say "we are only at 80% CPU, we are fine".

Solution sketch

Use dotnet-counters monitor -n YourApp --counters System.Runtime,Microsoft.AspNetCore.Hosting for in-flight requests and thread-pool queue length, and any load tool (k6, bombardier, wrk) for arrival rate. Cap concurrency deliberately with the .NET 8+ rate limiter (AddConcurrencyLimiter) so you know ρ exactly. Plot arrival rate on x and latency percentiles on y: the knee will appear at roughly 70–80% of the concurrency limit for p99 and 90%+ for p50. The second thing you will notice is that the queue length metric moves before latency does, which is why queue depth is a better alert signal than latency.

Interview Q&A

State Little's law and give me two design uses for it.

L = λW: the number of items in a system equals arrival rate times time in system, and it holds for any stable system regardless of distribution. The first use is sizing concurrency — at 2,000 requests per second with 50 ms latency you have 100 requests in flight, so anything that limits concurrency below 100 becomes the bottleneck. The second is sizing a connection pool: multiply requests per second by how long a request actually holds a connection, not how long the request takes overall, which usually reveals that the pool is an order of magnitude larger than needed. I also use it as a consistency check on other people's numbers, because it is very hard to state three throughput, latency and concurrency figures that are mutually inconsistent without it being obvious once you multiply.

Why do we target 60–70% utilisation instead of 90%?

Because queueing delay grows as 1/(1−ρ), so the last 20% of utilisation costs far more latency than the first 80%. At 50% utilisation a request waits about one service time; at 90% it waits nine; at 99% it waits ninety-nine. That curve is why a service can look healthy on a CPU graph and be missing its p99 badly. The other reason is failure headroom: if you run three pods at 90% and one dies, the survivors are instantly at 135% and you have a cascading failure rather than a degraded one. So the target is set by the latency SLO and the failure domain size, not by a wish to use the hardware efficiently.

Estimate the storage for five years of the system we just designed.

I separate the two storage classes because they have completely different cost and access profiles. Relational metadata is about 1.2 million rows a day at a few hundred bytes, so roughly 130 GB a year, or under a terabyte over five years — comfortably a single instance, and the real question is index size and vacuum behaviour rather than capacity. Document bodies are 48 GB a day raw, which is where the volume is, so they go to object storage where a gigabyte-month costs a couple of cents and lifecycle policies can tier old revisions to cold storage. The design consequence is the one I would state out loud: the relational database stores pointers and metadata, never blobs, and the retention policy is a product decision with a direct and calculable cost.

A dashboard shows p50 at 20 ms and p99 at 900 ms. Where do you look?

That gap is a variance problem, not a throughput problem, and there are only a handful of usual causes. First, queueing — check in-flight requests and thread-pool queue length, because a saturated pool produces exactly this shape. Second, a dependency with a long tail, which you find by comparing per-dependency histograms rather than the aggregate. Third, cold-start effects: connection establishment, TLS handshakes, JIT on the first hit, or a cache that expires in lockstep. Fourth, garbage collection or, in a container, CPU throttling from a low CFS quota, which shows up as pauses uncorrelated with load. I would look at queue depth first because it moves earliest and it distinguishes "we are saturated" from "one thing is slow".

How do you size a database connection pool?

From Little's law on the hold time, not on the request time: peak requests per second multiplied by the seconds a request actually holds a connection. If a request takes 60 ms overall but only holds a connection for 6 ms of that, the pool needs roughly a tenth of what people assume. Then I add headroom for burst and slow queries — a factor of a few, not a factor of fifty — and I check the total across all pods against the database's own limit, because the failure mode is the aggregate, not the per-pod number. The counter-intuitive part worth saying out loud is that a smaller pool is often faster: it queues requests where you can see and shed them instead of pushing contention into the database, where a hundred concurrent queries on eight cores just context-switch.

What is the cost of a synchronous cross-region call and when is it acceptable?

Roughly 70 to 100 milliseconds of round trip between Europe and the US east coast, which is a floor set by physics and cannot be optimised away — plus egress charges of around five to nine cents a gigabyte. That is acceptable only on paths that are already slow and rare: an admin action, a login against a single global identity provider, a nightly reconciliation. It is unacceptable on the hot read path, where it consumes the entire budget in one hop. When a design appears to need it, the usual right answer is to replicate the data asynchronously to the region that reads it and accept staleness, or to route the user to the region that owns their data, rather than paying for the hop on every request.

How do you estimate the number of pods for a service?

I compute the in-flight concurrency from Little's law at peak, decide how much concurrency one pod handles safely at the target utilisation, and divide — then round up for failure domains. For an I/O-bound ASP.NET Core service the per-pod limit is usually set by the connection pool or an explicit concurrency limiter rather than by CPU, so I set that limit explicitly instead of discovering it. Then I add at least one pod so that losing one does not push the others past the utilisation target, and I make sure the horizontal autoscaler scales on a signal that actually leads load — queue depth or in-flight requests — rather than on CPU, which lags for I/O-bound workloads. Finally I sanity-check the aggregate: pods times pool size must not exceed what the database will accept.

What is retry amplification and how do you bound it?

When a dependency degrades, every layer that retries multiplies the load on it, so a three-layer stack with three attempts each can turn one user request into twenty-seven backend calls exactly when the backend is least able to serve them. You bound it three ways: retry at one layer only, and make that explicit in the design; use a retry budget so retries are capped as a percentage of traffic rather than per request; and put a circuit breaker in front so that repeated failure stops generating load at all. Jittered exponential backoff matters too, because synchronised retries produce a thundering herd on recovery. The framing I would use in an interview is that retries convert a partial failure into a load test against a sick system unless they are bounded.

Give me a quick way to decide whether a dataset fits in memory.

Multiply row count by row size, then double it for index and object overhead, and compare with the memory you are willing to pay for — commodity instances go to hundreds of gigabytes, so the honest threshold in 2026 is much higher than most people's instincts. A hundred million rows at 200 bytes is 20 GB of data and perhaps 40 GB with overhead, which fits comfortably in a single instance's page cache, meaning your "database is slow" problem is almost certainly a query plan problem rather than an I/O problem. This calculation is how you decide whether to reach for a cache at all: if the working set already fits in the database's buffer pool, an external cache mainly buys you protection from expensive query plans, not from disk.

How do you present estimates when you are unsure of the inputs?

I state the assumption first, then the arithmetic, then the sensitivity: "assuming 30% daily active and 150 calls per session, that is about 90 requests per second average; if daily-active is really 60% it doubles, and the design does not change until we are about twenty times higher." That last clause is the valuable one, because it tells the interviewer how wrong the input can be before the conclusion changes, which is the real question. It also invites correction of the input rather than of my arithmetic, which keeps the conversation on design. Precision beyond one significant figure is wasted; knowing the regime boundary is not.

03The compute tier: from Kestrel to Kubernetes

This is the unit where being a .NET engineer specifically is an advantage, and where interviewers separate people who have operated ASP.NET Core from people who have only written controllers. The mental model to build: a request is a unit of work that passes through five queues on its way in — kernel accept queue, Kestrel connection handling, the .NET thread pool, your own concurrency limiter, and the database connection pool — and it can only ever be as fast as the slowest queue and as reliable as the shortest one. Almost every "the service fell over" story is one of those five queues filling.

The request path, honestly

  1. Edge. TLS termination, WAF, DDoS protection, and usually the first rate limit. In cloud terms: Front Door / CloudFront / an ingress controller. This is also where you get HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 (QUIC), which matter mainly for connection reuse and head-of-line blocking on lossy mobile networks.
  2. Reverse proxy / ingress. Routing, retries, header propagation. In .NET shops, YARP is the in-process option when you want routing logic in C# — the strangler-facade pattern (unit 15) is usually YARP or an ingress rule.
  3. Kestrel. The .NET web server. It accepts connections, parses HTTP, and dispatches to the pipeline. Kestrel is fast enough that it is essentially never your bottleneck; what does bite is Limits.MaxConcurrentConnections, request-body size limits, and KeepAliveTimeout behind a proxy.
  4. Middleware pipeline. Ordered, and order is a correctness concern: exception handler outermost, then HSTS/HTTPS redirection, routing, CORS, authentication, authorization, rate limiting, then endpoints. Authentication before authorization; rate limiting after authentication if limits are per-user, before it if they protect the auth endpoint itself.
  5. Thread pool. The single most misunderstood component. It grows slowly by design (roughly one thread per 0.5 s beyond the core count after .NET 6 tuning changes), so a burst of blocking work causes a latency cliff rather than a graceful slowdown. This is why Task.Result, .Wait() and GetAwaiter().GetResult() on a request path are a scaling defect, not a style issue.
  6. Your handler, which spends nearly all its time awaiting I/O, then the database pool, the HTTP client pool, and back out.

Stateless is a design constraint, not an aspiration

Horizontal scaling only works if any pod can serve any request. That means no in-process session state that matters, no in-memory-only cache that must be coherent, no local disk that must persist, no scheduled job that assumes one instance. In practice the four things that break statelessness in .NET apps are: in-memory session, IMemoryCache used as a source of truth rather than as an accelerator, background IHostedService jobs that run on every replica, and data protection keys — if you do not persist the ASP.NET Core data-protection key ring to shared storage, antiforgery tokens and auth cookies break the moment you scale past one pod or restart. That last one is a genuinely common production incident and a good thing to mention unprompted.

Serverless, and when to say no

Azure Functions / Lambda are excellent for spiky, event-shaped, embarrassingly parallel work — thumbnailing, webhook receivers, scheduled jobs — and poor for latency-sensitive request paths with warm connections, because cold starts and connection-pool fragmentation fight you. The honest lead answer is a hybrid: the request-path API on always-on compute with pooled connections, the burst-shaped background work on serverless. If asked about cold starts specifically for .NET, name the current mitigations: NativeAOT publishing (and, in .NET 11, the NativeAOT CLI on by default), ReadyToRun, and provisioned/pre-warmed instances.

Interview trap

Blaming "the thread pool" without knowing how it grows. Candidates say "we ran out of threads" as if it were random. The mechanism is specific: the pool starts at roughly the processor count, and when all threads are busy it injects new ones slowly — on the order of one or two per second — because rapid injection makes CPU-bound contention worse. So if you block threads on I/O (sync-over-async, a blocking SDK, lock around an await-heavy region), arriving requests queue with nobody to serve them, latency goes vertical, health checks time out, Kubernetes kills the pod, and the load moves to the remaining pods. The tell is ThreadPool.PendingWorkItemCount and queue length climbing while CPU stays low. The fix is async all the way down plus an explicit concurrency limit — not raising ThreadPool.SetMinThreads, which only masks it.

Gotcha

One HttpClient per request, or a static one that never sees DNS changes. Creating HttpClient per request exhausts sockets (TIME_WAIT) under load; a single static instance caches DNS forever and keeps talking to a decommissioned IP after a failover. IHttpClientFactory with typed clients solves both by pooling handlers with a rotation lifetime, and since .NET 8 AddStandardResilienceHandler() layers timeout, retry and circuit breaker on top with defaults you can defend. If you are on SocketsHttpHandler directly, set PooledConnectionLifetime — that is the DNS-rotation knob.

Try it

1. Starve the thread pool on purpose. Add an endpoint that calls a slow HTTP dependency with .Result instead of await, put 200 concurrent requests on it, and watch dotnet-counters. Expected outcome: threadpool-queue-length climbs into the hundreds, latency goes from 100 ms to many seconds, CPU stays under 20%, and your readiness probe starts failing. Change one keyword to await and rerun — the queue stays near zero. That contrast is the whole argument for async, in one experiment.

2. Break a deploy, then fix it. Deploy a container with terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 5 while the app has a 30-second shutdown timeout and a long-running request. Roll it. Expected outcome: 502s in the access log. Then add a preStop hook that sleeps 5 seconds and set the grace period above the shutdown timeout, and roll again — clean. The lesson to be able to articulate: the pod must stop receiving traffic before it starts draining, and the load balancer learns that asynchronously.

Solution sketch

For (1): dotnet-counters monitor -n App --counters System.Runtime[threadpool-thread-count,threadpool-queue-length],Microsoft.AspNetCore.Hosting[current-requests]. The signature is queue length up, CPU flat. For (2): the pod lifecycle is SIGTERM and endpoint removal happen concurrently, so the preStop sleep covers the propagation delay of endpoint removal to every kube-proxy/ingress; ASP.NET Core then stops accepting and drains within HostOptions.ShutdownTimeout (default 30 s in .NET 8+, previously 5 s). Set the Kubernetes grace period to shutdown timeout plus the preStop sleep plus a margin.

Interview Q&A

Walk a request through an ASP.NET Core service and name where it can queue.

It arrives at the edge for TLS and WAF, hits an ingress or reverse proxy such as YARP, is accepted by Kestrel, runs the middleware pipeline, is dispatched onto a thread-pool thread, executes my handler, and there acquires a database connection and possibly an outbound HTTP connection. There are five queues on that path: the kernel accept backlog, Kestrel's connection limit, the .NET thread pool, any explicit concurrency limiter I have configured, and the database or HTTP connection pool. Latency under load is always one of those filling, and the useful discipline is to make one of them the deliberate bottleneck — an explicit concurrency limiter — because then overload produces fast 429s that I control instead of an unbounded queue somewhere I cannot see.

What breaks first when you scale an ASP.NET Core app from one pod to twenty?

Usually the database connection count, because pool size is configured per pod and nobody multiplies. Twenty pods with a hundred-connection pool is two thousand connections against a Postgres that wants a couple of hundred, so you either lower the pool using Little's law or put a pooler such as PgBouncer in front. The second thing is data protection: if the key ring is not persisted to shared storage, each pod generates its own keys and auth cookies and antiforgery tokens fail for a fraction of requests in a way that looks random. Third is anything that assumed a single instance — in-memory caches used as truth, hosted services that now run twenty times, and scheduled jobs that need a distributed lock or a leader election.

Liveness versus readiness — why does the difference matter?

Liveness answers "is this process wedged and should it be killed", readiness answers "should this pod be sent traffic right now". They must check different things, and specifically liveness must not depend on external dependencies. If the database is briefly unavailable and it is wired into liveness, every pod fails its probe at the same moment and Kubernetes restarts the entire deployment, converting a two-minute database blip into a full outage plus a cold-start stampede. Readiness is where dependency checks belong, because failing readiness just removes the pod from the load balancer and it comes back automatically. I would also make readiness fail during shutdown deliberately, so the pod leaves rotation before it starts draining.

Your service has p99 spikes but average CPU is 30%. What is your first hypothesis in a container?

CPU throttling from the CFS quota. A container with a limit of, say, 500 millicores gets a quota per 100 ms period, and when the app briefly needs more it is stopped until the next period — which appears as multi-tens-of-milliseconds pauses uncorrelated with average CPU. I would look at throttled-periods and throttled-seconds metrics first because they confirm or eliminate it in seconds. The related .NET-specific effect is that the runtime derives the processor count from the cgroup, so a fractional limit shrinks the thread pool and the GC heap count too. The usual remedies are to set requests generously and either remove the limit or round it to whole cores; the second hypothesis, if throttling is clean, would be garbage collection pauses or a dependency tail.

How would you do a zero-downtime deploy of this service?

Rolling update with a surge, readiness gating, and a graceful drain: new pods must pass readiness before old ones are terminated, the terminating pod stops advertising readiness and sleeps briefly so endpoint removal propagates, and the grace period exceeds the app's shutdown timeout so in-flight requests finish. On top of that, the deployment must be backward compatible with both the previous code and the current database schema, which in practice means expand-and-contract migrations and no breaking API changes in a single step. For riskier changes I would add a canary — a small percentage of traffic to the new version with automated comparison of error rate and latency — and I would keep the rollback path as a redeploy of the previous image rather than a database restore, which is why schema changes are decoupled from code deploys.

When would you choose serverless over always-on compute?

When the load is spiky and the work is event-shaped and idempotent — image processing, webhook ingestion, scheduled reconciliation, fan-out jobs — because you pay only for the bursts and the platform handles the scaling curve. I avoid it on latency-sensitive request paths that need warm connection pools and predictable p99, because cold starts and per-invocation connection churn work against both. In .NET specifically I would mention that cold start is much better than its reputation with ReadyToRun or NativeAOT, and that .NET 11 turns the NativeAOT CLI on by default, but that it is still a poor fit for a chatty database-backed API. The hybrid is usually right: always-on for the API, serverless for the queue-driven work behind it.

What is your autoscaling signal, and why not CPU?

For an I/O-bound service, CPU is a lagging and weak signal — the pods are waiting on the database, not burning cycles, so CPU may sit at 25% while latency is already unacceptable. I scale on in-flight requests or on queue depth, because those move first and map directly to Little's law: if concurrency exceeds what the current pods can hold at target utilisation, I need more pods. For queue-driven workers, the signal is unambiguous — messages waiting, or better, estimated time to drain, which is what KEDA gives you. I would also set the scale-down cooldown much longer than scale-up, since flapping costs more than a few extra pods, and I would put a maximum in place so an autoscaler cannot amplify an incident by stampeding the database.

Where does YARP fit in a .NET architecture?

Anywhere you want routing decisions expressed in C# rather than in an ingress config file: a backend-for-frontend that fans out, a strangler facade in front of a legacy application while routes are moved one at a time, or a per-tenant router that picks a shard's cluster from a claim. It gives you the reverse-proxy primitives — load balancing, health checks, session affinity, request transforms — as middleware inside an ASP.NET Core app, which means the routing logic can share code and configuration with the rest of the platform. What I would not do is put it in the path purely for capability that the cloud edge already provides, because it is another hop and another thing to scale; the justification has to be logic that genuinely needs to be code.

How do you make a background job safe when it runs on every replica?

Either give it a single owner or make it idempotent and partitioned. The single-owner route is a leader election or a distributed lease — a row with an expiry in the database, a Redis lock with a token, or the platform's own primitive — and the critical detail is that the lease must be renewed and the work must be safe if the lease is lost mid-run, which means checking ownership before committing. The better route where possible is to make the job queue-driven: the schedule enqueues one message, and whichever worker picks it up does the work exactly once thanks to a deduplication key. I would avoid the pattern of "only run on instance zero", because instance identity is not stable in Kubernetes and it silently stops working after a rescheduling.

How does .NET 11 change anything about the compute tier?

Two things worth naming. Runtime-native async moves the async state machine from a compiler rewrite into the runtime, which mostly shows up as cleaner stack traces and lower allocation and overhead on deep async call chains — good for diagnosability of exactly the thread-pool and tail-latency problems we have been discussing. Separately, the SDK turns the NativeAOT CLI and the MSBuild server on by default, which shortens build times and makes AOT-published containers a more ordinary choice, which in turn helps cold start and container size for burst workloads. Neither changes the architecture, and that is the honest framing: it is a cost and diagnosability improvement on the same shapes, and .NET 11 is a two-year STS release reaching GA on 10 November 2026, so for a system being designed now the sensible baseline is still .NET 10 LTS.

04Boundaries, contracts and protocols

A system's architecture is mostly its boundaries: where you draw a line, what crosses it, and what is allowed to change on each side independently. Everything people argue about — monolith versus microservices, REST versus gRPC, shared database versus not — is downstream of one question: what is the unit of independent change? If two components must be deployed together to stay correct, they are one component with extra network calls, and you have taken the costs of distribution without the benefit.

Boundaries first: the modular monolith default

The defensible 2026 default for a new product is a modular monolith: one deployable, internally divided into modules with explicit interfaces, each module owning its own tables and never reaching into another module's tables. You get transactions, one deploy, one trace, in-process calls at 100 ns instead of 1 ms, and — critically — the option to extract a module into a service later at low cost, because the seam already exists. You give up independent scaling and independent deploy cadence.

Extract a service when there is a real forcing function, and be able to name it:

  • Divergent scaling shape — the OCR pipeline needs 40 CPU-heavy pods for three hours a day and the API needs three all day.
  • Divergent deploy cadence or risk — a payments module that needs a change-control process the rest of the product does not.
  • Divergent availability requirement — an ingest path that must accept writes when the rest of the system is down.
  • Team topology — Conway's law is not a joke; when two teams contend on the same deploy pipeline every day, the boundary is real whether or not you draw it.
  • A different technology is genuinely required — the ML inference path in Python, say.

"We want microservices" is not on that list. The lead-level line to have ready: "Distribution is a cost you pay to buy independence. If we are not buying independence, we are just paying."

The contract details that separate levels

Idempotency. Any unsafe operation that a client may retry needs an idempotency key. The standard shape: client generates a key, server stores (key, request-fingerprint, response) with a TTL, and on a repeat returns the stored response rather than acting again. Stripe-style semantics, now essentially standardised by the IETF Idempotency-Key draft, and the detail that scores is storing the response, not just a "seen" flag — otherwise a retry after a network failure gets a 409 the client cannot interpret.

app.MapPost("/documents", async (
    CreateDocument body,
    [FromHeader(Name = "Idempotency-Key")] string? key,
    IdempotencyStore store,
    AppDb db,
    CancellationToken ct) =>
{
    if (string.IsNullOrWhiteSpace(key))
        return Results.Problem("Idempotency-Key required", statusCode: 400);

    var fingerprint = Fingerprint.Of(body);                 // hash of the semantic request
    if (await store.TryGetAsync(key, ct) is { } prior)
        return prior.Fingerprint == fingerprint
            ? Results.Json(prior.Response, statusCode: prior.Status)   // safe replay
            : Results.Problem("Key reused with a different body", statusCode: 422);

    var doc = Document.Create(body);
    db.Documents.Add(doc);
    // One transaction covers the effect AND the idempotency record, or neither happens.
    await store.SaveAsync(key, fingerprint, 201, new { doc.Id }, db, ct);
    await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);

    return Results.Created($"/documents/{doc.Id}", new { doc.Id });
});

Pagination. Offset pagination (OFFSET 20000 LIMIT 20) makes the database scan and discard 20,000 rows and produces duplicates and gaps when the underlying data changes. Keyset (cursor) pagination — WHERE (created_at, id) < (@lastCreated, @lastId) ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC LIMIT 20 — is O(log n) with the right index, stable under concurrent writes, and the only correct answer for infinite scroll. Offset is acceptable only for small, bounded, admin-facing lists where users jump to page numbers.

Versioning. Additive changes never need a version: add optional fields, never remove or repurpose, never change a field's type or its meaning. When you must break, version the resource representation (URL segment or media type), run both for a deprecation window, and instrument usage per version per client so you can prove when it is safe to remove. The thing to say out loud: "the API version is a promise about compatibility, and the expensive part is not shipping v2, it is retiring v1."

Errors. Use RFC 9457 application/problem+json — it is built into ASP.NET Core (Results.Problem, AddProblemDetails()) — with a stable machine-readable type, a human detail, and a correlation id. Status-code discipline matters: 400 for malformed, 422 for semantically invalid, 409 for a state conflict, 429 with Retry-After for throttling, 503 with Retry-After for shedding. Clients build retry logic off these; getting them wrong causes retry storms.

Backend for frontend

The browser tier usually wants a different shape from what internal services expose: one call instead of six, exactly the fields the screen renders, and session-cookie auth rather than bearer tokens in JavaScript. A BFF — an ASP.NET Core app that fans out to the internal services and holds the OIDC tokens server-side — solves the shape problem and the token-storage problem at the same time. Since .NET 8 this pattern has first-class support in the ASP.NET Core + OIDC stack, and it is the recommended answer to "where do you store the access token in a SPA": nowhere in the browser; in an encrypted, HTTP-only, SameSite cookie against a BFF that keeps the tokens.

Interview trap

Splitting services along technical layers instead of business capability. The tell-tale failing design has a "database service", a "validation service" and an "API service". Every user story then touches all three, every change needs three deploys coordinated, and you have created a distributed monolith — the worst of both worlds: the coupling of a monolith with the failure modes and latency of a distributed system. Boundaries follow business capabilities and data ownership. The test to state: can this service be deployed on its own on a Tuesday without coordinating with anyone? If not, it is not a service boundary.

Gotcha

Two services writing the same table. Shared-database integration is the most common accidental coupling, and it removes every benefit of the split: neither team can change the schema, neither can be independently deployed, and the transactional guarantees you thought you had do not hold once both write concurrently. The rule to state without hedging: one writer per table. If another service needs the data, it gets it through an API or a published event and keeps its own read model.

Try it

1. Convert an offset endpoint to keyset and measure. Seed a table with a million rows, index (created_at desc, id desc), and time OFFSET 900000 LIMIT 20 against the equivalent keyset query. Expected outcome: hundreds of milliseconds versus roughly a millisecond, and the execution plan changes from a large scan to an index range scan of 20 rows. Then insert rows while paging and observe that offset paging shows duplicates and keyset does not.

2. Make a POST idempotent and prove it. Add the idempotency handler above, then fire the same request twice concurrently with the same key. Expected outcome: exactly one row created, both callers get 201 with the same id. Now break it deliberately by storing the key after committing the effect rather than in the same transaction, and race it again — you will create duplicates, which is the exact bug this pattern exists to prevent.

Solution sketch

The correctness hinge is a unique constraint on the idempotency key plus a single transaction covering the key row and the effect. Under concurrency the second inserter hits a unique-violation, and the handler must translate that into "wait briefly and read the stored response" rather than into a 500. In Postgres, INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING RETURNING gives you the race outcome in one statement. For keyset paging, the composite index must match the ORDER BY exactly, including direction and the tiebreaker column, or you get a sort node and lose the benefit — check with EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) that you see an index scan with a small rows estimate and no Sort.

Interview Q&A

Monolith or microservices for a new product with eight engineers?

A modular monolith, without hesitation, and I would say why in terms of what distribution costs. With eight engineers there is no team-topology pressure to split, and the things microservices buy — independent deploy cadence, independent scaling, fault isolation — are not yet needed, while the costs are immediate: distributed tracing to debug anything, eventual consistency where a transaction used to do, network failure modes on every call, and an operational surface several people are now on call for. What I would insist on is that the modules have real boundaries in code — separate schemas or at least strict table ownership, module-level public interfaces, and an architecture test that fails the build on a cross-module reference — because that is what makes extraction cheap later. Then I would name the triggers that would make me extract something, so the decision has an exit condition rather than being dogma.

How do you decide where a service boundary goes?

By data ownership and rate of change together. A boundary is right when one team can own the data behind it, when the operations inside it are transactionally cohesive, and when it can be deployed independently on any given day without coordination. I look for aggregates in the domain sense — clusters of entities that change together and have a consistency requirement between them — and I refuse to draw a boundary through the middle of one, because that turns a transaction into a saga. The pragmatic test I use in review is to take the last twenty pull requests and see how many touched more than one proposed service; if most did, the boundary is wrong regardless of how clean it looks on the diagram.

REST or gRPC between two internal services?

gRPC when the call volume is high, the contract is stable and generated, and both sides are ours — the binary encoding and HTTP/2 multiplexing genuinely reduce CPU and latency at scale, and streaming is first class. REST when the consumer set is open or diverse, when human debuggability matters, or when I want HTTP caching and standard infrastructure to work without special support. The point I would make is that this choice is much less consequential than people treat it: the contract's shape, its versioning discipline and its failure semantics matter far more than the wire format. If I were pressed for a default in a .NET shop, I would say REST at the edge and gRPC internally once a call path exceeds a few thousand calls per second or needs streaming.

Design idempotency for a payment endpoint.

The client sends an Idempotency-Key it generates per logical attempt, and the server stores that key with a fingerprint of the request body and the full response, in the same transaction as the effect itself. On a replay with a matching fingerprint the stored response is returned verbatim; on a replay with a different body the correct answer is a 422, because the client has reused a key for a different intent, which is a bug worth surfacing loudly. The key must be unique-constrained so concurrent duplicates lose the race deterministically rather than both proceeding, and the record needs a TTL — typically 24 hours to a few days — chosen to exceed the client's maximum retry window. The subtle part I would call out is that idempotency at the API boundary is not enough on its own: if the payment provider is called inside that transaction, I need its idempotency key too, derived deterministically from mine, so a retry does not double-charge downstream.

Offset or keyset pagination, and why?

Keyset for anything large or continuously changing. Offset makes the database produce and discard every skipped row, so cost grows linearly with page number, and because it is positional it shows duplicates and skipped items when rows are inserted or deleted while a user pages. Keyset carries the sort key of the last row as a cursor and turns the query into an index range scan, so page one thousand costs the same as page one, and it is stable under concurrent writes. The trade-offs I would name are that keyset cannot jump to an arbitrary page number and needs a total-order sort key — usually a timestamp plus an id tiebreaker with a matching composite index — so for a small admin table with page links, offset is still the simpler right answer.

How do you version a public API?

By making almost everything additive so that versioning is rarely needed: new optional fields, new endpoints, never removing or repurposing a field, and tolerant readers on both sides. When a genuine break is unavoidable I version the representation — a URL segment is the most operationally honest because it shows up in logs and routing — run both versions concurrently, and instrument calls per version per client so retirement is a data-driven decision rather than an argument. I would also insist on a deprecation policy stated up front with a timeline and Deprecation and Sunset headers, because the expensive part is never shipping v2, it is getting the last three clients off v1. Internally, between our own services, I would prefer expand-and-contract rollouts over versioning entirely, since we control both ends.

When is GraphQL the right answer, and what does it cost you?

It earns its place when you have several client types with genuinely different data needs over one connected graph, and the alternative is a growing pile of bespoke endpoints or badly over-fetching clients. It costs you HTTP caching, because everything is a POST to one URL, so you replace it with persisted queries and application-level caching; it costs you predictable performance, because a client can compose an expensive query, so you need depth limits, cost analysis and batching to avoid N+1 resolvers; and it makes authorization harder, since permissions have to be enforced per field rather than per endpoint. In .NET, Hot Chocolate handles the mechanics well. I would push back on GraphQL when there is one client, because then a BFF gives the same benefit with far less machinery.

What is a BFF and what problem does it actually solve?

A backend for frontend is a server-side component owned by the front-end team that composes calls to internal services into exactly the shapes one client needs. It solves two distinct problems that people conflate. The first is chattiness and shape: one round trip for a screen instead of six, with the aggregation happening on a fast internal network. The second, and in 2026 the more important one, is token custody — the BFF holds the OIDC refresh and access tokens server-side and gives the browser only an encrypted HTTP-only cookie, which removes the entire class of token-theft-by-XSS problems that come with storing tokens in JavaScript. The cost is another deployable per client type and the discipline to keep business logic out of it.

Two teams want to write to the same table. What do you do?

I refuse, and I make the alternative concrete rather than just saying no. One table has one writer; the second team either calls the owner's API, or the owner publishes events that the second team projects into its own read model. Shared writes look cheap for a week and then permanently freeze the schema, because neither team can change a column without coordinating a deploy, and any invariant either team believes in can be violated by the other. If the pressure is that the API is too slow or missing a capability, that is the real problem to fix, and it is a much smaller piece of work than untangling a shared table two years later. The one exception I would entertain is a genuinely append-only integration table with a documented contract, treated as an interface rather than as internal state.

How do you keep API contracts from breaking in CI?

By making the contract a build artifact and diffing it. For REST I generate the OpenAPI document at build time — built into ASP.NET Core since .NET 9 — check it into the repository, and fail the build on a breaking diff using a schema-diff tool, which turns "we accidentally removed a field" from a production incident into a red pipeline. For messaging and gRPC the equivalents are a schema registry with compatibility rules and protoc breaking-change detection. On top of that I like consumer-driven contract tests for the handful of consumers that matter most, because a schema diff catches shape changes but not semantic ones, such as a field that starts arriving null in cases it never did before. The point I would make is that a contract nobody verifies mechanically is a convention, and conventions decay.

05Data modelling and query performance

More systems are ruined by the data model than by the service topology, and it is the part of a design round where a full-stack engineer can show unusual depth. The mental model: a relational database is a machine for maintaining sorted structures (indexes) over a heap of rows, and every query is the planner's guess about the cheapest way to combine those structures. If you can predict the plan, you can predict the performance; if you cannot, you are guessing.

Normalise, then denormalise on evidence

Start normalised — third normal form, one fact in one place — because normalisation is what makes writes correct and cheap and makes the schema survive requirements you have not heard yet. Denormalise deliberately, per query, when you have a measured read path that cannot be served: a materialised view, a counter column, a duplicated tenant_id to avoid a join on every query, a JSON column for a genuinely schema-less attribute bag. The rule to state: every denormalisation is a cache, and every cache needs an invalidation story. A duplicated column that nobody updates on the write path is a bug waiting to be discovered by a customer.

Indexes: the five facts that answer most questions

  1. A B-tree index is useful left-to-right. An index on (tenant_id, created_at) serves WHERE tenant_id = ? and WHERE tenant_id = ? AND created_at > ?, but not WHERE created_at > ? alone. Column order is the whole design.
  2. Equality first, then range, then sort. Put equality predicates leftmost, then the single range column, and let the remaining columns match the ORDER BY. Anything after the range column cannot help the sort.
  3. Covering indexes eliminate the heap fetch. Postgres INCLUDE / SQL Server INCLUDE add payload columns so the query never touches the table. In Postgres an index-only scan also requires the visibility map to be current, which means it depends on vacuum — a nuance worth knowing.
  4. Every index costs writes. Each insert/update maintains every affected index and expands the WAL/log. Six indexes on a hot write table is a write-throughput decision you should have made deliberately.
  5. Selectivity decides use. The planner will ignore an index it believes matches most rows, because a sequential scan is cheaper than random I/O plus heap fetches. A "why isn't my index used?" question is usually answered by statistics or by a non-sargable predicate — a function on the column, an implicit type cast, or a leading wildcard.

EF Core at scale: the three failure modes

EF Core 10 (LTS, with .NET 10) is a good production ORM; the problems are always the same three, and interviewers ask about them because they are so common.

The lead-level framing: an ORM is for the write path, where object graphs and unit-of-work semantics earn their keep; the read path is queries, and it is entirely legitimate to drop to Dapper or raw SQL for the ten reports that matter. Saying that shows you are not ideological about the tool.

Reading a plan

You should be able to talk through EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) output. What to look for, in order: a Seq Scan on a big table where you expected an index; a large gap between estimated and actual rows, which means the statistics are wrong and every join decision above it is suspect; a Nested Loop with a high loop count, which is the plan-level signature of an N+1; a Sort with an external merge (spilling to disk because work_mem is too small); and high Buffers: read versus hit, which tells you the working set is not cached. In SQL Server the equivalents are the actual execution plan, key lookups, and the estimated-versus-actual row warning.

Interview trap

"We'll add an index" as the answer to every slow query. Three reasons this is a weak answer. First, an index that is not selective will not be used, so the query stays slow and the write path gets slower. Second, indexes are not free: on a table taking a thousand inserts a second, each additional index is real write amplification and WAL volume. Third, the actual problem is frequently not the index but the query — a non-sargable predicate such as WHERE lower(email) = ? without a matching expression index, an implicit type conversion (a varchar column compared to an nvarchar parameter in SQL Server, which silently disables index seeks), or a SELECT * that forces a key lookup per row. Say "I would look at the plan first" and then name what you would look for.

Gotcha

Soft deletes without partial indexes and without a retention plan. A global query filter on deleted_at IS NULL is easy to add and quietly poisons every plan: the table keeps growing, indexes carry rows nobody queries, and the planner's selectivity estimates drift. If you soft-delete, use partial indexes that exclude deleted rows and have an actual purge job that removes them after the retention window — otherwise "soft delete" is just "never delete", and it will also collide with your right-to-erasure obligations.

Try it

1. Watch UUIDv4 versus UUIDv7 insert behaviour. Create two identical tables in Postgres 18, one keyed by gen_random_uuid() and one by uuidv7(), insert two million rows into each, then compare index size (pg_relation_size), insert throughput, and WAL generated. Expected outcome: the v7 table's primary key index is noticeably smaller and denser and inserts are meaningfully faster, because v4 causes random page splits while v7 appends. This is the experiment that makes the key-design argument concrete rather than folkloric.

2. Make the planner refuse your index. Take a well-indexed column and query it with a function wrapped around it, then with an implicit cast, then with a leading wildcard LIKE '%foo'. Expected outcome: three sequential scans. Then fix each one — an expression index, a matched parameter type, and a trigram index for the wildcard — and watch the plan flip back. You will never again say "but there is an index on that column" without checking sargability.

Solution sketch

For (1), measure with pg_stat_statements and pg_relation_size('tbl_pkey'); also compare pg_stat_wal before and after, since random inserts produce more full-page writes. Expect roughly 20–40% larger index and a visible throughput gap at a few million rows; the effect grows with table size because it is about cache locality. For (2), the fixes are CREATE INDEX ... ON t (lower(email)), aligning the parameter type in the client (in Npgsql, make sure the parameter is text/uuid and not inferred as something else), and pg_trgm with a GIN index for infix search. In SQL Server the equivalent implicit-conversion trap is a varchar column compared against an nvarchar parameter, which is exactly what a default .NET string parameter produces unless you set SqlDbType.

Interview Q&A

What primary key would you choose for a table written by many clients, and why?

UUIDv7 as of today. It gives me client-side generation, which means a client can construct an entity and its children before any round trip and retry safely, while the embedded millisecond timestamp keeps inserts appending to the right-hand edge of the index instead of scattering across it. That last property is why the old advice against GUID keys existed: UUIDv4 is random, so every insert lands on a random page, causing splits, buffer-pool churn and larger write-ahead logs. PostgreSQL 18 added uuidv7() natively in September 2025 and .NET has Guid.CreateVersion7() since .NET 9, so there is no reason to hand-roll it. The trade-offs I would name are sixteen bytes instead of eight in every index and foreign key, and the fact that the id leaks a creation timestamp, which occasionally matters for privacy.

How do you decide the column order in a composite index?

Equality predicates first, then at most one range column, then columns that satisfy the sort. A B-tree is only useful as a prefix, so an index on tenant and created-at can serve a query filtering on tenant, or on tenant and a date range, but never on date alone. Once you use a range column, nothing to its right can help ordering, which is why a query that filters a range on one column and sorts by another almost always needs a sort node or a different index. I also check whether adding a couple of payload columns with INCLUDE turns it into a covering index, since eliminating the heap fetch is frequently a bigger win than anything else, and I weigh that against the write cost, because every index is paid for on every insert and update.

A query is slow. Walk me through your process.

I get the actual plan with timings and buffers before touching anything, because the plan tells me which of about five things it is. I look first for a sequential scan on a large table where I expected a seek, then for a large divergence between estimated and actual rows, which means statistics are stale or the predicate is unestimable and every join choice above it is suspect. Then I check for nested loops with high loop counts, sorts spilling to disk, and the ratio of buffer hits to reads, which tells me whether this is a caching problem or a plan problem. Only then do I decide between fixing the query — usually sargability or an unnecessary column list — adding or reshaping an index, or accepting that the query is legitimately expensive and moving it to a read replica or a materialised view.

When do you denormalise?

When a measured read path cannot meet its budget with a normalised schema and a correct index, and not before. The usual forms are a counter or aggregate column maintained on write, a duplicated key such as tenant id carried onto child tables so every query can filter without a join, and a materialised view or projection table for a report. The discipline that matters is treating each one as a cache: I write down what invalidates it, and I prefer mechanisms that cannot drift — a trigger, a transactional update in the same unit of work, or a projection rebuilt from an event log — over "we remember to update it". I also want a way to detect drift, because the failure mode of a denormalised value is a customer seeing a wrong number, which is far worse than a slow page.

How do you handle a table that is now 500 million rows and growing?

First I check whether it is actually a problem, because 500 million narrow rows with the right index is fine on modern hardware and the pain is usually maintenance rather than queries. If it is a problem, my order is: fix the access pattern and the indexes, then partition by the natural boundary — almost always time, sometimes tenant — so that queries prune to one or two partitions and old data can be detached and archived cheaply, then and only then consider sharding across instances. Partitioning also fixes the operational half: index rebuilds, vacuum and backups become per-partition instead of whole-table. I would call out that partitioning helps only if the partition key is in the predicate, so if the dominant query does not filter on time, time partitioning makes things worse by touching every partition.

EF Core or Dapper?

Both, split by direction. EF Core for the write path, where change tracking, an object graph and a unit of work genuinely reduce mistakes and where the queries are simple by-key loads. Dapper or raw SQL for the read paths that matter, where I want to control the exact statement, use database features the ORM abstracts away, and avoid materialising entities I do not need. Using both is not indecision, it is recognising they solve different problems, and it usually removes the temptation to fight the ORM with increasingly exotic LINQ. The caveats I would add are that two access paths mean two places to remember tenant filtering, so tenancy has to be enforced somewhere both share, and that raw SQL needs the same migration discipline as the mapped model.

What is an N+1 and how do you catch it before production?

It is one query to fetch a list followed by one query per item to fetch a related thing, so a page that looks fine with ten rows becomes five hundred round trips with five hundred. In EF Core it usually comes from lazy loading or from iterating a collection and awaiting inside the loop; the fix is an Include with a split query, or better, a projection that selects exactly the shape the screen needs. Catching it before production is the real answer: I add a test-time or development-time interceptor that counts database round trips per request and fails when a single request exceeds a threshold, and I put query counts on the request telemetry so a regression shows on a dashboard. Distributed tracing makes it visually obvious too — an N+1 is a staircase of identical spans.

When would you put JSON in a relational column?

For genuinely open-ended, sparse, per-tenant or per-integration attributes that no query needs to join on — custom fields, a webhook payload for audit, a settings blob. Both engines support it well now: Postgres jsonb with GIN indexing has been mature for years, and SQL Server 2025 added a native json type that EF Core 10 will use by default at compatibility level 170 or with UseAzureSql(), which is more efficient and safer than the old nvarchar(max) convention. What I would not do is use JSON to avoid deciding on a schema for core entities, because you lose constraints, foreign keys and predictable plans, and every consumer has to defend against every historical shape. The rule of thumb is that if a field is ever a filter or a join key for a common query, it should be a column.

How do you run a schema migration on a table with no downtime?

Expand and contract, in separate deploys. First expand: add the new nullable column or new table, deploy code that writes to both old and new while still reading old. Then backfill in batches with throttling so the migration does not saturate the database or hold long transactions. Then flip reads to the new shape behind a flag and verify. Finally, in a later deploy, contract by removing the old column and the dual-write. The details that make it actually safe are engine-specific and worth naming: in Postgres, adding a column with a non-volatile default is metadata-only since version 11, but adding a constraint or an index must use NOT VALID then VALIDATE, and CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY, or you will take an exclusive lock on a live table; and any migration must have a short lock timeout so a blocked DDL statement fails fast instead of queueing every query behind it.

How would you add vector search to this system in 2026?

I would start by keeping it in the database I already run, because a separate vector store is another system to operate, secure and keep in sync. On SQL Server 2025 there is a native VECTOR type with VECTOR_DISTANCE generally available and approximate search with DiskANN indexing behind a preview flag, and EF Core 10 maps both; on Postgres, pgvector with HNSW indexing is the mature answer. The design questions that matter more than the engine choice are where embeddings are generated and how they are kept current when the source text changes — which is a projection problem, so it belongs on the event or outbox path — and whether the query is pure similarity or hybrid, since in practice hybrid search combining a keyword index with vector similarity beats either alone. I would also insist on measuring recall against a labelled set, because approximate indexes trade recall for speed and that trade is invisible until someone complains the results got worse.

This unit is where interviewers find out whether you understand databases or merely use them. The mental model: a transaction is a promise that concurrent work will produce a result that looks like some serial order; isolation levels are how much of that promise you are willing to trade for throughput; and every level below serializable permits specific, nameable anomalies. Being able to name the anomaly a level permits — and the one it does not — is the whole exam.

Write skew is the anomaly worth being able to explain, because it is the one snapshot isolation does not prevent and the one that causes real bugs. Two transactions read the same set, each checks an invariant that still holds in its own snapshot, and each writes a different row; individually correct, jointly they violate the invariant. The canonical example: two on-call doctors, a rule that at least one must remain on call, both check "is someone else on call?" simultaneously, both see yes, both go off call. Your version of it is usually a quota, a seat count, or "only one primary contact per account".

Two databases, two philosophies

PostgreSQL uses MVCC: writers never block readers and readers never block writers, because each row update writes a new tuple version and old versions are cleaned up by VACUUM. The consequence you must know: long-running transactions and idle-in-transaction sessions prevent vacuum from reclaiming dead tuples, causing table and index bloat. That is the number-one operational failure mode of Postgres and it always originates in application code holding a transaction open — usually across an HTTP call.

SQL Server defaults to pessimistic locking with shared/exclusive locks and lock escalation (row → page → table at around 5,000 locks), so readers do block writers unless you enable READ_COMMITTED_SNAPSHOT — which almost every modern SQL Server deployment should, moving it to MVCC-like behaviour at the cost of tempdb version-store space. If you are asked to diagnose SQL Server blocking, the first question is whether RCSI is on.

Optimistic concurrency is the right default for web apps

A user loads a form, thinks for four minutes, and saves. You cannot hold a transaction for four minutes, so you need a way to detect that someone else changed the row in between. Optimistic concurrency: carry a version token, and make the update conditional on it.

// Postgres: xmin is the system column EF Core maps as a concurrency token.
modelBuilder.Entity<Document>().UseXminAsConcurrencyToken();
// SQL Server: a rowversion column.
modelBuilder.Entity<Document>().Property(d => d.Version).IsRowVersion();

try
{
    doc.Title = newTitle;                 // UPDATE ... WHERE id = @id AND xmin = @original
    await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);
}
catch (DbUpdateConcurrencyException ex)
{
    // 0 rows affected => someone else won. Decide the policy explicitly:
    //   - last-write-wins        (reload and overwrite)
    //   - first-write-wins       (409 to the client)
    //   - merge                  (field-level three-way merge; the right answer for docs)
    var entry = ex.Entries.Single();
    var current = await entry.GetDatabaseValuesAsync(ct);
    return Results.Conflict(Merge.ThreeWay(original, mine: entry.CurrentValues, theirs: current));
}

The scored insight is that a concurrency conflict is a product decision, not a technical one. "Someone else edited this — here is what changed" is a feature; a silent last-write-wins is data loss that nobody notices for months.

When you genuinely need a lock

Pessimistic locking is right when the conflict rate is high enough that optimistic retries become a livelock, and when the critical section is short. In SQL: SELECT ... FOR UPDATE (Postgres) or WITH (UPDLOCK, ROWLOCK) (SQL Server), always with a lock timeout, always acquiring locks in a consistent order to avoid deadlocks, and never spanning a network call. For cross-process coordination that is not a single row, use an advisory lock (pg_advisory_xact_lock) or a lease row rather than a distributed lock in Redis — and if you do reach for Redis, know that a Redis lock is a lease with a timeout and is not safe for correctness-critical mutual exclusion (unit 10).

The transaction boundary is an architectural decision

Three rules that hold up under pressure:

  • A transaction never contains a network call to a third party. The remote call takes an unbounded time, holds locks and a connection, and prevents vacuum. Do the external call before or after, and make the operation idempotent so a retry is safe.
  • A transaction spans one aggregate. If you need two aggregates to change atomically, that is a saga (unit 09) or the aggregates are drawn wrong.
  • Effects that must happen if and only if the transaction commits go through the outbox — a row written in the same transaction and relayed afterwards. This is how you get "publish an event when an order is placed" correct without a distributed transaction.

Interview trap

"We'll wrap it in a transaction" as an answer to a distributed problem. A database transaction guarantees nothing about the HTTP call to the payment provider, the message you published, or the file you wrote to blob storage. Candidates routinely describe "save the order and charge the card in one transaction", which cannot exist: if the commit fails after the charge succeeds, you have taken money for nothing. The correct shape is to make each side idempotent, order them so the recoverable failure is the likely one, record intent durably before acting (outbox / process manager), and reconcile. Saying "there is no transaction across these two systems, so I need idempotency and reconciliation" is the answer they are listening for.

Gotcha

Retrying a serialization failure by retrying the wrong thing. Under Postgres serializable isolation you will get 40001 serialization failures and 40P01 deadlocks, and they are normal, not exceptional — the level is optimistic by design. But the retry must re-run the whole transaction including the reads, because the reads are what became invalid. Code that catches the exception and retries only the write silently re-applies a decision made against stale data. Also: retries need jitter and a cap, or two conflicting transactions can livelock against each other indefinitely.

Try it

1. Produce write skew, then prevent it three ways. In Postgres, create a table of on-call doctors with an invariant "at least one on call". Open two REPEATABLE READ transactions, have each check the count and then set itself off call, and commit both. Expected outcome: both succeed and the invariant is violated. Now repeat under SERIALIZABLE (one aborts with 40001), then with SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the rows read, then with a database-level constraint or a materialised counter — and note that only the last one survives a developer forgetting the pattern.

2. Watch a long transaction bloat a table. Open a session, BEGIN, run a trivial SELECT, and leave it idle in transaction. In another session update a million rows repeatedly and watch pg_stat_user_tables.n_dead_tup climb and never fall while VACUUM reports it cannot remove tuples. Expected outcome: table and index size grow monotonically. Commit the idle transaction and vacuum — space is reclaimable again. This is the mechanism behind most "Postgres got slow overnight" incidents.

Solution sketch

For (1), the four remedies are ordered by robustness: an EXCLUDE/CHECK constraint or a maintained counter with a constraint is strongest because it cannot be bypassed; serializable isolation is next but requires every caller to use it and to retry 40001; explicit row locks work but only if every code path takes them in the same order; and application-level checking is the one that produced the bug. For (2), the diagnostic query is SELECT pid, state, xact_start, now()-xact_start AS age FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE state = 'idle in transaction' ORDER BY age DESC, and the production defences are idle_in_transaction_session_timeout, statement_timeout, and never opening a transaction before you have everything you need to finish it.

Interview Q&A

Explain the isolation levels and what each one permits.

Read uncommitted permits dirty reads — seeing data that may be rolled back — and in practice nobody should use it; Postgres does not even implement it distinctly. Read committed, the default in both Postgres and SQL Server, prevents dirty reads but each statement takes a fresh snapshot, so within one transaction the same query can return different results, which is the non-repeatable read, and new rows can appear, which is the phantom. Repeatable read in Postgres is snapshot isolation: the whole transaction sees one consistent snapshot, so no non-repeatable reads and no phantoms, but write skew remains possible because two transactions can each read a set, find an invariant satisfied, and write different rows. Serializable eliminates write skew too, and the important practical difference is how: Postgres does it optimistically with predicate tracking and aborts losers with a serialization failure, so callers must retry, while SQL Server takes range locks and blocks instead.

What is write skew and how do you prevent it?

Write skew is when two concurrent transactions each read overlapping data, each verifies an invariant that holds in its own snapshot, and each writes a different row, so that neither is individually wrong but together they break the invariant. The classic example is two doctors both going off call after each observes that the other is still on. It is the specific anomaly snapshot isolation does not prevent, which is why "repeatable read is good enough" is often wrong. There are four fixes in increasing order of robustness: enforce the invariant with a database constraint or a maintained counter so it cannot be violated at all; use serializable isolation and retry on serialization failures; take explicit locks on the rows you read, which materialises the conflict; or, weakest, check in the application and hope every path does. I prefer the constraint, because it survives the next developer who does not know the pattern exists.

Optimistic or pessimistic concurrency for a document editor?

Optimistic for the ordinary metadata edits, because users hold state for minutes and you cannot keep a transaction open across think time; a version token on the row and a conditional update detect the conflict cheaply. For the document body itself, neither is really the right frame — collaborative editing wants operational transformation or a CRDT so that concurrent edits merge rather than conflict, and the durable store then holds the merged state plus an operation log. The judgment I would voice is that a concurrency conflict is a product decision: silently overwriting is data loss, blanket rejection is a bad experience, and for structured records a field-level merge with an explicit prompt on true conflicts is usually right. Pessimistic locking would only be my answer for short, high-contention critical sections such as decrementing inventory, and even then only with a lock timeout.

How do you make a "charge the card and save the order" flow correct?

By accepting that there is no transaction spanning the two systems and designing for the crash between them. I write the intent first — an order row in a pending state, in a local transaction — then call the payment provider with an idempotency key derived deterministically from the order id, then record the outcome. If the process dies after the charge but before recording it, a reconciliation job or a retry with the same idempotency key finds the existing charge instead of creating a second one, which is exactly why the provider's idempotency key is not optional. The state machine has to tolerate every crash point, and there must be a reconciliation process that compares our records with the provider's for a window, because in a system that takes money "probably consistent" is not acceptable. What I would never do is call the provider inside a database transaction, since that holds locks and a connection for an unbounded time.

What is the outbox pattern and why do you need it?

It solves the dual-write problem: you want to commit a state change and publish an event, and there is no transaction spanning your database and your broker. The outbox writes the event as a row in the same database transaction as the state change, so they commit or roll back together, and a separate relay reads unsent rows and publishes them, marking them sent. That gives at-least-once delivery with no lost events, at the price of possible duplicates, so consumers must be idempotent. The implementation details that matter are that the relay should use change-data-capture or a polling query with a suitable index rather than scanning, that the outbox table needs a purge policy or it becomes the biggest table in the system, and that ordering is only guaranteed per key unless you do extra work. In .NET this is packaged in MassTransit and Wolverine, and I would generally use one of those rather than write the relay.

A Postgres database has slowed down over weeks with no traffic change. Where do you look?

Bloat from dead tuples, and behind that, long-running or idle-in-transaction sessions preventing vacuum. MVCC keeps old row versions until no snapshot can see them, so a single session left idle inside a transaction pins the horizon and vacuum cannot reclaim anything newer, and both tables and indexes grow while plans slowly degrade. I would query pg_stat_activity for the oldest transaction, check n_dead_tup and the last autovacuum time per table, and look at index versus table size ratios. The fixes are immediate and structural: kill or fix the offending session, then set idle_in_transaction_session_timeout and statement_timeout so it cannot recur, tune autovacuum to be more aggressive on the hot tables, and rebuild bloated indexes concurrently. The application-level lesson is to open transactions as late as possible and never across an external call.

Two services need to update their own databases atomically. Options?

Realistically there are three, and two-phase commit is the one I would not pick. A distributed transaction coordinator gives real atomicity but couples availability — if the coordinator or any participant is down, locks are held and both services block, which is exactly the failure amplification microservices exist to avoid. The mainstream answer is a saga: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions, coordinated either by choreography through events or by an explicit orchestrator, accepting that there is a window where the system is inconsistent and designing the business process to tolerate it. The third option, and often the best, is to notice that the requirement for atomicity means these two things are one aggregate and the service boundary is wrong, in which case the right fix is to move the data rather than to build a saga.

When is READ_COMMITTED_SNAPSHOT the right change on SQL Server?

Almost always for an OLTP web application, and it is one of the highest-value single settings in a legacy SQL Server system. Without it, read committed uses shared locks, so readers block writers and writers block readers, and under load you get blocking chains and timeouts that look like the database is slow when it is actually just serialising. With RCSI on, readers get a row-version snapshot and do not take shared locks, which is the behaviour developers already assume they have because it is how Postgres works. The costs to state are tempdb pressure from the version store, which needs monitoring and sizing, and a subtle behaviour change for code that was relying on read locks to serialise — which is code that was already fragile. I would test it under production-like load rather than flipping it on a Friday, and I would watch the version store size for the first week.

How do you avoid deadlocks?

Consistent lock ordering above all: if every code path acquires resources in the same order, a cycle cannot form. After that, keep transactions short and narrow so the window for a cycle is small, take the most contended lock last, and avoid escalation by touching fewer rows or by batching in ranges rather than in random order. Deadlocks cannot be eliminated entirely, so the second half of the answer is detection and retry — both engines detect and kill a victim, and the application must catch that specific error, back off with jitter, and retry the whole transaction. I would also capture the deadlock graph, because deadlocks in production almost always trace back to two specific code paths taking two specific tables in opposite orders, and once you see the graph the fix is usually a five-line change.

Do you use database transactions in a microservice per HTTP request?

Yes for the write path, scoped as tightly as possible — opened after validation and any external calls, closed before returning. What I avoid is the ambient "unit of work per request" pattern that opens a transaction in middleware at the start of the request and commits at the end, because it holds a connection and locks across everything the handler does, including calls to other services, and it is the pattern that produces the idle-in-transaction bloat we discussed. Read-only endpoints do not need an explicit transaction at all unless they require a consistent multi-statement snapshot. The framing I would offer is that the transaction boundary should mirror the aggregate boundary and the moment of decision, not the HTTP request lifecycle, which is an implementation detail of the transport.

07Scaling data: replicas, partitions, shards

There is a strict order to scaling storage, and getting the order right is most of the signal in this part of an interview. Buy a bigger machine, fix the queries, add a cache, add read replicas, partition, then — reluctantly — shard. Each step is roughly an order of magnitude cheaper in engineering effort than the next, and the last one is a permanent tax on every future feature. In 2026 a single managed Postgres or SQL Server instance with a few hundred gigabytes of RAM and NVMe storage handles tens of thousands of transactions per second; most systems that shard did not need to.

Vertical first, and it is not embarrassing

Doubling an instance takes an afternoon and a maintenance window. Sharding takes a quarter and changes every query. Say the numbers out loud: the largest cloud database instances offer hundreds of vCPUs and terabytes of RAM, and a well-indexed OLTP workload on that hardware is in the tens of thousands of transactions per second. The interesting question is therefore rarely "how do we scale past one machine" but "what in our workload is disproportionate" — usually one table, one query, or one tenant.

Read replicas and the consistency you just gave up

Replication is asynchronous by default in both engines: the primary commits, ships WAL/log records, and the replica applies them milliseconds later — or seconds later under load, which is the part people forget. Sending reads to a replica therefore introduces replication lag, and with it two classic bugs:

  • Read-your-writes violation. User saves, is redirected, the read goes to a lagging replica, and their change has vanished. Fixes: route a user's reads to the primary for a short window after a write (sticky-by-session), or carry the commit LSN/log sequence number in the session and require the replica to have caught up to it, or simply keep the post-write read on the primary.
  • Monotonic-read violation. Two successive reads hit different replicas with different lag and the user sees time go backwards. Fix: sticky replica selection per session.

Sending all reads to replicas is the wrong default. Send the ones that tolerate staleness: reports, search, listings, analytics, exports. And measure lag explicitly (pg_last_wal_replay_lsn versus the primary, or SQL Server's AG dashboard) with an alert, because a replica that has fallen ten minutes behind while still serving traffic is worse than one that is down.

Connection management is the real replica problem

Postgres allocates a process per connection, so a few hundred connections is a lot and a few thousand is an outage. With twenty pods times a fifty-connection pool you are at a thousand before any replicas. Put a pooler in front — PgBouncer in transaction mode, or a cloud-managed equivalent — and know the constraint that transaction-mode pooling imposes: no session-level state, so no SET that must persist, no session advisory locks, no prepared statements unless the pooler supports protocol-level preparation (recent PgBouncer versions do). This detail comes up constantly and knowing it marks you as someone who has run Postgres in Kubernetes.

Partitioning: the step most systems actually need

-- Declarative range partitioning by month: the shape that fits time-series
-- workloads (audit logs, revisions, events, telemetry).
CREATE TABLE audit_event (
    id         uuid        NOT NULL DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    tenant_id  uuid        NOT NULL,
    occurred_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
    kind       text        NOT NULL,
    payload    jsonb       NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (occurred_at, id)          -- partition key must be in the PK
) PARTITION BY RANGE (occurred_at);

CREATE TABLE audit_event_2026_09 PARTITION OF audit_event
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2026-09-01') TO ('2026-10-01');

-- Queries that filter on occurred_at prune to one partition:
--   EXPLAIN shows "Partitions removed: 47"
-- Retention becomes metadata, not a 200-million-row DELETE:
ALTER TABLE audit_event DETACH PARTITION audit_event_2025_09;
DROP TABLE audit_event_2025_09;

Partitioning buys three things that matter operationally: query pruning (only if the partition key is in the predicate), cheap retention (detach and drop instead of a massive delete that generates bloat and WAL), and per-partition maintenance (vacuum, reindex and backup at a manageable granularity). It does not buy you more machines. Choose the key by the dominant query: time for append-heavy logs, tenant for multi-tenant systems with a few large tenants — and be ready for the counter-question, which is what happens when one partition is a hundred times bigger than the others.

Polyglot persistence, chosen on access pattern

Do not add a datastore because it is fashionable; add one when the access pattern is genuinely different. Blob/object storage for bytes — always, never in the relational database. A search index (OpenSearch/Elasticsearch, or Postgres full-text for modest needs) for ranked text queries, kept in sync through the outbox rather than dual-written. A key-value/cache tier for hot lookups. A columnar store or warehouse for analytics, fed by CDC, so analysts cannot take down the transactional system. A document store when the aggregate genuinely is a document with no cross-entity queries — and note that Postgres jsonb covers a large fraction of that case without a new operational surface. Every additional datastore is a new backup, a new failover story, a new security boundary, and a new on-call runbook; the burden of proof is on adding, not on refusing.

Interview trap

Reaching for sharding before doing the arithmetic, and then describing modulo sharding. Two errors in one. First, most systems presented in interviews fit comfortably on one instance for years; jumping to shards signals you cannot size a system. Second, if you do shard, hash(key) mod N is the classic wrong scheme because increasing N remaps almost every key, requiring a full data migration with downtime. Consistent hashing or a fixed large number of virtual buckets mapped to physical shards is the standard answer: you then move buckets, which is a bounded, resumable, online operation.

Gotcha

Replica promotion is not a free failover. With asynchronous replication, promoting a replica after a primary failure loses any transactions not yet shipped — that is data loss, measured as your RPO, and it must be a stated, accepted number rather than a surprise. Synchronous replication removes the loss but couples your write latency and availability to the replica: if the synchronous standby is unreachable, writes stall. The mature answer names the choice explicitly per workload ("synchronous within the region for the orders database, asynchronous cross-region with an RPO of 30 seconds") and confirms that the failover has actually been rehearsed, because untested failover is not a capability.

Try it

1. Observe replication lag causing a real bug. Set up a Postgres primary and a streaming replica locally (Docker or Aspire), point writes at the primary and reads at the replica, then write and immediately read in a loop while generating heavy write load. Expected outcome: a measurable fraction of reads miss the just-written row. Then implement LSN-based read-your-writes — capture pg_current_wal_lsn() after commit and have the reader wait until pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() passes it, or fall back to the primary — and watch the failures go to zero.

2. Prove partition pruning. Build the partitioned audit_event table above with 24 monthly partitions and 20 million rows, then run the same aggregate with and without a date predicate under EXPLAIN (ANALYZE). Expected outcome: with the predicate you see "Partitions removed: 23" and a scan of one partition; without it, all 24 are scanned and the query is roughly 24 times slower. That single contrast is the entire argument for choosing the partition key by the dominant query.

Solution sketch

For (1), the production-grade version of the fix lives in the data-access layer, not in each handler: after a write, stash the LSN in the user's session or in a signed cookie, and have the read router either pick the primary or wait briefly for a replica that has replayed past it. Npgsql does not do this for you, so it is a small piece of infrastructure — which is exactly why "send all reads to replicas" is not a one-line change. For (2), also check pg_stat_user_tables per partition to see that autovacuum now works on manageable units, and try DETACH PARTITION CONCURRENTLY to confirm that retention no longer takes a heavy lock.

Interview Q&A

Walk me through your escalation ladder for a database that is running out of headroom.

Measure first: which resource is actually saturated — CPU, IOPS, memory, connections, or lock contention — because the remedy is different for each and the answer is frequently none of them, it is a bad plan. Then fix queries and indexes, which is usually the cheapest order of magnitude. Then vertical scaling, which is an afternoon of work and buys a factor of several. Then offload: reads to replicas, hot lookups to a cache, analytics to a warehouse via change data capture, blobs out of the database entirely. Then partition, which fixes maintenance and enables pruning. Only then shard, because sharding is a permanent tax on every query, every migration and every join. I would also say explicitly what triggers each step, so the ladder is a plan rather than a list.

You add read replicas and users start reporting that their edits disappear. Diagnose it.

That is a read-your-writes violation caused by replication lag: the write commits on the primary, the redirect issues a read that lands on a replica which has not applied the change yet, and the user sees stale data. I would confirm by correlating the failures with a lag metric and by checking whether affected reads are on replicas. There are three fixes in increasing sophistication: route reads to the primary for a short window after a write for that session, which is simple and usually sufficient; carry the write's log-sequence number in the session and require the replica to have replayed past it before serving, which is precise; or keep specific endpoints on the primary permanently. The broader point is that adding replicas silently changes the consistency model, so it is a design decision that has to be made per read path rather than a configuration change.

How would you shard a multi-tenant B2B system?

By tenant, using a directory table that maps tenant to shard rather than a hash, because tenants are already the isolation boundary and a lookup gives me operational freedom. With a directory I can move a single tenant to relieve a hot shard, put an unusually large customer on dedicated infrastructure, and satisfy data-residency requirements by pinning a tenant to a region — none of which a hash function allows. The directory is on the request path so it is cached aggressively with a short TTL and a versioned invalidation, and the migration procedure is the interesting engineering: freeze writes for that tenant briefly or dual-write, copy, verify with checksums, flip the directory entry, then clean up. The costs I would state plainly are no cross-tenant queries without scatter-gather, migrations that must run on every shard and can be half-applied, and a much more complicated story for anything global such as a cross-tenant admin report.

One tenant is a thousand times larger than the median. What do you do?

This is the hot-partition problem, and the first move is to stop treating tenants as uniform. Operationally I would give that tenant its own shard or its own database so its load, its maintenance windows and its incidents are isolated from everyone else, which is also usually what that customer wants and will pay for. Inside the schema I would check that the partition or index design still works at that tenant's scale, since a composite index that starts with tenant id is fine but a per-tenant table scan is not. I would also add per-tenant rate limits and quotas so a single customer's bulk import cannot consume the shared capacity, and per-tenant metrics so the next whale is visible before it becomes an incident. The general principle is that in multi-tenant systems the distribution is always heavy-tailed, so designs that assume uniformity fail on the largest customer, who is also the most important one.

When does partitioning help and when does it hurt?

It helps when the dominant queries filter on the partition key, because the planner can prune to one or two partitions, and it helps operationally almost always, because vacuum, reindexing, backup and retention become per-partition operations instead of whole-table ones — dropping a month of data becomes a metadata change rather than a hundred-million-row delete. It hurts when queries do not include the key, since every query then touches every partition and you have added overhead for nothing, and it hurts when the partitions are uneven, because one of them behaves exactly like the unpartitioned table you were trying to escape. There are also sharp edges to know: the partition key must be part of the primary key, unique constraints cannot span partitions without including it, and having thousands of partitions costs planning time. So the design question is entirely about the dominant access pattern, which is why I ask about read patterns before choosing.

Why is a connection pooler necessary in front of Postgres?

Because Postgres uses a process per connection, so connections are expensive in memory and scheduling, and a few thousand of them will degrade or kill an instance that would happily serve the same workload through a few dozen. In a Kubernetes deployment the arithmetic gets away from you quickly: pods multiplied by pool size, multiplied again by every worker and job, easily reaches a thousand. PgBouncer in transaction mode multiplexes many client connections onto few server ones, which fits web workloads well because a connection is only held for the duration of a transaction. The constraint to know is that transaction pooling forbids session state — session-level SET, session advisory locks, and traditionally server-side prepared statements, although recent PgBouncer versions support protocol-level prepared statements — so the application must be written to not rely on session affinity.

Synchronous or asynchronous replication?

It is a per-workload choice between write latency and recovery point objective, and it should be stated as such. Asynchronous means the primary commits without waiting, so writes are fast and the primary stays available if a replica is down, but a failover loses whatever had not shipped — typically milliseconds to seconds of transactions, which for a document product is acceptable and for a payments ledger is not. Synchronous means no loss on failover but the write path now depends on the standby being healthy and on the network between them, so a slow standby becomes slow writes and an unreachable one can stall commits unless you configure a fallback. My usual shape is synchronous to a standby within the same region for the workloads that cannot lose data, and asynchronous across regions with an explicitly stated RPO, and I would insist that the failover has been rehearsed, because an untested failover is not a capability.

How do you keep a search index in sync with the database?

Through the same transaction boundary as the write, using an outbox or change data capture, never by dual-writing from the application. Dual-writing means the process can die between the database commit and the index update, leaving the index permanently wrong with no way to notice; the outbox makes the intent durable and the relay retries until it succeeds. Because delivery is at least once, the indexing consumer must be idempotent, which is natural since indexing a document is a set operation keyed by id. I would add two things a good answer includes: a version or sequence number on each document so an out-of-order update cannot overwrite a newer one, and a periodic full reconciliation that compares counts and checksums between the source and the index, because in practice indexes drift and the only way to know is to check.

When would you introduce a NoSQL store into a .NET system?

When the access pattern genuinely does not fit relational and I can name the specific property I need. Examples that pass the test: extremely high-volume append-only telemetry where a time-series or columnar store is orders of magnitude cheaper; a globally distributed workload where something like Cosmos DB's multi-region writes and tunable consistency solves a problem Postgres would not; or a graph traversal that is many joins deep. Examples that fail the test are "the schema might change" and "it will scale better", because Postgres jsonb handles schema flexibility and a single relational instance outscales most products. The cost side I would insist on covering is that a second store means a second backup and restore procedure, a second failover story, a second security and audit boundary, and consistency work between the two — and that cost is paid forever, by whoever is on call.

How do you move a large table to a new schema with no downtime?

Expand, backfill, verify, cut over, contract — each as a separate deploy with its own rollback. Expand means creating the new structure and dual-writing so both are current. Backfill means copying historical rows in bounded batches with a sleep between them, monitoring replication lag and lock waits so the migration yields to production traffic, and making the batch job resumable because it will be interrupted. Verify means comparing counts and checksums by range, ideally with a shadow read that runs both paths and logs mismatches without affecting the user. Cut over is a feature flag on the read path, per tenant or percentage, so it can be reversed in seconds. Only after the new path has been serving reads for a while, in a separate deploy, do I remove the old column and the dual-write. The specific engine hazards I would name are always taking a short lock timeout on DDL and using concurrent index creation so a migration never blocks live queries.

08Caching at every layer

Caching is the highest-leverage and most dangerous tool in the box. Leverage, because a well-placed cache removes an order of magnitude of load for a few lines of code. Danger, because a cache converts a correctness problem into a timing problem — the bug is now "sometimes, for some users, for up to five minutes" — and because a system that depends on a warm cache has a hidden dependency that appears only during recovery, which is the worst possible moment.

The mental model: a cache is a derived, expendable copy with a staleness budget. Two questions decide everything: how stale may this be? and what happens when it is empty? If you cannot answer the second question with "the system is slower but works", you do not have a cache, you have an undeclared database.

Patterns, and the ordering rule that actually matters

Cache-aside (read: try cache, miss, load, populate; write: update the database, then invalidate) is the default and is right for almost everything. Write-through keeps the cache authoritative-ish and adds write latency. Write-behind buys write throughput and risks losing data. Refresh-ahead renews before expiry to avoid a latency cliff on hot keys.

The detail that separates levels: invalidate after the commit, and prefer deleting to updating. If you update the cache before the transaction commits, a rollback leaves the cache holding a value that never existed. If you write the new value into the cache instead of deleting it, two concurrent writers can interleave such that the older value lands last and sticks until TTL. Deleting is idempotent and self-healing; the next read repopulates from the source of truth.

The three failure modes you must be able to name

  • Stampede / dogpile. A hot key expires and a thousand concurrent requests all miss and all hit the database. Fixes: single-flight coalescing (what HybridCache does), a short lock per key, or probabilistic early expiration so one request refreshes slightly early while others still serve the old value.
  • Cold-start avalanche. A deploy, a Redis failover, or an eviction storm empties the cache and the database instantly sees the full uncached load — often 20–50x. This is why cache hit ratio must be on the dashboard and why the database must be sized to survive some multiple of steady-state, or the API must shed load while warming.
  • Synchronised expiry. Everything cached at deploy time expires at the same second forever after. Fix: jitter every TTL by ±10–20%.

Two adjacent techniques worth naming: negative caching (cache the "not found" for a short TTL, or an attacker can turn every miss into a database query) and, for large key spaces, a Bloom filter in front to answer "definitely not present" without a lookup.

HTTP caching is free infrastructure

The cheapest cache is the one you do not run. Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable on content-hashed assets means the browser never asks again. ETag plus If-None-Match turns a repeat API call into a 304 with no body — still a round trip, but no serialization and no database work. stale-while-revalidate lets a CDN serve slightly stale content instantly while refreshing in the background, which is the single best latency-versus-freshness trade for public read paths. And ASP.NET Core's output caching (since .NET 7, with tag-based eviction) covers the server-side equivalent for responses shared across users.

Interview trap

Treating the cache as an optimisation when the system cannot survive without it. The question that exposes this is always the same: "what happens if Redis is down?" If the answer is "everything fails", the cache is a critical dependency and must be designed like one — with a fallback to the origin, a circuit breaker so a slow Redis does not add latency to every request, and a capacity plan for the uncached load. A good answer states the degraded mode explicitly: "reads fall through to the database, which can serve roughly 30% of peak, so we shed the rest with 429s and keep writes working." A bad answer is "we'd add a Redis replica", which addresses availability but not the capacity question.

Gotcha

Caching per-user data under a key that is not per-user — and caching authorization decisions. The catastrophic version of the first is an output cache or CDN caching a personalised response and serving one tenant's document list to another; the rule is that anything varying by identity must either not be cached at a shared layer or must have identity in the key and Cache-Control: private. The second is subtler: caching "user X may edit document Y" means a revoked permission stays effective for the TTL. Cache the data, evaluate the permission — or keep authorization TTLs to seconds and support explicit invalidation on revocation.

Try it

1. Cause and then fix a stampede. Put a 500 ms database call behind a plain IMemoryCache.GetOrCreate (which does not coalesce concurrent factory calls), warm the key, wait for expiry, and fire 200 concurrent requests. Expected outcome: roughly 200 database calls in one burst and a latency spike. Swap to HybridCache.GetOrCreateAsync and repeat — one database call, the other 199 wait on it. That single-flight behaviour is the main reason to use it.

2. Measure what your cache is actually worth. On any endpoint, record hit ratio and the origin latency, then compute the effective latency as hit-ratio-weighted, and separately compute what the origin QPS would be at a 0% hit ratio. Expected outcome: you discover either that a cache with an 85% hit ratio is saving less than you thought (because the miss path dominates p99) or that your database could not survive a cold cache — both are conclusions worth having before an incident.

Solution sketch

For (1), instrument the factory with a counter to see the call count directly rather than inferring it from database metrics. Note that IMemoryCache stampede protection is exactly the gap HybridCache was created to fill, and that the equivalent hand-rolled fix is a ConcurrentDictionary<string, Lazy<Task<T>>> — worth writing once so you understand what the library does. For (2), the arithmetic is effective latency ≈ hit_ratio × hit_latency + (1 − hit_ratio) × miss_latency, and cold-cache origin load = full request rate; compare that against the origin's measured capacity, and if it exceeds it, you need either load shedding during warm-up or a staged warm-up that populates the top-N keys before taking traffic.

Interview Q&A

Where would you put a cache in this design, and why there?

I work outward-in and stop as early as possible, because the cheapest cache is the one closest to the user and the one I do not operate. Static assets get content-hashed URLs with a one-year immutable cache header, so the browser never revalidates. Public or semi-public GETs go to the CDN with a short TTL plus stale-while-revalidate, which gives instant responses and background refresh. Inside the application I cache things that are read constantly and change rarely — feature flags, tenant configuration, reference data — in process, and I use a distributed cache only for values that are expensive to compute and need to be shared across pods. What I try hard not to cache is anything already served by a one-millisecond indexed lookup, because that adds a coherence problem for almost no latency win.

What happens when your cache is empty?

That is the question that decides whether the cache is an optimisation or a dependency, and I want a specific number rather than a shrug. I compute the uncached request rate against the origin's measured capacity: if steady state is 400 requests per second at an 85% hit ratio, a cold cache means 400 hitting the database instead of 60, which is a factor of about seven and probably more than it can serve. Knowing that, the design needs one of three things: an origin sized for the cold case, a load shedder that returns 429s while the cache warms so the database is protected, or a staged warm-up that populates the top keys before the pod takes traffic. I would also make sure a Redis failover cannot take the site down — a circuit breaker around the cache client so a slow cache is skipped rather than added to every request's latency.

Cache invalidation: delete or update on write?

Delete, after the transaction commits. Deleting is idempotent and self-healing: the next reader repopulates from the source of truth, so a lost or duplicated invalidation costs at most one extra database read. Updating the cache with the new value looks more efficient but introduces a race — two concurrent writers can have their cache writes land in the opposite order from their database commits, leaving the older value cached until it expires, which is a genuinely hard bug to see. Ordering matters just as much: invalidating before the commit means a concurrent reader can repopulate the cache with the pre-commit value and pin it. So the sequence is commit, then delete, and if the delete can fail, the TTL is the backstop, which is why an unbounded TTL is a bad idea even when you invalidate explicitly.

Explain a cache stampede and three ways to prevent it.

A stampede is what happens when a hot key expires and every concurrent request misses simultaneously, so instead of one recomputation you get hundreds hitting the origin at once — and the origin is usually the expensive thing the cache existed to protect. The first prevention is single-flight coalescing: only one caller runs the factory for a key and the rest await the same task, which is what HybridCache does natively and what a plain memory cache does not. The second is probabilistic early expiration, where each reader has a small, increasing chance of refreshing before the true expiry, so the refresh is spread out and one request renews while the others still serve the old value. The third is to serve stale while revalidating in the background, which turns the worst case from a latency spike into slightly stale data. On top of any of them I jitter TTLs, because otherwise everything cached during a deploy expires in the same second forever.

How do you cache in a multi-tenant system without leaking data?

The tenant identifier goes in every cache key, at the front, without exception, and it is derived from the authenticated principal rather than from anything the client supplies. I would enforce that structurally — a key-building helper that takes a tenant context and refuses to produce a key without one — because a single hand-written key string is all it takes to serve one customer's data to another, and that is a breach, not a bug. At shared layers such as a CDN or output cache, anything that varies by identity is either not cached there at all or is marked private with the identity in the vary key. I would also add tenant-prefixed tags so I can invalidate everything for a tenant in one call, which matters for offboarding and for right-to-erasure requests.

HybridCache, IMemoryCache or IDistributedCache in 2026?

HybridCache for almost anything new. It has been generally available since .NET 9 and it gives you the two-level combination — in-process L1 and distributed L2 — plus stampede protection and tag-based invalidation, which is precisely the code everyone used to write badly by hand. IMemoryCache is still fine for genuinely local, cheap things like a parsed configuration object, and it is the layer HybridCache uses underneath. Raw IDistributedCache I would now treat as a backing abstraction rather than an application API, because its byte-array interface pushes serialization concerns into every call site. The one thing I would still be careful about is the L1 expiration: it should be much shorter than L2, because L1 is per pod and therefore your bound on cross-pod staleness.

When is an in-process cache dangerous?

When the values must be coherent across pods, because each pod has its own copy and invalidation does not cross the boundary — so after a write, some users see the new value and some see the old one for up to the TTL, and which one they see depends on load-balancer routing, which makes it look random and irreproducible. It is also dangerous for large objects, because it competes with the rest of the process for memory and can push you into garbage-collection pressure or a container OOM kill, and for anything security-sensitive such as permissions, where staleness means a revoked access still works. The way I make it safe is to keep the in-process TTL short — seconds — treat it strictly as a load reducer rather than a coherent store, and use a distributed cache or a pub-sub invalidation channel when coherence actually matters.

How do you cache an API response at the CDN when it varies by user?

Usually the honest answer is that you do not, and instead you split the response. The parts that are identical for everyone — the product catalogue, the document metadata, the rendered public shell — go through the CDN with a short TTL, and the per-user parts come from a separate uncached call or are hydrated client-side. If a shared cache must hold personalised content, the identity has to be in the cache key through a vary header or a signed edge key, and the response must be marked private so intermediaries do not share it; that is workable but it fragments the cache and usually destroys the hit ratio that motivated it. The failure I would be most careful to avoid is a misconfigured shared cache serving one user's response to another, which is why I would rather have a boring uncached endpoint than a clever cached one on any path carrying identity.

What TTL do you choose, and how?

From the product's staleness tolerance, not from a habit. I ask what the user-visible consequence of stale data is: for a document listing, thirty seconds is invisible; for a permissions check, thirty seconds is a security issue; for a currency rate, it may be a financial one. Then I set the TTL to the largest value that stays inside that tolerance, because a longer TTL means a higher hit ratio, and I add explicit invalidation on write so the typical staleness is far below the TTL and the TTL is only a backstop for missed invalidations. I always jitter it, and for hot keys I prefer a refresh-ahead or stale-while-revalidate strategy so expiry never produces a latency cliff. If I cannot get an answer about tolerance from the product side, that itself is the finding — it means nobody has decided, and I would rather force the decision than assume.

Redis or Valkey, and does the licence change matter?

Technically they are still close relatives — Valkey is the Linux Foundation fork created after Redis changed to a source-available licence in 2024, and the protocol and client libraries are compatible, so StackExchange.Redis talks to either. In practice the decision is usually made for you by what your cloud provider offers and supports, and both are available as managed services. The considerations I would actually raise are operational rather than ideological: what the managed offering does on failover, whether you are using it as a cache where data loss is acceptable or as a store where it is not, and whether you depend on any module or feature that has diverged between the two. My default is to use whichever the platform team already operates well, and to design so that the choice is reversible by keeping the usage to plain key-value and pub-sub rather than exotic features.

09Messaging, events and workflows

Asynchronous messaging is how you decouple availability, absorb bursts, and do work that must survive a process restart. The mental model that keeps you out of trouble: a message broker is a durable, replayable buffer between two components that are allowed to be down at different times. Everything hard about messaging — duplicates, ordering, poison messages, backlogs — follows from that sentence, because "allowed to be down" implies retries and retries imply duplicates.

Queue or log? They are different tools

The interview-grade way to say it: use a queue when you are handing out work, and a log when you are publishing facts that several independent consumers interpret differently. Kafka 4.0 (18 March 2025) removed ZooKeeper entirely — KRaft is now the only metadata mode — so if you mention a ZooKeeper ensemble in a 2026 design you are describing a system that cannot be built on current Kafka; the migration path is 3.x-to-KRaft first, then 4.x.

Delivery semantics, honestly

There are three claims and only two of them are real. At-most-once means you may lose messages (ack before processing). At-least-once means you may process twice (ack after processing) and is what every practical system uses. Exactly-once does not exist as an end-to-end network property; what Kafka offers is exactly-once processing semantics within Kafka via idempotent producers and transactional offset commits, which does not extend to the side effect you perform in a third system. So the honest lead answer is: "at-least-once delivery plus idempotent consumers equals effectively-once processing, and that is the only shape I would design."

// Idempotent consumer: the dedup record and the effect commit together.
public async Task Consume(ConsumeContext<DocumentUploaded> ctx)
{
    var msgId = ctx.MessageId ?? throw new InvalidOperationException("no message id");

    await using var tx = await _db.Database.BeginTransactionAsync(ctx.CancellationToken);

    // Unique index on (consumer, message_id) makes the second delivery lose the race.
    var inserted = await _db.ProcessedMessages
        .InsertIfAbsentAsync("thumbnailer", msgId, ctx.CancellationToken);
    if (!inserted) return;                      // already handled: ack and move on

    await _thumbnails.GenerateAsync(ctx.Message.DocumentId, ctx.CancellationToken);
    await _db.SaveChangesAsync(ctx.CancellationToken);
    await tx.CommitAsync(ctx.CancellationToken);
}

Note what makes this work: the deduplication row and the effect are in one local transaction. If the effect is in a different system (an S3 write, an email), you cannot do that, so you make the effect itself idempotent — a deterministic object key, a provider-side idempotency key — and accept that the ordering of "do it" and "record it" determines whether your failure mode is a duplicate or a missing side effect. Pick the one you can tolerate and say which.

Ordering, and why you probably need less of it than you think

Global ordering is expensive and rarely required. What is usually required is per-entity ordering: two updates to the same document must not be applied out of order, while updates to different documents are independent. That maps exactly onto partition-by-key in Kafka or session/group id in Service Bus: hash the entity id to a partition, and one consumer owns that partition, so order is preserved for that entity and parallelism scales with partition count. The follow-up to be ready for: a hot key becomes a hot partition, and no amount of adding consumers helps. The mitigations are a composite key that spreads a whale entity, or accepting out-of-order and using version numbers with last-write-wins on the consumer side.

Long-running workflows: sagas and process managers

When a business process spans services, it becomes a sequence of local transactions with compensations. Choreography (each service reacts to events) is simple for two or three steps and becomes untraceable at six — nobody can answer "where is order 4711 stuck?". Orchestration (an explicit coordinator holding the state machine) costs a component but gives you a single place to see state, apply timeouts, and compensate. For anything with more than about three steps, or with human approval or timeouts in it, use an orchestrator: MassTransit or Wolverine sagas, Azure Durable Functions, Temporal, or a hand-rolled state machine table. Compensations are business logic, not rollbacks — you do not "un-send" an email, you send a correction — and every step needs a timeout, because the failure mode of a saga is not an error, it is a process that silently stops.

Operating a broker

  • Dead-letter queues for poison messages, with a retry policy in front (a few immediate retries, then delayed retries, then DLQ). A DLQ nobody monitors is a data-loss mechanism with extra steps; alert on depth > 0.
  • Consumer lag is the health metric that matters, expressed as estimated time-to-drain rather than message count, and it is the right autoscaling signal.
  • Backlog drain plans. After a four-hour outage you have four hours of messages and normal capacity, so you will never catch up without extra consumers — and extra consumers may then flatten the database. This is the "recovery is the hard part" scenario interviewers love.
  • Schema evolution. Events outlive the code that wrote them, especially in a replayable log. Only additive changes, tolerant readers, a schema registry with compatibility checks, and an explicit event version field.

Interview trap

Claiming exactly-once delivery. Interviewers ask this deliberately. The correct answer names the impossibility (the two-generals problem: the sender cannot know whether an ack was lost or the message was), then states the practical construction — at-least-once plus an idempotent consumer keyed on a message id or a business key, with the dedup record committed in the same transaction as the effect. If you mention Kafka's exactly-once, be precise that it covers read-process-write within Kafka using transactional offsets, not your database write or your HTTP call. Candidates who say "we'd use exactly-once delivery" without that nuance are marked down immediately.

Gotcha

Publishing an event from inside the request handler, before or after the commit. Both orders are wrong. Publish before the commit and the transaction may roll back, so consumers act on something that never happened. Publish after the commit and the process may die in between, so the event is lost forever with no trace. There is no ordering that fixes this because it is a dual-write across two systems — the answer is the transactional outbox (unit 06): write the event row in the same transaction, and let a relay publish it. Say "dual write" out loud when you spot it; it is the vocabulary interviewers listen for.

Try it

1. Force duplicate delivery and defend against it. With a local RabbitMQ or Kafka (Aspire will compose either), write a consumer that performs a side effect and then throws after the effect but before the ack. Expected outcome: on redelivery the effect happens twice — a second thumbnail, a second email, a double increment. Then add the dedup table above and repeat: exactly one effect, and the second delivery is acked immediately. Finally, move the dedup insert to after the effect commits in a separate transaction and re-run to see the window reappear.

2. Build a backlog and drain it. Stop a consumer, produce 500,000 messages, then start it and measure the drain rate and time-to-empty; then scale consumers up and watch what happens to database CPU. Expected outcome: drain time falls roughly linearly with consumers until the database saturates, at which point adding consumers makes everything worse. That knee is the number you need before an incident, and it is why "we'll just scale the consumers" is an incomplete recovery plan.

Solution sketch

For (1) the key detail is the unique constraint doing the work: INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING returning zero rows is the "already processed" signal, and it is race-safe where a select-then-insert is not. Keep a TTL on the dedup table and index it by insertion time so it can be purged. For (2), instrument the drain with lag-in-seconds rather than message count, since that is what tells you whether you are gaining. The realistic mitigation set is: a consumer concurrency limit tied to database capacity, prioritisation so recent messages are processed before the backlog if the business prefers freshness, and a documented decision about whether stale backlog messages should be dropped rather than processed — for example, cache-invalidation events older than the TTL are pointless to replay.

Interview Q&A

Queue or event log for this system, and how do you choose?

I choose by asking whether I am distributing work or publishing facts. A queue is right when a message represents a task with exactly one intended outcome — resize this image, send this email, charge this card — because competing consumers scale naturally and a completed message can simply disappear. A log is right when the message is a fact that several independent consumers will interpret differently, and especially when I want replay: a new consumer can rebuild its state from history, which is what makes projections and analytics feasible without dual writes. Retention is the other discriminator: logs keep data by time regardless of consumption, so I can add a consumer next quarter and backfill. In a typical product I end up with both — a log for domain events and queues for the work those events trigger.

Can you guarantee exactly-once processing?

Not as a delivery property — that is impossible over an unreliable network, because the sender can never distinguish a lost message from a lost acknowledgement. What is achievable is effectively-once processing: at-least-once delivery combined with a consumer that is idempotent on a stable key, so a duplicate is detected and ignored. The implementation that makes it real is committing a deduplication record in the same transaction as the effect, so there is no window where one exists without the other. Kafka's exactly-once semantics are worth naming precisely: they cover read-process-write cycles within Kafka through idempotent producers and transactional offset commits, and they do not extend to a write in my database or a call to a payment provider. So the design rule is that every consumer must be safe to run twice, and I would treat any handler that is not as a defect.

How do you guarantee ordering when you need it?

By partitioning on the entity that needs ordering rather than trying to order everything. If updates to a single document must apply in order, I hash the document id to a partition or a session, and the broker guarantees order within it while different documents proceed in parallel. That converts a global constraint into a local one and keeps throughput proportional to partition count. The two consequences I would name are that parallelism is now capped by the number of partitions, which is awkward to change later in Kafka, and that a very hot entity becomes a hot partition that adding consumers cannot help. Where ordering is not strictly required I prefer to avoid it entirely by putting a version or timestamp on each event and having consumers discard anything older than what they have applied, which is more robust than depending on the transport.

Design the flow for "user uploads a document, we scan it, thumbnail it, and index it".

The API accepts the upload, streams the bytes straight to object storage rather than through the database, writes a metadata row in a pending state, and writes an outbox event in the same transaction — then returns 202 with a status URL. A relay publishes the event; three independent consumers pick it up for scanning, thumbnailing and indexing, each idempotent on the document id and each writing its own status back. The user-facing state is a projection of those three statuses, which lets the UI show progress and lets any single stage fail and retry without blocking the others. The parts I would highlight as design decisions are that the response is asynchronous because virus scanning has an unbounded tail; that the virus scan gates visibility, so the document is not downloadable until it passes, which is an ordering requirement expressed in state rather than in the broker; and that everything is derivable from the object in storage plus the metadata row, so a consumer's data can be rebuilt by replaying.

What is the outbox pattern solving that a broker's own guarantees do not?

The gap between my database and the broker. A broker can promise that once it has accepted a message it will deliver it, but it cannot promise that my database commit and my publish happen together, and that is a dual write across two systems with no shared transaction. If I publish first the transaction may roll back, and consumers act on a fact that never became true; if I commit first the process may die before publishing, and the event is lost with no record that it should have existed. The outbox removes the dual write by making the event a row in the same transaction, so atomicity is local, and a relay then moves rows to the broker with retries. The price is at-least-once publication and therefore duplicate events, plus an outbox table that needs indexing and purging — both of which are far easier problems than silent inconsistency.

Choreography or orchestration for a multi-step process?

Choreography for two or three steps where the coupling is genuinely low, orchestration once the process has real branching, timeouts, or a human in it. The deciding question I ask is operational rather than architectural: when a customer calls and asks why their order is stuck, can someone answer in under a minute? With choreography the state is smeared across services and event logs and the answer is usually no, which is what makes it expensive at scale even though it looks decoupled on a diagram. An orchestrator concentrates the state machine in one place, gives every step a timeout and a compensation, and makes the process queryable and testable — and modern tooling, whether MassTransit sagas, Durable Functions or Temporal, removes most of the implementation burden. The cost is a component that knows about the process, which is a real coupling, but I would rather have explicit coupling I can see than implicit coupling I cannot.

A consumer is failing on one message and blocking the queue. What do you do?

Immediately, I get it out of the way: a retry policy with a small number of fast attempts for transient faults, then delayed retries with backoff, then the dead-letter queue so the poison message stops blocking the partition or the queue. Then I diagnose from the DLQ rather than in production traffic, because the DLQ preserves the message and its headers. Structurally, the important part is that dead-lettering must be visible — an alert on DLQ depth above zero, with the message id and the failure reason in the telemetry — because a silent DLQ is data loss with extra steps. I would also make sure the handler distinguishes retryable from non-retryable failures, since retrying a validation error thirty times is pure load, and I would keep a documented replay path for reprocessing a DLQ after the bug is fixed.

How do you evolve an event schema that consumers already depend on?

Additively, and with a registry that enforces it. New optional fields are safe; removing a field, renaming one, changing a type, or changing the meaning of an existing field are all breaking, and in a replayable log they break not just current consumers but any future replay of history. So the rules are: tolerant readers that ignore unknown fields, explicit version numbers on the event, and a schema registry with backward-compatibility checks in CI so a breaking change fails the build rather than production. When a genuine break is unavoidable I publish both the old and the new event for a deprecation window and migrate consumers one at a time, which is the same expand-and-contract discipline as a database migration. The framing worth stating is that events are a published API with a much longer lifetime than a REST endpoint, because the log remembers.

Your consumers are four hours behind after an outage. Walk me through recovery.

First I establish whether the backlog is still growing, because if the drain rate is below the arrival rate nothing else matters until that is fixed. Then I decide what the backlog is worth: some messages are worthless when stale — cache invalidations older than the TTL, presence updates, notifications about something the user has already seen — and dropping or collapsing those is legitimate and often the fastest safe recovery. For the rest I scale consumers, but with an eye on the shared dependency, because the usual second incident is that the drain saturates the database and takes down the live path; so I would put a concurrency cap on the consumers tied to database headroom, and if necessary prioritise recent messages so current users are served while the tail catches up. Afterwards I would want two things in the postmortem: a documented drain plan with the measured knee point, and an alert on time-to-drain rather than on message count, since count alone does not tell you whether you are winning.

When is messaging the wrong answer?

When the caller needs the result to continue, when the work is trivially fast, or when the added eventual consistency is not something the product can absorb. Putting a queue in front of a five-millisecond database write buys nothing and costs you a broker, a consumer, duplicate handling, a dead-letter queue and a much harder debugging story. It is also the wrong answer when a strong invariant must hold across the two sides, because a queue is precisely a mechanism for letting them diverge temporarily. The heuristic I use is that messaging earns its place when the work can be done later, when the producer must be able to succeed while the consumer is down, or when several consumers want the same fact — and if none of those apply, an in-process call is simpler and more reliable. Being willing to say "this does not need a queue" is as valuable in an interview as knowing how to run one.

10Distributed systems and failure

This unit is the theory that makes the previous four coherent. The single idea underneath all of it: in a distributed system you cannot distinguish "slow", "dead" and "unreachable" — you only ever observe "no answer yet". Every distributed-systems concept is a strategy for making decisions under that ambiguity, and every distributed-systems bug is a place where someone assumed the ambiguity away.

CAP, and the more useful PACELC

CAP says that when a network Partition occurs, you must choose between Consistency and Availability. That is true but nearly useless in practice, because partitions are rare and the choice you actually make every day is a latency choice. PACELC extends it: if there is a Partition, choose A or C; Else (normal operation), choose Latency or Consistency. Every real system is a set of per-operation answers to PACELC, and saying it that way is a strong signal:

  • "Reading a document listing: EL — I take the lower latency of a replica and accept a second of staleness."
  • "Checking a seat is still available before charging: EC — I go to the primary and pay the latency."
  • "During a regional partition: A for reads and ingest, C for anything that moves money — writes are refused rather than made twice."

The insight that scores: users do not perceive "eventual consistency", they perceive specific broken interactions — my comment disappeared, the counter went down, I saw the old avatar. So the useful design conversation is not "strong or eventual" but "which session guarantees do the user journeys require?", and read-your-writes plus monotonic reads covers most of what people mean by "it should feel consistent".

Consensus, quorums and why you should not build one

Raft and Paxos solve one problem: getting a group of nodes to agree on an ordered log of decisions despite crashes, with a majority quorum. You will use them constantly — inside Kafka's KRaft controller, etcd behind Kubernetes, a database's failover coordinator — and you should essentially never implement one. What to know: a majority quorum means a cluster of 3 tolerates 1 failure and a cluster of 5 tolerates 2, even numbers buy nothing, and a partitioned minority cannot make progress, which is the availability cost of consistency made concrete. For quorum-replicated stores, R + W > N gives read-your-writes across replicas; that formula is worth remembering.

Time, and why you cannot trust it

Clocks drift, NTP steps, and a virtual machine can pause for a second under a noisy neighbour. Therefore: never use wall-clock comparison between machines to decide ordering or to expire a lock. Use monotonic clocks for durations, logical clocks or sequence numbers for ordering, and fencing tokens for mutual exclusion. This is the substance behind the classic Redis-lock critique: a lock with a TTL is a lease, and if the holder pauses past its lease (GC pause, CPU throttling, VM freeze) it will resume believing it still holds the lock while another process also holds it. The fix is a monotonically increasing fencing token checked by the resource on every write — and if the resource cannot check a token, then a distributed lock cannot give you correctness, only an optimisation. Google's Spanner buys real time bounds with atomic clocks and TrueTime; you do not have that.

The failure modes to enumerate on a whiteboard

  1. Partial failure — one of five dependencies is down. The design question is whether the feature degrades or the request fails.
  2. Gray failure — a node is up, passes health checks, and is slow or corrupting. Harder than a crash, because failover does not trigger. Outlier detection and per-instance latency metrics are the defence.
  3. Cascading failure — one slow dependency fills every caller's thread pool. Bulkheads and timeouts (unit 11).
  4. Metastable failure — the system stays broken after the trigger is removed, because retries and cold caches sustain the load. This is why "just restart it" often fails and why load shedding is needed to break the loop.
  5. Correlated failure — a bad config, a certificate expiry, or a poison deploy hits all instances at once. Redundancy does not help; staged rollout, config validation and certificate expiry alerts do.
  6. Split brain — two nodes both believe they are primary. Prevented by quorum and fencing, not by heuristics.

// holder cannot write after losing the lease. Without the resource-side check,
// a distributed lock is an optimisation, not a correctness mechanism.
public sealed record Lease(string Owner, long Token, DateTimeOffset ExpiresAt);

// RETURNING token;

// Write, guarded: the target refuses stale tokens.

Interview trap

Reciting CAP as a menu choice ("we'll pick AP"). Three problems. First, P is not optional — partitions happen whether you choose them or not, so the real choice is only what you do during one. Second, systems are not uniformly C or A; a single product has operations at both ends, and the mature answer is per-operation. Third, CAP says nothing about the normal case, which is where 99.99% of your time is spent — that is the "ELSE" of PACELC, a latency-versus-consistency trade you make on every read. If you can reframe a CAP question into "here is what each operation does during a partition, and here is the latency-consistency choice the rest of the time", you have converted a trivia question into a design discussion.

Gotcha

Assuming a timeout means the work did not happen. A timeout tells you nothing about the remote side — the request may have completed, may be in progress, or may never have arrived. Code that treats a timeout as failure and retries will double-apply unless the operation is idempotent; code that treats it as success will lose work. This is the single most common source of duplicate charges, duplicate emails and duplicate rows in real systems. The correct posture: every remote mutation gets an idempotency key, and every ambiguous outcome either retries safely or is resolved by a reconciliation process that asks the remote side what actually happened.

Try it

1. Break a lease with a pause. Write two processes that acquire a Redis or database lease with a 5-second TTL and then write to a shared row. Suspend the holder (SIGSTOP, or a long Thread.Sleep under a debugger break) for longer than the TTL, let the second process acquire, then resume the first. Expected outcome: both write, and the resource has no idea. Add a fencing token column and the guarded update above, repeat: the resumed process's write affects zero rows and it can detect that it lost the lease. This is the experiment that makes "locks are leases" permanent knowledge.

2. Simulate a partition, not a crash. With two containers, use iptables or a toxiproxy to drop packets rather than stopping the process, and watch how differently the system behaves from a clean kill: connections hang for the OS timeout, health checks may still pass on the local side, and clients accumulate. Expected outcome: you will see that timeouts, not failures, are what actually take systems down, and that a missing client-side timeout turns a partition into an outage.

Solution sketch

For (1), the point to internalise is that no lock implementation — Redlock included — can prevent this on its own, because the pause happens inside the holder where the lock service cannot see it. Only the resource enforcing monotonic tokens closes the window. For (2), the defaults matter: a .NET HttpClient with no timeout will wait 100 seconds, a TCP connect on a black-holed route can take tens of seconds, and Npgsql has its own connect and command timeouts. Set all three explicitly, then check that the total across a call chain is less than the caller's timeout, or your timeouts are decorative.

Interview Q&A

Explain CAP, then tell me why PACELC is more useful.

CAP says that during a network partition a system must choose between remaining consistent and remaining available, since it cannot be both when nodes cannot talk. It is true but limited, because partitions are rare and it says nothing about the other 99.99% of the time. PACELC adds that: if partitioned, choose availability or consistency; else, choose latency or consistency. That second clause describes the decision I actually make constantly — whether a read goes to a lagging replica or a nearby cache for speed, or to the primary for correctness. It also forces the useful framing that the choice is per operation rather than per system, so within one product I might serve listings from replicas and check inventory on the primary, and during a partition accept writes for uploads but refuse them for payments.

What consistency guarantees do users actually notice?

Session guarantees, not the abstract model. Users notice when their own write does not appear on the next screen, which is read-your-writes; they notice when a value goes forwards and then backwards because two reads hit replicas with different lag, which is monotonic reads; and they notice when a reply appears before the message it answers, which is causal ordering. None of those requires linearizability across the whole system, and providing them is much cheaper — sticky routing after a write, a sticky replica per session, and a causal token carried in the client. So when someone says "the system must be consistent", the productive response is to ask which of those three properties they mean for which journey, because the answer usually turns a very expensive requirement into a modest one.

Why can't a distributed lock give you mutual exclusion?

Because a lock over a network is really a lease with an expiry, and the holder can be paused for longer than the lease without knowing it — a garbage-collection pause, CPU throttling in a container, a virtual machine freeze, or simply a long network stall. When it resumes it still believes it holds the lock, while another process legitimately acquired it, and both write. No lock service can prevent this, because the pause happens inside the client where the service has no visibility. The only real fix is a fencing token: the lease hands out a monotonically increasing number and the protected resource rejects any write carrying a number lower than the highest it has seen. If the resource cannot enforce that, then the lock is a performance optimisation that reduces contention, not a correctness mechanism, and the system must be safe when it fails.

A service call times out. What do you assume happened?

Nothing — that is the point. A timeout is the absence of information: the request may have been lost on the way, may have completed with the response lost on the way back, or may still be running. So the code cannot branch on "it failed"; it has to be designed so that both possibilities are survivable. In practice that means the operation carries an idempotency key so a retry is safe, and where the operation cannot be made idempotent, the caller records intent before acting and a reconciliation process later asks the downstream what actually happened. This is where duplicate charges and duplicate emails come from in real systems, and it is why I would call out any mutating remote call in a design that lacks an idempotency key.

What is a metastable failure and how do you get out of one?

It is a state where the system stays broken after the original trigger is gone, because the recovery dynamics themselves sustain the overload. The classic shape is a brief slowdown that causes timeouts, timeouts that cause retries, retries that triple the load, and a cache that emptied during the incident so every request now hits the database — remove the trigger and the loop keeps running. You cannot exit by adding capacity, because the amplified load scales with the capacity you add. You exit by reducing the input: shed load aggressively, open circuit breakers, disable retries temporarily, and let the system serve a fraction of traffic successfully so caches refill and queues drain, then ramp back. Designing to avoid it means bounded retries with budgets, jittered backoff, and load shedding that is automatic rather than a human decision at three in the morning.

How many nodes in a consensus cluster, and why odd?

Three for most things, five when the availability requirement justifies the extra cost and latency. Consensus needs a majority to make progress, so three tolerates one failure and five tolerates two. Even numbers are wasteful because four still tolerates only one failure — the majority is three either way — while adding a node that increases the chance of a failure and the cost of every commit. The other half of the answer is placement: three nodes in one availability zone tolerate a process failure but not a zone failure, so for a zone-tolerant cluster they go in three zones, and for cross-region you have to weigh the commit latency of a quorum spanning tens of milliseconds. And a partitioned minority cannot make progress at all, which is exactly the availability cost of choosing consistency.

What is a gray failure and why is it worse than a crash?

A gray failure is a component that is up and passing its health checks but performing badly or incorrectly — a node with a degraded disk, a pod being CPU-throttled, an instance with a corrupted cache, or a dependency returning success with wrong data. It is worse than a crash because none of the automatic protections trigger: the load balancer keeps sending traffic, the orchestrator does not restart it, and failover does not happen, so a fraction of users have a bad time indefinitely while the aggregate dashboards look acceptable. The defences are per-instance metrics rather than only aggregates, outlier detection at the load balancer that ejects instances whose latency or error rate diverges from their peers, health checks that exercise a real code path instead of returning 200, and end-to-end synthetic checks that would notice wrong answers. The general lesson is that averages hide gray failures, so I want percentiles broken down by instance.

How do you handle clock skew in a distributed system?

By never depending on clock comparison across machines for correctness. For measuring durations I use a monotonic clock, because the wall clock can step backwards when NTP corrects it, which has produced negative durations and infinite loops in real systems. For ordering I use logical means — a database sequence, a per-entity version number, or a hybrid logical clock — rather than timestamps from different hosts. For expiry I have the authoritative party evaluate it: a token is validated by the issuer's clock domain, and a lease is enforced by fencing rather than by the holder's opinion of the time. Where timestamps must be compared across systems, such as a token's validity window, I allow a small explicit skew tolerance and I monitor actual drift, because a machine with a badly wrong clock is an incident that manifests as inexplicable authentication failures.

Design for a full region outage. What do you actually do?

First I make the business decide the recovery objectives, because active-active, warm standby and backup-restore differ by orders of magnitude in cost and the engineering follows from the numbers rather than from ambition. With an RTO of hours and an RPO of minutes, asynchronous cross-region replication and infrastructure as code that can rebuild the stack is sufficient, and it is dramatically cheaper than the alternative. With near-zero RTO you need traffic management, data replicated continuously, and — the hard part — an answer for writes: either one region owns writes for a given tenant so failover is a routing change, or you accept conflict resolution, which is a product decision about what happens when two regions both accepted a change. Then the parts people forget: the failover must be rehearsed on a schedule, dependencies such as identity providers and secret stores must also be regional, and the DNS or global load balancer must fail over faster than the TTLs allow, which is why anycast or a health-checked global router beats DNS games.

Where does idempotency show up across a whole architecture?

Everywhere there is a retry, which is everywhere. At the API edge it is the idempotency key on unsafe requests so a client retry does not duplicate an order. Between services it is the same key propagated, or a deterministic derivation of it, so a retried call downstream does not double-apply. In message consumers it is a deduplication record committed with the effect, because delivery is at least once by construction. In workflows it is the state machine refusing to re-run a completed step. And in data pipelines it is upserts keyed on a natural identity so a replayed batch converges rather than duplicating. The way I frame it in an interview is that idempotency is the property that lets you retry, and retrying is the only recovery mechanism a distributed system has — so a design without idempotency has no recovery story, whatever else it has.

11Resilience and load management

Unit 10 said failures are ambiguous; this unit is what you build because of that. The mental model: resilience is about bounding blast radius in two dimensions — time (how long a failure can affect you) and breadth (how much of the system it can reach). Timeouts bound time. Bulkheads, circuit breakers and load shedding bound breadth. A system without both will convert any single dependency problem into a full outage, every time.

Timeouts are the foundation, and they are usually wrong

Every remote call needs an explicit timeout, and the defaults are all wrong for a web service: HttpClient defaults to 100 seconds, a TCP connect to a black-holed host can hang for tens of seconds, and an unbounded database command will wait as long as the lock does. Three rules:

  • Set the timeout from the dependency's measured p99, not from a round number. If p99 is 120 ms, a 250 ms timeout is generous; a 30-second timeout means one sick dependency can hold your threads for 30 seconds each.
  • Timeouts must decrease down the call chain. If the client gives up at 2 s and your service waits 5 s on a downstream, you are doing work nobody will read while holding resources. Propagate a deadline (a CancellationToken from HttpContext.RequestAborted plus a budget) rather than configuring independent numbers.
  • Cancellation must actually flow. Passing CancellationToken through every async call is not style — it is what makes a timeout release the thread, the connection and the database work instead of merely abandoning the caller.

Retries: necessary, and the most common cause of outages

Retry only what is safe (idempotent) and only what is likely transient (a connection reset, a 503, a 429 with Retry-After) — never a 400 or a 422, which will fail identically forever. Use exponential backoff with full jitter, because synchronised retries produce a thundering herd; cap the total attempts; and add a retry budget so retries can never exceed, say, 10% of traffic no matter how many callers are unhappy. Retry at one layer only: if the HTTP client retries and the caller retries and the message consumer retries, you have 27 attempts for one user action.

// .NET 8+: Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience wraps Polly v8 with defensible defaults.
builder.Services.AddHttpClient<SearchClient>()
    .AddResilienceHandler("search", (pipeline, ctx) =>
    {
        pipeline.AddTimeout(TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(400));           // total attempt budget

        pipeline.AddRetry(new HttpRetryStrategyOptions
        {
            MaxRetryAttempts = 2,                                       // 3 calls at most
            BackoffType = DelayBackoffType.Exponential,
            UseJitter = true,                                           // full jitter: no herd
            Delay = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(50),
            ShouldHandle = args => ValueTask.FromResult(
                args.Outcome.Result is { StatusCode: HttpStatusCode.ServiceUnavailable }
                || args.Outcome.Exception is HttpRequestException or TimeoutRejectedException)
        });

        pipeline.AddCircuitBreaker(new HttpCircuitBreakerStrategyOptions
        {
            FailureRatio = 0.5,                    // half the calls failing...
            MinimumThroughput = 20,                // ...over a meaningful sample
            SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30),
            BreakDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15)
        });

        pipeline.AddConcurrencyLimiter(permitLimit: 24, queueLimit: 8);  // bulkhead
    });

The order in the pipeline matters and is worth being able to explain: an attempt timeout inside the retry bounds each attempt, a total timeout outside bounds the whole operation, the circuit breaker sits outside the retry so that repeated failures open it rather than being hidden by retries, and the concurrency limiter is the outermost gate that stops this dependency from consuming the whole process.

Circuit breakers: fail fast so you can recover

A breaker watches a rolling window; when the failure ratio crosses a threshold over a minimum sample size it opens, and calls fail immediately without touching the dependency. After a break duration it goes half-open and lets a trial through: success closes it, failure re-opens it. Two subtleties interviewers probe: the minimum-throughput requirement (without it, two failures out of two open the breaker on a quiet service), and what you do while it is open — a breaker without a fallback just converts slow errors into fast errors. The fallback is usually stale cache, a reduced feature, or a queued write.

Bulkheads and load shedding

A bulkhead is a per-dependency concurrency limit, so a slow dependency can consume only its own allocation of threads and connections instead of the whole process. This is the direct defence against cascading failure: without it, one dependency at 5-second latency fills every thread and the service stops serving the other 90% of requests that do not need it.

Load shedding is the admission-control side: when in-flight work exceeds what you can serve inside the SLO, reject the excess immediately with 429 or 503 plus Retry-After. This is counter-intuitive but essential — serving 70% of requests well beats serving 100% of them past the timeout, because a timed-out request consumed resources and delivered nothing. ASP.NET Core's built-in rate limiter (since .NET 7) gives you fixed-window, sliding-window, token-bucket and concurrency limiters with queueing; the design choices are the limit (from Little's law), the queue depth (small — a deep queue just adds latency before failing), and the partition key (per tenant or per API key, so one customer cannot consume everyone's capacity).

Interview trap

Adding retries without a budget, a breaker, or idempotency. Candidates reach for "we'd add retries with exponential backoff" as a complete answer. It is half an answer and the dangerous half: retries multiply load onto a component that is already failing, and without idempotency they duplicate side effects. The complete answer names all four pieces — retry only idempotent operations, only on transient failures, with jittered backoff and a bounded budget, behind a circuit breaker that stops generating load when the dependency is clearly down. If you can add "and I would retry at exactly one layer", you have covered the thing that actually causes the outage.

Gotcha

Health checks that pass while the service is useless, and readiness that flaps. A health endpoint returning a hard-coded 200 tells you the process started, nothing more; one that checks every dependency means a single dependency blip pulls every pod out of rotation simultaneously, which is a self-inflicted outage. The workable middle: liveness checks only the process, readiness checks the dependencies the pod genuinely cannot work without, and everything else is reported as degraded through metrics rather than by removing capacity. Add hysteresis so a probe does not oscillate, and never let the aggregate of readiness failures be able to empty the entire pool — some platforms support a minimum-available guard for exactly this.

Try it

1. Cause a cascade, then bulkhead it. Build a service with two endpoints, one calling a fast dependency and one calling a slow one, and make the slow dependency take 5 seconds. Load both. Expected outcome: the fast endpoint's latency collapses too, because the slow calls have consumed the shared thread and connection capacity. Add a per-dependency concurrency limiter around the slow call and repeat: the slow endpoint degrades and starts rejecting, the fast one stays healthy. That contrast is the entire argument for bulkheads, and it is far more convincing than the diagram.

2. Compare shedding with queueing. Take an endpoint at its concurrency limit and configure, in turn, a deep queue (1,000) and a shallow one (10) with fast rejection. Push 3x the sustainable load through each. Expected outcome: the deep queue produces high latency and mass timeouts where clients have already given up — work done for nobody — while the shallow one produces immediate 429s and stable latency for the requests it accepts. Then compute goodput for each; the shedding configuration wins clearly.

Solution sketch

For (1), the .NET-specific mechanism to observe is the thread pool: threadpool-queue-length climbing while CPU stays low is the cascade signature, and the bulkhead prevents it by refusing to start more slow work rather than by making it faster. For (2), the metric that makes the argument is goodput — responses delivered within the SLO — rather than throughput, and the second-order observation is that clients that time out usually retry, so a deep queue also increases arrival rate. This is why the guidance is a queue just deep enough to absorb jitter, on the order of one to two times the permit limit, and no deeper.

Interview Q&A

How do you choose a timeout value?

From the measured latency distribution of the dependency and the budget I have inherited from my caller, never from a round number. I start at roughly the dependency's p99 plus a margin, because anything slower than that is already an anomaly and waiting longer mostly consumes my own capacity. Then I check the arithmetic down the chain: my timeout plus my retries has to fit inside my caller's timeout, or I am doing work for a client that has already gone away. In practice I prefer propagating a deadline rather than configuring independent timeouts at each hop, because independent numbers drift out of alignment as services change. And the timeout is only real if cancellation flows — the token has to reach the database command and the HTTP call, or the request is abandoned while its resources are still held.

When is a retry harmful?

When the operation is not idempotent, when the failure is deterministic, and when the dependency is overloaded rather than briefly unavailable. Retrying a non-idempotent write turns one charge into two; retrying a validation error just triples the load for the same outcome; and retrying against a saturated service is the mechanism that converts a slowdown into an outage, because the offered load rises exactly when capacity falls. Retries also compose badly across layers, so three layers with three attempts each is twenty-seven calls for one user action, which is why I want retries at exactly one clearly designated layer. The defences are a retry budget capping retries as a fraction of traffic, jittered backoff so attempts do not synchronise, and a circuit breaker that stops the retries entirely once failure is sustained rather than transient.

Explain a circuit breaker's states and its parameters.

Closed is normal operation with failures being counted in a rolling window. When the failure ratio exceeds a threshold over a minimum number of calls, it opens and every subsequent call fails immediately without touching the dependency, which both protects the dependency and stops me burning threads waiting. After a break duration it moves to half-open and lets a limited number of trial calls through; success closes it, failure re-opens it. The parameters that need thought are the failure ratio and the minimum throughput — without a minimum sample a quiet endpoint opens on two failures — and the break duration, which should be long enough for the dependency to recover but short enough that recovery is noticed quickly. The part people skip is what happens while it is open: without a fallback, a breaker converts slow failures into fast failures, which helps the dependency and not the user.

What is a bulkhead and what does it protect against?

A bulkhead is a per-dependency limit on concurrent work, so that a slow or failing dependency can only consume its own allocation of threads, connections and memory rather than the whole process. It is the specific defence against cascading failure: without it, an endpoint calling a five-second dependency will accumulate in-flight requests until the thread pool and connection pools are exhausted, and every other endpoint in the same process stops working even though its dependencies are healthy. With it, the affected endpoint degrades and starts rejecting quickly while the rest of the service stays fine, which converts a total outage into a partial one. In .NET this is a concurrency limiter around the call, ideally combined with a small queue and fast rejection, and the limit itself comes from Little's law on that dependency's own throughput and latency.

Why shed load instead of queueing it?

Because past a certain point queueing produces work that nobody will use. A request that waits four seconds in a queue and then gets served has usually already been abandoned by the client, which has likely retried, so the queue increased both latency and load while delivering nothing. Rejecting immediately with a 429 and a Retry-After keeps latency stable for the requests you do accept, gives the client actionable information, and holds the system at the point of maximum goodput. The metric that makes the argument is goodput — successful responses within the SLO — rather than raw throughput. I would still keep a small queue to absorb short bursts and jitter, on the order of the permit limit itself, but a deep queue is a latency amplifier disguised as resilience.

How do you decide fail-open versus fail-closed?

By asking what the cost of a wrong answer is compared with the cost of no answer. Anything touching authorization, payments, safety or compliance fails closed, because allowing an action because the checking service was unavailable is exactly the failure an attacker would try to induce. Additive or advisory features fail open: recommendations, related content, enrichment, personalisation — the page renders without them and the user may not even notice. The interesting middle cases need a product decision, such as whether an upload should be accepted when the virus scanner is down, and my answer there would be to accept the upload but hold it invisible until scanned, which is fail-closed on visibility and fail-open on ingestion. What matters in an interview is showing that this is decided per feature, in advance, and written down rather than being an accident of how the code handles exceptions.

Where do you put rate limiting, and what do you limit on?

At several layers, for different purposes. At the edge, a coarse limit per IP or per API key protects against abuse and volumetric attacks and should be handled by infrastructure that is cheap to run. Inside the application, a limit per tenant or per user protects fairness so one customer's bulk import cannot consume everyone's capacity, and that one needs the authenticated identity, so it sits after authentication. Then per-dependency concurrency limits protect specific downstreams. The algorithm choice follows the intent: a token bucket allows bursts within a sustained rate, which suits APIs, while a concurrency limiter maps directly to Little's law and suits protecting a database. Practically, the limit must be observable and the response must include Retry-After and clear headers, because a rate limit that clients cannot reason about produces retry storms rather than backpressure.

A downstream service starts responding in 5 seconds instead of 50 ms. Trace the consequences.

By Little's law, my in-flight count for that call path rises a hundredfold, so with unchanged arrival rate I need a hundred times the concurrency I have. Requests accumulate, the thread pool queue grows, connection pools drain, and — the important part — endpoints that have nothing to do with that dependency start failing too, because they share the process's resources. Then my own callers time out and retry, multiplying the load, and my readiness probe may start failing so pods are pulled from rotation, concentrating the same load on fewer instances. The defences that break this chain are a tight timeout so each call fails fast, a bulkhead so only a bounded number of requests can be in that state at once, a circuit breaker so sustained failure stops the calls altogether, and load shedding at the entry point. I would also make sure the fallback is defined, because failing fast without a fallback is still a broken feature, just a cheaper one.

How do you test resilience rather than assume it?

By injecting the failures deliberately and often enough that the results are trusted. At the unit level, tests that assert the fallback path and the timeout behaviour, which is easy once resilience is expressed as a pipeline rather than scattered try-catch blocks. At the integration level, a proxy such as toxiproxy that adds latency, drops packets or returns errors, so I can exercise partitions rather than clean shutdowns — which behave completely differently. Then game days in a staging or production environment, where the team practises a specific failure with the runbook open, because the goal is as much to test the humans and the observability as the code. The mark of maturity is that failure injection is scheduled and automated rather than heroic, and that every incident postmortem adds a scenario to the set.

What resilience do you deliberately not build?

Anything whose failure mode is cheaper than the mechanism protecting against it. I would not build multi-region active-active for an internal tool where a few hours of downtime costs nothing, and I would not add a circuit breaker and a fallback around a dependency that is in the same process. I am also cautious about automatic remediation that acts on ambiguous signals, because an automated failover triggered by a false positive can cause the outage it was meant to prevent — split brain, or a stampede onto a cold standby. The framing I would use is that every resilience mechanism adds a code path that is rarely exercised and can itself fail, so each one needs to justify its complexity against a real, costed failure scenario. Saying no to unnecessary resilience is the same judgment as saying no to unnecessary scale.

Data fetching is a distributed-systems problem

Modern front ends have converged on a server-state cache — TanStack Query, RTK Query, SWR, or a framework's own loader/cache — and the reason is exactly the reason you have Redis: the same data is needed in many places, it is expensive to fetch, and it goes stale. The patterns map one-to-one onto the backend ones:

  • Stale-while-revalidate in the client cache is the same trade as at the CDN: show instantly, refresh in the background.
  • Request deduplication is single-flight coalescing — three components asking for the same key produce one request.
  • Optimistic updates are optimistic concurrency in the UI: apply locally, reconcile on the server response, roll back on conflict. And they need the same conflict policy conversation.
  • Retries and backoff belong here too, with the same idempotency requirement — which is why the Idempotency-Key from unit 04 should be generated by the client, once per user intent, and reused across the retry.
// The client is a cache with a staleness budget - state that explicitly.
const { data } = useQuery({
  queryKey: ['documents', tenantId, { page }],
  queryFn: ({ signal }) => api.listDocuments({ tenantId, page, signal }), // cancellation flows
  staleTime: 30_000,          // do not refetch for 30s: our agreed staleness budget
  gcTime: 5 * 60_000,
});

// Optimistic write = optimistic concurrency in the UI. Same conflict policy question.
const rename = useMutation({

    await qc.cancelQueries({ queryKey: ['documents', tenantId] });
    const previous = qc.getQueryData(['documents', tenantId]);
    qc.setQueryData(['documents', tenantId], (old) => applyRename(old, v));

  },

The scaling facts to have ready: a persistent connection is state, so N clients over M servers means each server holds N/M connections and any message must reach whichever server holds the target connection — that is what SignalR's Redis backplane or Azure SignalR Service does, and it is why connection count, not message rate, is usually the binding constraint. Reconnection needs exponential backoff with jitter or a deploy becomes a self-inflicted DDoS as every client reconnects at once. And any realtime channel needs a resume protocol: a client that was disconnected for 30 seconds must be able to catch up (a sequence number and a replay window) rather than silently missing events.

Performance budgets are architecture

Core Web Vitals in 2026: LCP (largest contentful paint) under 2.5 s, INP (interaction to next paint, which replaced FID in March 2024) under 200 ms, CLS under 0.1. These are architectural constraints, not CSS polish. LCP is decided by how many round trips stand between the HTML and the first meaningful pixel — which is a caching and rendering-strategy decision. INP is decided by main-thread work — which is a bundle-size and hydration decision. CLS is decided by whether you reserve space for async content — which is an API-shape decision, because if your API does not return dimensions, the layout must shift.

Interview trap

Treating the front end as "just the UI" and ignoring what it does to the backend. Front-end choices are load-generating decisions: a component that polls every second with 50,000 concurrent users is 50,000 requests per second, which dwarfs everything else in the design. A page that fires eleven parallel API calls on mount has made your p99 the maximum of eleven distributions, not the average. A retry loop in the client with no backoff is a DDoS generator aimed at yourself. In a full-stack round, saying "the client caches this for 30 seconds and coalesces duplicate requests, so the backend sees one call per user per 30 seconds rather than one per component mount" shows you understand that the browser is part of the capacity plan.

Gotcha

Storing access tokens in localStorage. It is readable by any script on the origin, so a single XSS — including one from a compromised npm dependency — exfiltrates a token that is valid until it expires, everywhere, with no way to revoke a single browser. The current recommended pattern is the BFF (unit 04): tokens live server-side, the browser holds an encrypted, HTTP-only, SameSite=Lax or Strict cookie, and CSRF is handled by SameSite plus an antiforgery token. If tokens genuinely must live in the browser, keep them in memory only, keep lifetimes very short, and pair with refresh-token rotation and a strict Content Security Policy — but say clearly that this is the weaker option.

Try it

1. Measure what your front end does to your backend. Open your app's main screen with the network tab filtered to XHR and count requests on mount, then count again after a navigation and back. Expected outcome: more requests than you expected, several duplicated across components, and at least one that could have been cached. Add a query cache with a 30-second stale time and re-measure — a typical dashboard drops by half or more, which is a capacity change achieved with no backend work at all.

2. Break realtime on purpose. Run a SignalR or SSE endpoint with a few hundred simulated clients, then restart the server. Expected outcome: all clients reconnect simultaneously and the server takes a load spike far above steady state; if any of them also refetch their state on reconnect, the database sees it too. Add jittered exponential backoff on reconnect and a sequence-number-based resume so clients fetch a delta instead of a full reload, then repeat and compare the spike.

Solution sketch

For (1), the numbers to write down are requests per screen and bytes per screen, then multiply by your peak concurrent users to get the backend load the front end is generating — that product is the honest capacity input, and it is usually the first time anyone has computed it. For (2), the reconnect storm is the same thundering-herd problem as cache expiry, and the fixes are identical: jitter, plus a cheap resume path so the recovery request is small. SignalR's automatic reconnect has a default retry schedule you should override with jitter, and the server should be able to shed reconnections with a 503 and Retry-After rather than accepting them all and falling over.

Interview Q&A

SSR, CSR or static for this product, and why?

I decide per route rather than per application, because a product usually contains all three shapes. Marketing and documentation pages are static and served from a CDN, because they change slowly, must be indexable, and get the best possible first paint for free. The authenticated application shell can be client-rendered, since SEO is irrelevant behind a login and the user pays the bundle cost once. Pages that mix — a shared document link that must render fast for someone who is not logged in — are the case for server rendering or streaming, where the shell arrives immediately and slower sections stream in. The costs I would name are that server rendering puts CPU on my servers per request and needs its own scaling and caching story, and that static generation needs a revalidation pipeline with an explicit staleness window.

Where do you store the access token in a SPA?

Ideally nowhere in the browser. The pattern I would propose is a backend-for-frontend that performs the OpenID Connect flow, keeps the access and refresh tokens server-side, and gives the browser only an encrypted, HTTP-only, SameSite cookie that identifies the session. That removes the entire class of token theft via cross-site scripting, including from a compromised third-party script, and it gives me server-side revocation, which a bearer token in the browser does not. If a BFF is genuinely impossible, the fallback is to keep the token in memory only — never localStorage or sessionStorage — with very short lifetimes, rotating refresh tokens and a strict content security policy, and I would say plainly that this is the weaker option. Whatever the choice, cookies mean CSRF has to be handled, so SameSite plus an antiforgery token on unsafe methods.

The dashboard makes eleven API calls on load. What do you do?

First I check what the p99 actually is, because with eleven parallel calls the page's latency is the maximum of eleven distributions, so even a small tail on any one of them dominates the experience. Then I would consolidate at the edge rather than in the browser: a backend-for-frontend endpoint that returns exactly what the screen needs in one round trip, composed server-side on a fast internal network. Where consolidation is not possible, I would prioritise — render the shell and the primary widget first and load the rest progressively, so the perceived latency is the fastest call rather than the slowest. I would also add client-side caching with an explicit stale time so a navigation back does not refetch everything, and deduplication so two components asking for the same data produce one request. The framing I would use is that this is a chattiness problem, identical in kind to a chatty service-to-service design, and the fix is the same: change the granularity of the contract.

Polling, SSE or WebSockets for live updates?

I choose the least stateful thing that meets the latency requirement. If updates every thirty seconds are acceptable, polling is genuinely fine and it is the only option that is trivially cacheable and stateless, which matters more at scale than people admit. If the flow is server-to-client only — notifications, progress, a feed — server-sent events give me a single HTTP connection with built-in reconnection and no protocol upgrade, and it is underrated for exactly this. WebSockets, and SignalR in .NET, are for genuinely bidirectional low-latency interaction such as collaborative editing or presence. The costs to name are that any persistent connection is server state, so scaling out needs a backplane to route a message to whichever server holds that client, connection count becomes the binding capacity limit rather than request rate, and reconnection storms after a deploy need jittered backoff and a resume protocol.

How does the front end affect backend capacity?

Directly and often dominantly, which is why I would compute it. Requests per screen multiplied by screens per session multiplied by concurrent users is the arrival rate my backend sees, and a single decision — a component that polls every second, or a missing client cache that refetches on every navigation — can change that by an order of magnitude. Client retry policy matters just as much: a retry loop without backoff aimed at a struggling API is a self-inflicted denial of service, and it is the browser tier that decides whether a partial outage stays partial. Conversely, the cheapest capacity win available is usually client-side, because a thirty-second stale time on a query removes most repeat traffic for free. So in a full-stack design I treat the front end as a load generator with a configuration, and I put its caching and retry behaviour in the design explicitly.

What are Core Web Vitals and why are they architectural?

They are the three user-centric metrics Google standardised: largest contentful paint under 2.5 seconds, interaction to next paint under 200 milliseconds since it replaced first input delay in 2024, and cumulative layout shift under 0.1. They are architectural because each is decided by a structural choice rather than by styling. Largest contentful paint is a function of how many round trips separate the HTML from the first meaningful pixel, which is a rendering-strategy and caching decision. Interaction to next paint is a function of main-thread work, which is bundle size, hydration cost and how much JavaScript runs on interaction. Cumulative layout shift is a function of whether space is reserved for asynchronous content, which frequently depends on whether the API returns dimensions or counts up front — an API design question. That is why I would treat a performance budget as a constraint on the design rather than a task for the end of the project.

Would you use Blazor here?

It is a real option and I would evaluate it on team and product shape rather than on ideology. For an internal, line-of-business, C#-heavy product it can be a clear win: one language, shared validation and models, and since .NET 8 a unified model where each component picks its render mode, including static server rendering with streaming, so you are not forced into an all-server or all-WebAssembly choice. The trade-offs I would state are that WebAssembly has a larger initial download and a different debugging story, that the JavaScript ecosystem is much larger if the product needs rich third-party components, and that Blazor Server keeps a persistent circuit per user, which is server state with real memory cost and a poor experience on unreliable networks — .NET 11 improves this with circuit pausing, which is worth knowing. For a consumer-facing product where front-end hiring and the component ecosystem matter, React remains the lower-risk choice.

How do you handle offline or flaky connectivity?

By deciding how much offline capability the product actually needs, because the answers differ by an order of magnitude in cost. The cheapest tier is graceful handling: detect the offline state, queue user intents in local storage with an idempotency key each, retry with backoff when connectivity returns, and tell the user clearly what has not been saved. The next tier is offline reads through a service worker with a cache-first strategy for the shell and stale-while-revalidate for data. The expensive tier is offline writes with genuine merge semantics, which is the same conflict problem as multi-master replication and usually needs either a last-write-wins policy per field or a conflict-free replicated data type. The mistake I would avoid is promising full offline support without deciding the conflict policy, because that decision is the actual work and it is a product question, not a technical one.

What is the React Compiler and does it change how you design a front end?

It is a build-time optimiser that reached version 1.0 in October 2025 and automatically memoises components and hook results, so the manual useMemo and useCallback discipline that dominated React performance work becomes largely unnecessary. It changes day-to-day code more than architecture: components can be written straightforwardly and still avoid unnecessary re-renders, provided the code follows the rules of React, which the compiler checks and which is why an existing codebase may need cleanup before enabling it. Architecturally it slightly weakens one old argument — that fine-grained state splitting was necessary to avoid re-render cascades — but it does nothing for the costs that actually matter at the system level, which are bundle size, the number of network round trips and hydration cost. So I would adopt it for the maintenance benefit and keep the architectural decisions where they were.

How do you make the front end resilient to a partially failing backend?

By designing per-section failure rather than per-page failure. Each region of the screen owns its own data dependency and its own loading, empty, error and stale states, so a failing recommendations service leaves a gap with a retry affordance instead of taking down the page. Requests carry timeouts on the client too, because a hanging fetch is indistinguishable from a slow one and the user needs an answer; and retries use backoff with jitter and an idempotency key so they are safe and do not amplify an incident. Where data is cached I prefer showing stale content with a clear indicator over showing a spinner, since stale usually beats nothing. And I would make sure error states are honest and actionable rather than a generic failure message, because in a partial outage the difference between "search is temporarily unavailable, showing recent documents" and "something went wrong" is the difference between a degraded product and a broken one.

13Security, identity and multi-tenancy

Security in a design round is not a checklist recital; it is a set of boundary decisions. The mental model: every request carries a claim about who it is and what it may do, and the design's job is to make that claim unforgeable, to evaluate it as close to the data as possible, and to ensure that a compromise of any one component does not grant the others. Multi-tenancy is the same problem with the stakes raised: a single missing predicate is a cross-customer data breach.

Identity: the modern shape

OAuth 2.0 is authorization, OpenID Connect is the authentication layer on top of it, and the distinction matters because using an access token as proof of identity is a classic mistake. The 2026 baseline for a web application:

  • Authorization Code with PKCE for every client type, including confidential ones. The implicit flow is dead; the resource-owner password flow is dead. OAuth 2.1 consolidates this.
  • Tokens stay server-side — the BFF pattern (units 04 and 12). The browser gets a cookie.
  • Short-lived access tokens (minutes) with refresh-token rotation and reuse detection, because revocation of a JWT is otherwise impossible before expiry.
  • Validate properly: signature against the published JWKS with key rotation, plus iss, aud, exp, nbf, and the algorithm — reject alg: none and never accept an algorithm the token itself chooses.
  • Service-to-service uses workload identity (managed identity, SPIFFE/SVID, or client credentials with a certificate), never a shared secret in configuration.

JWT versus reference tokens — a real trade-off

A JWT is self-contained: any service can validate it offline, which scales beautifully and is why it is the default. The cost is that it cannot be revoked before expiry, so a stolen token is valid until it expires and a fired employee keeps access for the token lifetime. A reference (opaque) token requires introspection against the issuer on each use, which gives instant revocation and a central audit point at the cost of a dependency on the hot path. The mature answer is a hybrid: short-lived JWTs so the revocation window is bounded to minutes, with a revocation list checked for high-value operations, and long-lived sessions held by the BFF where they can be killed centrally.

The threats to name, and the .NET-specific defences

  • Injection — parameterised queries always; EF Core and Dapper do this by default, and the risk is string-interpolated SQL, which FromSqlInterpolated handles correctly but FromSqlRaw with concatenation does not.
  • Broken access control — still the number-one category. The specific shape is IDOR: authorising the action but not the object. Every handler must check "may this principal act on this resource", which is resource-based authorization, not just a role check.
  • XSS — Razor and React escape by default; the risk is @Html.Raw, dangerouslySetInnerHTML and unvalidated URLs. Add a strict Content Security Policy with nonces.
  • SSRF — any feature that fetches a user-supplied URL (webhooks, imports, thumbnails from links) must use an allowlist and block link-local and private ranges, or it becomes a door into your cloud metadata endpoint.
  • Secrets — never in configuration files or environment variables checked into anything; managed identity where possible, a secret store otherwise, with rotation that is tested.
  • Supply chain — lockfiles, dependency scanning, signed builds and an SBOM; a compromised transitive npm package is a realistic path to your users' tokens, which is another argument for the BFF.

Interview trap

Authorising the endpoint instead of the object. [Authorize(Roles = "Editor")] on GET /documents/{id} says the caller is an editor; it says nothing about whether this document belongs to their tenant. That is insecure direct object reference, and it is still the most common serious flaw in real applications precisely because the endpoint-level attribute makes it feel handled. The design answer is resource-based authorization evaluated against the loaded entity (ASP.NET Core's IAuthorizationService.AuthorizeAsync(user, resource, requirement)), plus the tenant predicate enforced in the data layer so that the resource cannot even be loaded across a tenant boundary. Guessable sequential ids make it worse, which is one more argument for UUIDv7 keys.

Gotcha

Trusting a tenant identifier that came from the client. A header, a query parameter or a path segment naming the tenant is attacker-controlled input. The tenant must be derived from the authenticated principal — a claim in the validated token, or a lookup from the session — and where a subdomain or a route segment carries it, it must be checked against the claim rather than used as the source. The same applies to "impersonate tenant" features for support staff: they need a separate, audited, time-bounded permission, and every impersonated action must be logged with both identities, because the alternative is an unauditable path across every customer's data.

Try it

1. Break your own tenancy. In a test, seed two tenants, then try to read tenant B's row from tenant A's scope through four paths: a LINQ query, a query with IgnoreQueryFilters(), a Dapper query, and a direct FromSqlRaw. Expected outcome: only the first is blocked by the EF global filter. Then enable Postgres row-level security with the tenant set per connection and repeat — all four return zero rows. That experiment is the fastest way to understand why the query filter is ergonomics and RLS is the control.

2. Test token validation properly. Take a valid JWT and try four tampered variants against your API: modified payload with the original signature, alg changed to none, a token signed by a different issuer with a matching iss claim, and an expired token. Expected outcome: all four rejected with 401. If any is accepted, you have found a real vulnerability; the third is the one that most often slips through when the JWKS endpoint or the issuer is not pinned.

Solution sketch

For (1), the mechanics are that RLS applies to the database role, so the application must connect as a non-superuser role and set the tenant with SET LOCAL inside the transaction — which is also a reason to be careful with transaction-mode connection pooling, since a session-level SET would leak across tenants. For (2), in ASP.NET Core the validation parameters are explicit: ValidateIssuer, ValidateAudience, ValidateLifetime, ValidateIssuerSigningKey and a restricted ValidAlgorithms list; the default AddJwtBearer configuration with an authority handles key rotation via the JWKS document, but a hand-rolled validator frequently forgets the algorithm restriction, which is the hole that alg: none and key-confusion attacks walk through.

Interview Q&A

How do you enforce tenant isolation so it cannot be forgotten?

With defence in depth and at least one control that no code path can bypass. The ergonomic layer is a global query filter in the ORM so ordinary LINQ reads are automatically scoped, and a request-scoped tenant context populated from the authenticated principal rather than from anything the client sends. The control layer is row-level security in the database, with the application connecting as a non-privileged role and setting the tenant per transaction, because that also covers raw SQL, Dapper, reporting queries and anyone who calls IgnoreQueryFilters. The ratchet layer is tests: a standing test that a query executed in tenant A's scope returns zero of tenant B's rows, and an architecture test that fails the build on raw SQL without a tenant predicate. I would also add per-tenant metrics and alerting on cross-tenant query patterns, because the failure here is a breach rather than a bug.

JWT or reference tokens?

JWTs by default, because offline validation removes a dependency from every request and scales without an introspection hop, but with the revocation problem stated rather than ignored. A signed self-contained token is valid until it expires, so revoking access — a compromised token, a fired employee, a withdrawn consent — cannot take effect immediately. The mitigation is to keep access tokens very short-lived, on the order of minutes, and to use rotating refresh tokens with reuse detection so a stolen refresh token is detectable and the session can be killed centrally. For genuinely high-value operations I would additionally check a revocation list or re-authenticate. And if the environment is small and centralised, reference tokens with introspection are a perfectly good answer whose cost is one cached lookup, so I would not treat JWTs as automatically correct.

Where does authorization live in a distributed system?

The decision is made as close to the data as possible, and the policy is defined centrally. Doing it only at the gateway means any service reachable another way is unprotected and means the gateway must understand every resource's ownership rules, which does not scale. Doing it entirely per service means the policy drifts and nobody can answer who has access to what. The shape I would propose is that identity is established once at the edge and propagated as a validated token, each service enforces resource-level authorization against the object it has loaded, and policy is expressed in a shared, versioned form — an authorization service or a policy engine with locally cached decisions — so it can be audited and changed in one place. I would also insist that permission caching has a short lifetime and an explicit invalidation on revocation, because a cached allow is a stale security decision.

Design multi-tenancy for a product with 5,000 small customers and 5 very large ones.

A shared-schema model with a tenant column for the 5,000, and a directory that maps tenant to a physical location so the 5 large ones can be moved onto dedicated databases without changing application code. The shared model keeps per-tenant cost near zero, which is what makes a long tail of small customers viable, while the directory is the escape hatch that avoids a re-architecture when a customer becomes big, demands data residency, or signs a contract requiring isolation. Alongside that I would put per-tenant quotas and rate limits so a bulk import from one customer cannot consume shared capacity, and per-tenant metrics so the next whale is visible before it becomes an incident. The costs I would name are that migrations must now run across shared and dedicated deployments consistently, and that any cross-tenant reporting becomes a scatter-gather or a separate analytics store fed by change data capture.

What is IDOR and how do you prevent it structurally?

Insecure direct object reference is when a handler authorises the action but not the object, so an authenticated user can change an identifier in the URL and act on someone else's resource. Attribute-based checks on the endpoint make this easy to miss because the code looks protected. Preventing it structurally means two things. First, the data access layer cannot return an object outside the caller's scope at all, which is what tenant-scoped queries and row-level security give you, so the object is not merely unauthorised, it is invisible. Second, for finer-grained ownership within a tenant, resource-based authorization evaluated against the loaded entity, so the check is on the object rather than on the route. Non-sequential identifiers help by removing enumeration, but they are a mitigation and not a control, and I would say so rather than presenting them as the fix.

How do you handle secrets and credentials?

By preferring identities to secrets wherever the platform allows it — managed identity or workload identity federation means there is no long-lived credential to leak, rotate or accidentally commit, and that is a categorical improvement rather than an incremental one. Where a secret is unavoidable it lives in a secret store, is fetched at runtime rather than baked into an image or a configuration file, and has a rotation procedure that has actually been executed rather than documented. I would scan the repository history as well as the current tree, because a rotated-but-committed secret is still a finding, and I would add pre-commit and CI scanning so the next one is caught. The operational detail that gets forgotten is expiry: certificates and client secrets expire, and an unmonitored expiry is one of the most common causes of a complete, simultaneous, correlated outage.

A feature lets users supply a URL that we fetch. What are the risks?

Server-side request forgery, primarily. A URL fetched by my server runs from inside my network with my identity, so an attacker can point it at the cloud metadata endpoint to steal credentials, at internal services that assume network-level trust, or at localhost. The defences have to be layered because each individually is bypassable: resolve the hostname and validate the resulting IP against a blocklist of private, link-local and loopback ranges rather than validating the string, re-check after redirects since a redirect can move from a public address to a private one, disable or bound redirect following, and prefer an allowlist where the use case permits. Beyond SSRF there are also resource concerns — response size limits, timeouts and content-type checks — because a user-supplied URL can return a very large or very slow response. The strongest structural answer is to perform these fetches from an isolated egress-only worker with no network access to internal services and no ambient cloud identity.

How would you satisfy a data-deletion request across this architecture?

By having designed for it, because retrofitting is where this becomes very expensive. I would want a data inventory that says where personal data lives, which in a real system is more places than the primary database: search indexes, caches, message logs, analytics warehouses, backups, and third-party processors. Then a deletion process that fans out to each with a completion record, because a request that is only partially honoured is worse than none. The genuinely hard cases are immutable stores — an append-only event log or a backup — and the accepted approaches are crypto-shredding, where each subject's data is encrypted with a key that can be destroyed, or a documented backup retention window after which the data ages out, agreed with legal. I would also separate deletion from anonymisation, since aggregate analytics usually survive if identifiers are removed, and I would keep an audit record of the deletion itself, which is legitimate data to retain.

What does zero trust mean for service-to-service calls?

That being inside the network grants nothing: every call is authenticated and authorised on its own merits. Concretely that means mutual TLS or signed workload identity between services rather than relying on a private subnet, per-service identities rather than one shared credential, and authorisation policy about which service may call which endpoint, not merely which network can reach it. It also means the calling user's identity is propagated as a validated token rather than being dropped at the edge, so downstream services can make resource-level decisions instead of trusting the caller's assertion. A service mesh gives much of this for free, and so does the platform's workload identity if you are on one cloud. The reason this matters architecturally is blast radius: with network trust, one compromised pod reaches everything; with workload identity and per-call authorisation, it reaches only what its identity is permitted to reach.

How do you keep security work from being deferred forever?

By turning it into things that fail a build or a review rather than things on a backlog. Dependency and secret scanning in CI, an authorization test for every new endpoint as a review checklist item, and architecture tests for the invariants that matter such as tenant scoping — these are cheap and they hold. Threat modelling gets done once per significant feature as a thirty-minute conversation with a template rather than as a document nobody reads, and its output is a small number of concrete tasks in the same backlog as the feature, which is what stops it being deferred. For the parts that cannot be automated I would rely on periodic penetration testing and on making the findings visible to the same people who prioritise features. The leadership framing is that security debt behaves like any other debt except that the interest is paid all at once, in public, and that argument is more effective with an executive than any technical description of the risk.

14Observability, delivery and operations

A design is not finished when the boxes are drawn; it is finished when you can say how you would know it is working, how you would change it safely, and what happens at 3 a.m. This is the unit that most distinguishes lead candidates, because it is the part you can only learn by having been on call. The mental model: observability is the ability to answer questions you did not anticipate, using data you already collect. Monitoring answers known questions ("is CPU high?"); observability answers new ones ("why is this one tenant slow, only on Tuesdays, only on the export endpoint?").

The three signals, and the fourth

Metrics are cheap, aggregated, and good for alerting and trends; they cannot tell you about one request. Logs are expensive at volume and good for detail; structured, sampled, and correlated or they are useless. Traces show one request across services and are how you find where the time went. The fourth, often forgotten: events/profiles — deploys, config changes, feature-flag flips — because "what changed?" is the first question in every incident and the answer is usually a deploy.

OpenTelemetry is the answer to "how" in 2026 and is native in .NET: System.Diagnostics.Activity is the tracing API, Meter is the metrics API, ASP.NET Core and HttpClient and EF Core emit spans and metrics out of the box, and .NET 11 adds native OpenTelemetry tags on ASP.NET Core so requests are traced without extra instrumentation. Aspire wires the collector and a dashboard for local development. What to say: "instrument with OpenTelemetry, export to whatever backend the company has — the point is that the instrumentation is vendor-neutral so the backend is a reversible decision."

What to alert on: symptoms, not causes

Alert on what users experience, and use everything else for diagnosis. The two standard frames: RED for services (Rate, Errors, Duration) and USE for resources (Utilisation, Saturation, Errors). Concretely, page on: error rate above the SLO burn rate, p99 latency beyond budget, queue time-to-drain growing, and a "no data" condition — because a silent pipeline looks identical to a healthy one on a graph of zero errors.

SLOs and error budgets give this structure. An SLO of 99.9% availability over 30 days is 43 minutes of allowed failure; the error budget is that 43 minutes, and burning it fast is what should page someone, not any single failed request. The organisational value is that the budget converts an argument ("are we shipping too fast?") into a measurement: budget remaining means ship; budget exhausted means the next work is reliability. Multi-window burn-rate alerting — a fast window for acute incidents and a slow one for slow leaks — is the current standard and is worth naming.

Delivery: the design has to be deployable

  • Trunk-based development with feature flags, so deploying and releasing are separate events. This is what makes canaries, kill switches and progressive rollout possible, and it is the prerequisite for every safe migration in unit 07.
  • Expand/contract for schema, always, so code and schema deploys are independently reversible.
  • Progressive delivery: canary a small percentage with automatic comparison of error rate and latency against the baseline, then ramp. Blue/green is simpler but doubles resources and gives you a binary switch rather than a gradient.
  • Rollback must be the boring path. If rolling back requires a database restore, you do not have a rollback; you have a recovery procedure. This is the practical reason schema changes are always backward-compatible for at least one release.
  • Everything as code: infrastructure, dashboards, alerts, and runbooks in the repository, so a rebuild is a pipeline run rather than an archaeology project.

Incident command

At lead level you may be asked to run an incident, not just fix one. The structure: an incident commander who does not debug (they coordinate, decide and communicate), a scribe, and operators. Priorities in order: mitigate, then diagnose — roll back, fail over, shed load, or flip the flag first, and understand afterwards. Communicate on a fixed cadence even when there is nothing new, because silence generates its own escalations. Afterwards, a blameless postmortem that produces a small number of specific, owned, dated actions; a postmortem with twenty actions produces zero.

Interview trap

Cardinality: putting a unique id in a metric dimension. Metrics are cheap because they are pre-aggregated per unique combination of label values. Adding tenant_id, user_id, request_id or a raw URL path as a metric label multiplies the time series by that cardinality, and it is a genuinely common way to take down a monitoring system — or to receive a very large bill. The rule: low-cardinality dimensions on metrics (status code, endpoint template, region), high-cardinality context on spans and structured logs. Then exemplars link an aggregate metric to a specific trace, which gives you the drill-down without the explosion.

Gotcha

Alerting on causes rather than symptoms, and paging on things nobody acts on. An alert on "CPU > 80%" fires when nothing is wrong and stays silent when a dependency is down; it trains people to ignore pages. Every alert should map to a user-visible symptom and to a documented action; if the action is "look at it and go back to sleep", it should be a dashboard or a ticket, not a page. Alert fatigue is not a cultural failing, it is a design failure with a measurable cause — and in an interview, saying "I would delete most of the alerts and page only on SLO burn rate" is a stronger answer than adding more.

Try it

1. Trace an N+1 you cannot see in logs. Wire OpenTelemetry with the EF Core / Npgsql instrumentation into a local app (Aspire's dashboard is the fastest way), then hit an endpoint with a lazy-loaded collection. Expected outcome: the trace shows a staircase of dozens of near-identical database spans inside one request — instantly recognisable, and invisible in aggregate metrics, which would only show "the endpoint is a bit slow". Then fix it with a projection and compare the span counts.

2. Compute an error budget and a burn rate. Take any service and write down a 99.9% availability SLO over 30 days, convert it to minutes (43), then take last month's actual error rate and compute how much budget it consumed. Expected outcome: either you find you have been running well inside budget, in which case you are probably shipping too cautiously, or you find a single incident consumed it all, which reframes the reliability conversation from opinion to arithmetic. Then define a fast-burn alert (2% of budget in one hour) and a slow-burn one (10% in six hours).

Solution sketch

For (1), the general lesson is which signal answers which question: metrics told you it was slow, the trace told you why, and the log line gave you the parameters. A team missing any one of the three ends up guessing. For (2), the arithmetic is allowed_downtime = (1 − SLO) × window, so 99.9% over 30 days is 0.001 × 43,200 minutes ≈ 43 minutes; burn rate is the ratio of the observed error rate to the budgeted rate, so a 14.4x burn over one hour consumes 2% of a 30-day budget — those are the standard multi-window thresholds from the SRE workbook, and quoting them shows you have implemented this rather than read about it.

Interview Q&A

What would you put on the dashboard for this system?

One screen with the user-facing signals first: request rate, error rate and latency percentiles per endpoint group, then the saturation signals that predict them — in-flight requests, thread-pool queue length, database connection pool usage, and queue time-to-drain for each consumer. Then the dependency view: per-dependency latency and error rate, cache hit ratio, and replication lag, because those are the things that turn into user-facing symptoms. I would add deploy and feature-flag markers on the time axis, since the first question in any incident is what changed and the answer is usually visible as a step at a deploy line. Everything else belongs on drill-down dashboards rather than the front page, because a dashboard with sixty graphs is a dashboard nobody reads under pressure.

Metrics, logs or traces — when do you reach for each?

Metrics for "is something wrong and how bad", because they are cheap, pre-aggregated and suitable for alerting over time. Traces for "where is the time going", because a trace shows one request across every service and immediately reveals a slow dependency, an N+1 staircase, or a retry chain that metrics can only hint at. Logs for "what exactly happened to this request", with the identifiers and parameters that neither of the others carries. The thing that makes them useful together is correlation: the trace id must be present on every log line and linked from metrics via exemplars, so a spike on a graph is two clicks from the specific request that caused it. Without that correlation you have three separate tools and a lot of manual guessing.

What do you page on?

User-visible symptoms and imminent ones, and nothing else. Concretely that is error-budget burn rate at fast and slow windows, latency beyond the SLO, and saturation signals with a short runway such as a queue whose time-to-drain is growing or a disk that will fill within the hour. I would also page on absence of data, because a broken pipeline looks exactly like a perfectly healthy system on an error-rate graph. What I would deliberately not page on is resource utilisation by itself, individual failed requests, or anything whose documented response is to look and go back to sleep — those become dashboards or tickets. The reason to be strict is that every unnecessary page reduces the response to a necessary one, so pruning alerts is reliability work, not housekeeping.

Explain SLOs and error budgets to a product manager.

An SLO is a promise about how often the product may be broken for users, expressed as a number rather than an aspiration — say 99.9% of requests succeed within 500 milliseconds over a rolling 30 days. The error budget is the flip side: that 0.1% is about 43 minutes a month where we are allowed to fail, and it is a resource we choose how to spend. If we have budget left, we can ship faster and take more risk, because the users are getting the reliability we promised. If we have burned it, the next work is reliability until we are back inside the promise. That framing is what makes the conversation productive, because it replaces "engineering wants to do stability work and product wants features" with a shared number, and it also forces the honest question of whether 99.9% is what the business actually needs, since every extra nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more.

Walk me through how you would run a Sev-1.

Declare it early and appoint an incident commander who does not debug — their job is to coordinate, decide, and communicate, and separating that role from the hands-on work is the single biggest improvement most teams can make. Mitigate before diagnosing: roll back the last deploy, flip the feature flag, fail over, or shed load, because understanding is not required to stop the bleeding and time spent diagnosing is time customers are affected. Communicate on a fixed cadence, even when there is nothing new, since silence produces escalations and duplicated effort. Keep a running timeline as you go, because reconstructing it afterwards is unreliable. Then a blameless postmortem within a few days that produces a small number of specific, owned, dated actions — three real ones beat twenty aspirational ones — and follow up on whether they were actually done, since the failure mode of postmortems is that the actions quietly expire.

How do you deploy a breaking change safely?

By making it non-breaking in sequence. The schema goes first as an additive change, then code that writes both old and new shapes while reading the old, then a backfill, then reads flipped behind a flag with a percentage rollout, then — in a later release, once the new path has been stable — the old column and the dual-write are removed. Each step is independently deployable and reversible, which means at no point does rollback require a data restore. For the API surface the same logic applies: add the new field or endpoint, migrate clients, instrument usage per client, then remove. The detail that makes it real rather than theoretical is that the intermediate states must be tested — a test suite that runs the old code against the new schema, since that is exactly the combination that exists during a rolling deploy.

What is high-cardinality data and why does it matter?

Cardinality is the number of distinct values a dimension can take, and it matters because a metric system stores one time series per unique combination of label values. Adding a tenant id with five thousand values to a metric that already has ten endpoints and five status codes turns fifty series into a quarter of a million, which is how teams take down their monitoring system or receive a startling bill. The discipline is to keep metric dimensions low cardinality — status class, endpoint template, region, result — and put the high-cardinality context on spans and structured logs, which are stored differently and are meant for it. Exemplars then link the aggregate metric to a specific trace, so you keep the drill-down without paying for the explosion. It is worth adding that URL paths with ids in them are the most common accidental source, which is why instrumentation should use the route template rather than the raw path.

How do you debug a problem that only affects one customer?

By having built for it, which means tenant id on every span and every structured log line even though it is not a metric dimension. With that in place I can filter traces to that tenant and compare their latency distribution against the fleet, which usually shows the answer immediately: a much larger data volume hitting a query whose plan degrades at scale, a hot partition, a feature flag enabled only for them, or a rate limit they are hitting. If it is intermittent I would look at exemplars from the slow tail rather than at averages, since a customer-specific problem is invisible in aggregate. The structural point I would make is that per-tenant observability is a design decision made at instrumentation time, and a system that cannot answer "what is happening for this customer" will handle every enterprise escalation badly.

How much logging is too much?

When the cost or the noise stops it being read, which happens sooner than people expect — at high request rates, logging every request at information level can cost more than the compute serving them. My approach is structured logs with levels used meaningfully, a sampling strategy that keeps everything for errors and a small percentage of successes, and trace-based sampling so that when a request is sampled, its whole trace and its logs are kept together rather than fragments of unrelated requests. Debug-level logging should be enableable at runtime for a specific scope — a tenant, an endpoint, a short window — rather than being either always on or requiring a deploy. And logs should never carry secrets or personal data beyond what is necessary, because a log store is usually the least protected copy of your data and it is retained for a long time.

What operational readiness would you require before this goes live?

A short, specific list rather than a ceremony. Dashboards and alerts that exist, are tested by inducing the condition, and page a rota that is actually staffed. Runbooks for the top three or four failure modes, written to be followed by someone who did not build the system. A rehearsed rollback and a rehearsed restore, because a backup that has never been restored is a hope. Capacity numbers with a load test behind them, including the cold-cache case. Security basics: secrets in a store, dependency scanning, and an authorization test per endpoint. And a documented degradation table saying what happens when each dependency is unavailable, agreed with product. If any of those is missing I would rather delay the launch or launch to a limited set of customers, and being willing to say that clearly is part of the job.

15The big Q&A

Everything that matters but is not a core building block: architecture and ecosystem decisions, migration reality, performance debugging, cost, tooling, and the judgment and scenario questions that dominate lead and principal loops. Twenty-two questions, grouped.

Architecture & ecosystem decisions

How do you decide between building, buying and using a managed service?

I start from whether the thing is differentiating. If it is what customers pay us for, we build it; if it is undifferentiated infrastructure — a queue, a search index, an identity provider, a database — the default is managed, because the total cost is not the licence fee, it is the engineer-years of operating, patching, upgrading and being paged for it. Then I check the exit: can we leave if the price changes or the vendor is acquired, and what would that cost? That usually argues for using the standard interface rather than the proprietary extension, so the decision stays reversible. The cases where I would build are when the managed option genuinely cannot meet a hard requirement, when the scale makes the pricing absurd, and occasionally when the operational knowledge itself is strategic. What I try to avoid is the reflex to build because it looks interesting, which is how teams end up maintaining a bespoke workflow engine instead of shipping product.

When would you introduce event sourcing?

Rarely, and only where the history is genuinely the domain — a ledger, a trading book, an audit-critical workflow, anything where "how did we get to this state" is a business question rather than a debugging one. The benefits are real: a perfect audit trail, the ability to build new projections retroactively, and temporal queries that a current-state model cannot answer. The costs are also real and permanent: eventual consistency between the log and every read model, schema evolution on events that must remain replayable forever, snapshotting to keep rebuilds tractable, and a much steeper learning curve for everyone who joins. I would also separate it clearly from CQRS, which people conflate — separating read and write models is often useful on its own and does not require event sourcing. My usual recommendation is to use an outbox and domain events for integration, keep a current-state model as the source of truth, and reserve event sourcing for the one bounded context that truly needs it.

Modular monolith or microservices — how do you argue it to a team that wants microservices?

Not by refusing, but by making the cost concrete and the path available. I would agree the destination in principle and then ask what we are buying: independent deploys, independent scaling, fault isolation, and team autonomy are real benefits, and I would ask which of them we need in the next year. Then I would lay out what we would pay from day one — distributed tracing to debug anything, sagas where a transaction used to work, a service mesh or its equivalent, a deployment pipeline per service, and an on-call surface that is now several times larger with the same headcount. The compromise that usually satisfies both is a modular monolith with genuinely enforced boundaries — separate schemas, module interfaces, and a build that fails on cross-module references — plus a written trigger list for extraction. That gives the team the design discipline they actually want, keeps the option open, and defers the operational cost until there is something to buy with it.

How do you choose between Azure, AWS and staying portable?

I optimise for the team's existing depth first, because a platform your engineers know is worth more than a marginally better feature matrix, and for a .NET shop that is frequently Azure — although .NET runs equally well everywhere and that has not been a real differentiator for years. On portability, I would distinguish two things: portability of the workload, which containers and Kubernetes give you cheaply and which is worth having, and portability of the managed services, which is expensive and usually not worth it. My rule is to use managed services enthusiastically but through their standard interfaces where one exists — Postgres rather than a proprietary dialect, Kafka or AMQP rather than a bespoke API, OpenTelemetry rather than a vendor agent — so that a move is a project rather than a rewrite. And I would state plainly that multi-cloud as an availability strategy is usually a mistake, because it forces you to the lowest common denominator and the added complexity causes more outages than the cloud outages it protects against.

Where does .NET Aspire fit in a real architecture?

It is mainly a developer-experience and composition layer rather than a runtime dependency, and I would present it that way. An app host declares the services, the databases, the cache and the queue, wires connection strings and service discovery, and gives every developer a one-command local environment with an OpenTelemetry dashboard — which addresses a genuine architectural risk, because a system that cannot be run locally drifts and only the person who set up the environment can debug it. Since version 13 it dropped the ".NET" prefix and treats JavaScript and Python as first-class, which matters for a full-stack product where the front end is part of the composition, and 13.3 made a TypeScript app host generally available. On the deployment side it can generate manifests for Kubernetes or Azure Container Apps, and how far to lean on that depends on whether your platform team already has an opinionated pipeline. What it does not do is change the architecture, so I would not present it as one.

Legacy, migration and evolution

How would you migrate a .NET Framework monolith to something modern?

Incrementally, behind a facade, driven by business value rather than by a desire to be modern. I would put a reverse proxy — YARP is the natural choice in a .NET shop — in front of the existing application so that routes can be moved one at a time with no change visible to clients, which is the strangler pattern and it is the only approach I have seen work at scale. Then I would pick the first slice by value and risk: something with real pain, a clear boundary and manageable data coupling, not the hardest or the most sentimental part. The hard problems are never the C#; they are shared session state, the data-protection and authentication story spanning both worlds, and a database that both sides write to during the transition. I would insist on a definition of done that includes deleting the old code path, because the failure mode of these programmes is running two systems forever, and I would keep a visible metric of what fraction of traffic is served by the new stack so progress is arguable with numbers.

You inherit a system with no tests and no documentation. What do you do first?

Get visibility before making changes, because changing a system you cannot observe is how you find out what it did. So the first work is instrumentation and a few characterisation tests around the paths that matter — not comprehensive coverage, but enough to notice a regression in the behaviour customers depend on. In parallel I would build the map: which endpoints get traffic, which tables are actually read, which jobs run, which dependencies exist, and I would derive that from telemetry rather than from what people believe, because the two differ. Then I would find the riskiest operational gap — usually backups that have never been restored, or a deploy that cannot be rolled back — and fix that, since it is the failure that would hurt most. Only after that would I start refactoring, and I would do it along the lines of features being requested so the work is funded by delivery rather than presented as a separate rewrite that will be cancelled in month three.

How do you migrate a live database from SQL Server to PostgreSQL?

First I would challenge whether it is worth doing, because the benefit is usually licensing and the cost is a quarter of engineering plus a long tail of subtle behavioural differences — collation and case sensitivity, identity and sequence semantics, date handling, and every stored procedure. If it is going ahead, the shape is dual-running rather than a cutover weekend: replicate changes continuously into Postgres with change data capture, run the application in shadow mode against both and compare results, then move reads over per feature behind a flag, then writes, with the ability to reverse each step. The application-layer work is mostly making the data access engine-agnostic first, which for EF Core is largely achievable and for raw SQL and stored procedures is the real project. I would also plan for the operational learning explicitly — vacuum, bloat, connection pooling and plan behaviour are different enough that the team needs runway before it owns production, and pretending otherwise is how these migrations produce their first outage a week after cutover.

A predecessor built a distributed monolith. How do you unwind it?

I would first measure the coupling honestly: how many deployments require coordination, how many services are touched by a typical change, and where synchronous call chains are more than two deep. That gives an ordered list rather than an opinion. Then the counter-intuitive move is usually to merge services back together where the boundary was wrong, because two services that always change together are one service paying a network tax, and consolidating is much cheaper than trying to decouple them. For the rest, the work is removing synchronous coupling — replacing a call chain with an event and a local read model, giving each service exclusive ownership of its tables, and eliminating shared libraries that force lock-step upgrades. I would sequence it so that each step reduces coordination measurably, and I would report progress with the deployment-coordination metric rather than with an architecture diagram, because that is what the business actually feels.

Performance, debugging and cost

Production is slow and you have five minutes. What do you look at?

What changed, first: deploys, config changes and feature-flag flips in the last hour, because the overwhelming majority of incidents are self-inflicted and the fastest mitigation is a rollback. Then the shape of the slowness — is it everything or one endpoint, everyone or one tenant, all instances or a subset — because each answer eliminates a different half of the possibility space. Then saturation signals in order: in-flight requests and thread-pool queue length, database connection pool waits, and dependency latency broken out per dependency, since one slow dependency filling a shared pool is the single most common mechanism. Cache hit ratio and replication lag are the next two, because a cold cache or a lagging replica produces exactly this picture. Throughout I would be biased toward mitigation over diagnosis: if a rollback or a flag flip restores service, take it and investigate afterwards.

Memory keeps climbing in a .NET service until it is OOMKilled. How do you diagnose it?

I would first separate a leak from configuration, because in a container the most common cause is not a leak at all: Server GC sizes heaps from the cgroup limit and will happily grow toward it, so a container that is killed at its limit may simply need a heap hard limit or more headroom for non-GC memory. If it is a genuine leak, I capture a dump — dotnet-gcdump or dotnet-dump on the running pod — and compare two snapshots taken some time apart to see which types are growing. The usual culprits in a web service are static collections and caches without eviction, event handlers or timers holding references, HttpClient instances created per request, and captured contexts in long-lived delegates. I would also check the large object heap, since large buffers and big strings fragment it and produce growth that looks like a leak. The structural fix is almost always a bounded cache with an eviction policy, plus a memory metric with an alert well below the kill threshold so this is caught before an outage.

How do you load-test a system properly?

With a realistic shape rather than a flat maximum. That means a workload mix that matches production ratios, realistic data volumes and cardinality — a database with a thousand rows behaves nothing like one with a hundred million — and think time, because back-to-back requests from a single connection produce a pattern no real user generates. I run a ramp to find the knee rather than a single number, since the useful output is the arrival rate at which latency percentiles break, and I measure goodput within the SLO rather than raw throughput. Two scenarios matter beyond the steady state: the cold-cache case, because that is what recovery looks like, and the failure case with a dependency degraded, because that is where cascades appear. Finally I would run it against an environment whose topology matches production in the ways that matter — same instance classes, same connection limits, same network hops — and be explicit about where it does not, since a load test against a smaller database tells you about the load generator, not the system.

The cloud bill doubled. How do you find out why and what do you cut?

I would break the bill down by service and by tag first, then by change over time, because a doubling is usually one or two line items rather than a general drift, and the common culprits are data egress, over-provisioned always-on compute, log and telemetry ingestion, and storage that nobody has a lifecycle policy for. Telemetry is the one that surprises teams most often — verbose logging at high request rates can cost more than the compute producing it, and the fix is sampling rather than switching it off. Then I would look at architectural causes rather than knobs: a chatty cross-zone or cross-region path generating egress, a cache with a poor hit ratio pushing load onto expensive database IOPS, or autoscaling that never scales down. What I would resist is cutting reliability capacity to save money without stating the trade explicitly, and I would attach a cost-per-tenant or cost-per-request metric to the dashboard so this becomes a continuously visible number rather than a quarterly panic.

What are the most common performance mistakes you see in code review?

Four dominate. N+1 queries, usually hidden behind lazy loading or an innocent-looking loop with an await in it, which is invisible in tests with small data and catastrophic with real data. Loading entities to compute something a database aggregate could do, and materialising far more columns and rows than the screen needs. Synchronous blocking on async work — anything with .Result or .Wait() on a request path — which does not fail in a unit test but starves the thread pool under load. And missing cancellation propagation, so a client that gives up leaves the server doing work nobody will read. Below those, I look for unbounded collections, caches without eviction, and any remote call without a timeout. What I try to teach rather than just correct is the habit of asking "what does this do at a thousand times the test data" before merging, since all four mistakes are invisible at small scale.

Security, compliance and data

How do you design for data residency across regions?

By making the tenant the unit of residency and putting the routing decision in a directory rather than in code. Each tenant is pinned to a home region, a global router sends their requests there, and the data for that tenant never leaves it — which means the design must not have a single global database that everything writes to, so shared reference data is replicated read-only and identity is either regional or carefully scoped. The parts that catch people out are the secondary copies: backups, logs, telemetry, search indexes and analytics warehouses all contain customer data and all need the same constraint, and telemetry pipelines in particular default to a single global sink. I would also plan for the operational reality that regions diverge — a deploy now rolls out to several places, and a schema migration must be idempotent and resumable per region. And I would push back on treating residency as an all-or-nothing property, since usually only a defined class of personal data is regulated and pretending otherwise makes the architecture much more expensive than the requirement demands.

How would you add an audit trail that auditors will accept?

It has to be append-only, complete for the actions in scope, tamper-evident, and queryable, and each of those is a design decision. Append-only means writes go to a store with no update or delete path for the application role, and retention is enforced by policy rather than by convention. Complete means the audit record is written in the same transaction as the change — an outbox row or a trigger — because an audit log written on a best-effort basis after the fact will have gaps exactly when it matters. Tamper-evident usually means hash chaining each entry to its predecessor so any modification is detectable, and signing periodically if the requirement is strict. Queryable means it needs indexes for the questions auditors ask — by actor, by subject, by time range — and a retention policy that matches the regulation rather than being infinite. I would also be careful about what goes in it: enough to reconstruct who did what to which record and when, without turning the audit store into a second, less protected copy of the sensitive data.

A dependency has a critical CVE. Walk me through your response.

First, determine exposure rather than presence: is the vulnerable code path actually reachable in our usage, is the affected component internet-facing, and what would an exploit achieve — because that determines whether this is a patch-tonight or a patch-this-sprint. In parallel I would check for indicators of compromise, since a publicly disclosed critical vulnerability in an exposed component means someone has probably already tried it. Then mitigate before patching if a mitigation exists — a WAF rule, disabling the feature, or a configuration change — because it is often available in minutes where a patched build is hours. Patching itself should be routine rather than heroic, which is the real answer: dependency scanning in CI, a lockfile, and a pipeline capable of shipping a patched build the same day are what turn a CVE into an afternoon rather than a crisis. Afterwards I would look at whether our dependency surface is bigger than it needs to be, because the most reliable way to reduce this work is to have fewer dependencies.

Judgment, leadership and scenarios

How do you introduce a significant architectural change to a skeptical team?

By starting from their pain rather than my preference. If I cannot express the change as a solution to something the team already complains about — a slow deploy, a fragile area, a recurring page — then either I have not understood the problem or the change is not worth making. Then I would make it small and demonstrable: a spike on a real slice of the system with a measurement attached, presented as a proposal rather than a decision, and I would genuinely invite the case against it, because the objections are usually where the implementation risk lives. Writing it down as an architecture decision record with the alternatives and the trade-offs makes the reasoning inspectable and separates the idea from my authority, which matters if I want honest disagreement. And I would find a respected engineer on the team to co-own it, since adoption is a social process and a change nobody but the lead believes in will be reverted quietly within a year.

What do you look for when reviewing a design document?

I read for the parts that are missing rather than the parts that are present. Are the non-functional requirements stated as numbers, and do the design choices follow from them? Are the alternatives listed with honest reasons for rejection, or is there one option presented as inevitable? What happens when each dependency fails, and is that degradation agreed with product? How is it migrated in and, more importantly, rolled back? How will we know it works — what is on the dashboard and what pages someone? And what is the reversal condition, meaning what would we learn that would make us change this decision. I also look for scope discipline: a document that solves a problem three times larger than the one we have is a risk, and asking "what is the smallest version of this that delivers the value" often improves it more than any technical comment. Style, naming and framework preferences I mostly leave alone, because a review that mixes those with the substantive questions dilutes both.

The business wants a feature that the architecture makes expensive. How do you handle it?

I separate the request from the implementation and get to the underlying need, because the expensive part is frequently an incidental detail of how the feature was specified rather than the value being asked for. Then I present options with costs rather than a refusal: a cheap version that delivers most of the value in two weeks, a full version in two months, and what each does to the system afterwards, including the maintenance it creates. That framing puts the decision where it belongs, with the person accountable for the trade, and it is far more effective than an engineering veto. If the cheap version incurs debt I would name the debt explicitly, agree when it gets paid, and write it down, because undocumented shortcuts become permanent. And occasionally the honest answer is that the architecture is wrong for where the product is going, in which case the feature request is useful evidence and I would use it to make the case for the structural work, with this cost as the first data point.

How do you decide what to standardise across teams and what to leave to them?

I standardise the things where divergence creates cross-team cost, and leave the rest alone. Interfaces and contracts, observability conventions, authentication and authorization, deployment and rollback mechanics, and the data classification rules are worth enforcing, because incompatibility there means every team pays a translation cost and incidents become unanswerable. Internal implementation choices, library preferences and code organisation I would leave to teams, because the cost of divergence is local and the cost of enforcement is high and continuous. The mechanism matters as much as the choice: standards that are paved roads — a template, a shared library, a pipeline that does the right thing by default — are adopted willingly, while standards that are documents get ignored. And I would keep an explicit exception process, because a standard with no way out becomes a reason to route around the platform team entirely.

How has generative AI changed how you design and review systems?

Two ways, and I would separate them carefully. As a tool it has compressed the cost of producing code, which shifts the bottleneck further toward design, review and verification — so I weight architecture reviews, contract tests and observability more heavily than I did, because more code arriving faster makes weak boundaries and thin test coverage more dangerous, not less. As a component in the system, an LLM is an unusually awkward dependency: it is slow by the standards of everything else in this guide, non-deterministic, priced per token, and can fail in ways that look like success, so it needs strict timeouts, aggressive caching of embeddings and results, a fallback path, and evaluation that measures output quality rather than only latency and errors. I would also treat any model output that reaches a privileged action as untrusted input, with the same posture as user input, since prompt injection through retrieved content is a real and current attack. What has not changed is everything in the preceding units — the AI features still sit on top of a system whose correctness comes from the boring parts.

16Night-before cheat sheet

Ten sentences to say out loud until they are yours. Each one is a position you can defend and expand for two minutes if pushed.

  1. "Let me do the arithmetic before I design." Ten million registered users is usually under a few hundred requests per second; a day is roughly 100,000 seconds; Little's law says concurrency equals arrival rate times latency; and queueing delay grows as 1/(1−ρ), which is why we target 60–70% utilisation rather than 90%.
  2. "The default is one deployable, one relational database, and boring technology." A modular monolith on .NET 10 LTS with PostgreSQL 18 or SQL Server 2025 serves most products for years; distribution is a cost you pay to buy independent deploys, independent scaling or fault isolation, and if you are not buying one of those, you are just paying.
  3. "Every unsafe operation carries an idempotency key, because a timeout tells you nothing." A timeout means the work may have happened, may not have, or may still be running, so retries are only safe when the operation is idempotent — and retries are the only recovery mechanism a distributed system has.
  4. "There is no transaction across two systems, so I use an outbox and reconciliation." Write the event in the same transaction as the state change, relay it afterwards, accept at-least-once delivery, and make consumers idempotent by committing a dedup record with the effect. Exactly-once delivery does not exist; effectively-once processing does.
  5. "Isolation levels are a menu of anomalies." Read committed permits non-repeatable reads and phantoms; snapshot isolation still permits write skew; serializable removes it, optimistically in Postgres with 40001 retries and pessimistically in SQL Server with range locks — and long transactions are what bloat a Postgres table.
  6. "Scale in order: fix queries, go vertical, cache, replicate, partition, then shard." Sharding is a permanent tax on every query and every migration, so it needs a forcing function; if we do it, it is virtual buckets or a tenant directory, never hash mod N.
  7. "A cache is a derived copy with a staleness budget, and I know what happens when it is empty." Invalidate by deleting after the commit, jitter every TTL, use single-flight to prevent stampedes — HybridCache gives you that natively since .NET 9 — and size the origin for the cold-cache case or shed load while it warms.
  8. "Bound failures in time and breadth: timeouts, bulkheads, breakers, and shed the rest." Timeouts come from the dependency's measured p99 and shrink down the chain; retries are jittered, budgeted and applied at one layer only; serving 70% of requests well beats serving 100% past the deadline.
  9. "The browser is part of the capacity plan and part of the security boundary." Requests per screen times concurrent users is real backend load, a client cache with a stale time is free capacity, and access tokens belong server-side behind a BFF with an HTTP-only cookie — never in localStorage.
  10. "I would know it was working, and I could change it safely." OpenTelemetry everywhere with low-cardinality metrics and high-cardinality context on spans; page on error-budget burn rate rather than on CPU; expand-and-contract migrations behind feature flags so rollback is a redeploy, never a restore.
