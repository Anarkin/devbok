01The p99 nobody could reproduce

tier · senior performance problem diagnosed

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The complaint was the kind you dread because it is unfalsifiable: "the dashboard is slow sometimes". Our own numbers said it was fine — p50 was 120 ms, and the synthetic check from the same region was green all week. What made it real was switching the SLO dashboard from averages to percentiles per route: the account overview was 140 ms at p50 and 4.2 s at p99, and the p99 line was thick between 09:00 and 10:00 every weekday. That shape — fine at the median, terrible at the tail, correlated with a time of day rather than a deploy — is almost always queueing behind a limited resource, not slow code.

I started at the top, because "the dashboard" is a full-stack noun. In the React 19.2 front end the overview page issued six fetch calls, and three of them were serialised because each waited on an id from the previous response — a classic request waterfall that turns a 120 ms API into a 700 ms page even when the server is healthy. That explained the baseline sluggishness but not the four-second tail, so I followed one slow trace end to end in the OpenTelemetry data. The ASP.NET Core span was 4.1 s, its child EF Core spans totalled 300 ms, and the 3.8 s gap sat before the first database command. Time inside a request that belongs to no span is nearly always waiting for a pool.

It was the Npgsql connection pool. The overview endpoint loaded an account, then its subscriptions, then per subscription the last invoice — a lazy-loading N+1 that fired 60–90 commands per request. Each command is cheap; holding a pooled connection for the whole 90-command sequence is not. With Maximum Pool Size=100 (the Npgsql default) and 40 concurrent overview requests in the morning peak, arrivals exceeded the pool and requests queued for a connection until the connection timeout was nearly hit. Little's law told the story better than any argument: at roughly 35 requests/second and 250 ms of connection hold time we needed about nine connections; the N+1 pushed hold time past 2.5 s, which needs about 88 — right at the edge, so the pool tipped over only when the morning burst arrived.

The fix was three changes, in order of how much they bought. First, kill the N+1: one projection with an explicit join, AsNoTracking because the endpoint is read-only, and TagWith so the query is findable in pg_stat_statements. That took the endpoint from 60–90 commands to two and dropped hold time to about 40 ms. Second, add the covering index the new query wanted — invoices (subscription_id, issued_at desc) — which turned a 30 ms sort into a 2 ms index scan. Third, on the front end, fetch the three independent resources in parallel and let the dependent one stream in, so the page stopped waiting on its own waterfall. p99 went from 4.2 s to 210 ms and stayed there through the next quarter's growth.

// before: lazy loading, one command per subscription, one per invoice
var account = await db.Accounts.FirstAsync(a => a.Id == id, ct);
foreach (var sub in account.Subscriptions)              // N+1 fires here
    rows.Add(new Row(sub.Name, sub.Invoices.Max(i => i.IssuedAt)));

// after: one projection, no tracking, tagged so it is findable in pg_stat_statements
var rows = await db.Subscriptions
    .Where(s => s.AccountId == id)
    .Select(s => new Row(s.Name, s.Invoices.Max(i => (DateTime?)i.IssuedAt)))
    .AsNoTracking()
    .TagWith("account-overview")
    .ToListAsync(ct);

The trade-off worth naming out loud is what you do when the projection needs several collections at once, because that is where people reach for the wrong lever:

ShapeRound tripsRiskUse when
Single query, joined collections1Cartesian explosion: two 50-row collections return 2,500 rowsOne collection, or small ones
AsSplitQuery()1 per collectionNo single snapshot unless you raise the isolation levelSeveral collections; my default on read models
Lazy loading1 per entity touchedN+1 that only appears at real data volumesAlmost never in a request path

What I would do differently: I would have found this in week one if the connection-pool metric had been on the dashboard from the start. Npgsql emits connection-pool metrics and ASP.NET Core has emitted OpenTelemetry-shaped built-in metrics since .NET 8, so pool saturation is a chart, not a detective story. I now treat "pool in use vs. pool max" as a golden signal alongside latency, traffic, errors and saturation, and I put p99 on the first dashboard anyone sees, because a median hides exactly the customers who complain.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you debug systems by measurement or by folklore. The signal they want: you formed a hypothesis from the shape of the latency (tail-only, time-correlated), you followed one trace instead of guessing, and you can explain the queueing model that turned a 40 ms query into a 4 s request. They also want the boring second half — the index, and the metric you added so it cannot come back silently.

The mistake candidates make here is jumping straight to a fix ("we added caching") with no diagnosis, or narrating tool names instead of findings. The other one, specific to this archetype: quoting an average. Say "p99", say the number before and after, and say how long the fix took to verify.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

When did something you built behave differently under real load than in dev?

Hint: dev has one user and a warm cache. Look for the first time you saw queueing — a pool, a semaphore, a lock, a rate limit, a single-threaded consumer. What was the resource, and how did you prove it rather than assume it?

What is the worst tail-latency number you have personally measured, and what caused it?

Hint: dig up an actual figure — a p99, a slow-query log line, a timeout count. If the number is gone, remember the shape: constant, spiky, time-of-day, tenant-specific, or post-deploy? Each shape points at a different class of cause, and saying so is itself a strong answer.

Where in your stack does a request currently wait on something you cannot see?

Hint: unspanned time. Connection pools, HttpClient handler pools, thread-pool queueing, consumer lag, a lock inside a cache client. Naming a gap you still have reads as maturity — provided you also say what you would add to close it.

Have you ever fixed a front-end waterfall, and what did it buy?

Hint: sequential fetches, a client waiting on an id before the next call, a chatty page that needed a BFF or one aggregate endpoint. Quantify with a user-facing metric (LCP or INP, time to interactive) rather than a server metric — this is where full-stack candidates separate themselves from backend-only ones.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just raise the connection pool size to 500?

Because the pool is not the bottleneck, it is the queue in front of one — raising it moves the contention into the database, where it is far more expensive. Postgres allocates a backend process per connection, so a few hundred active connections cost memory and context switching and can push the instance into a spiral; the usual answer at that point is a pooler such as PgBouncer in transaction mode, not a bigger client pool. The real fix is to shorten hold time, because pool demand is arrival rate multiplied by hold time. I would raise the limit only as a deliberate, time-boxed mitigation while the real fix ships, and I would raise the database's own limits and alerts in the same change.

At what scale does your fix stop working?

Roughly where a single primary stops absorbing the read volume. Two commands at 40 ms of hold time supports a few thousand requests per second on one instance; past that I would move the overview to a read replica and accept replication lag for everything except the tenant's own just-written data, then to a materialised read model updated from change events if the join cost keeps growing. The next wall after that is cardinality: a tenant with 50,000 subscriptions cannot be rendered as one page at all, so the design has to become paged or pre-aggregated. I would rather cross those bridges deliberately than pre-build them.

What did you trade away?

Two things. The projection duplicates shape knowledge that used to live in the entity model, so a schema change now touches the read query too — I mitigated that with a query test running against a real Postgres in Testcontainers rather than an in-memory provider, which would not have caught the SQL at all. And AsNoTracking means the results are outside the change tracker, exactly right for a read endpoint but a trap if someone later mutates them; I made the read model a separate record type so it is impossible to save it back by accident.

How did you know the front-end change mattered rather than just the backend one?

I shipped them separately and watched a user-centric metric, not the server one. The backend fix moved p99 API latency; the waterfall fix moved field LCP in real-user monitoring, because three parallel fetches removed two round trips of dependency. Shipping them together would have made attribution impossible, and the cost of separating them was one extra deploy. One variable per deploy when you are measuring is what makes performance work compounding rather than superstitious.

Would a load test have caught this?

Only with a realistic data distribution, which is the honest lesson. Our load test used a seeded tenant with three subscriptions, so the N+1 fired three times and looked fine; the production tenant that hurt had ninety. Load tests find capacity limits, but they only find data-shape bugs when the seed mirrors real cardinality — including the outlier tenant. I now seed load tests from an anonymised copy of the real distribution and test the p99 tenant, not the average one.

What would you do differently now, on .NET 10?

I would start from the built-in telemetry instead of adding my own: ASP.NET Core, HttpClient and EF Core all emit OpenTelemetry-shaped metrics natively, and the Aspire dashboard gives you traces locally without standing up a collector, so the unspanned 3.8 seconds would have been visible on a laptop. I would also lean on EF Core 10's query improvements rather than hand-rolling caches. The diagnostic sequence would not change at all — percentiles, one trace, find the queue — only how fast I could run it.

02Modular monolith over microservices

tier · principal hard architectural trade-off

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were twelve engineers on one ASP.NET Core product, and the proposal on the table was to split it into nine services because deploys had become frightening. The frightening part was real: one solution, one database, 22 minutes of CI, and a release train that stopped whenever anyone's change broke someone else's test. What was not real was the causal story. The pain was coupling and test flakiness, and microservices do not remove coupling, they convert it from a compile error into a 500 at 2 a.m. Twelve people cannot operate nine deployable services and still ship features; the coordination cost per service does not shrink with team size, it grows.

So I argued for a modular monolith with genuinely enforced seams, and for extracting exactly one service — the one with a different scaling and failure profile. We split the codebase into modules (Billing, Catalogue, Identity, Notifications), each with its own folder, its own schema in the same Postgres database, and a public contract of a handful of interfaces and message types. Everything else was internal. Crucially, the boundary was enforced mechanically rather than socially: an architecture test failed the build if a type outside a module's public contract was referenced across the boundary, and a migration test failed if a module's tables were touched by another module's DbContext. Rules a linter enforces survive a busy quarter; rules in a wiki page do not.

The one extraction was the document renderer: CPU-bound, spiky, needing GPU-less but large instances, and able to fail without taking checkout down. Different scaling profile plus tolerable failure semantics is the actual test for "should this be its own service" — not "is it a different noun in our domain model". We ran it behind a queue with an idempotent consumer, so the monolith enqueues and never blocks on it.

[Fact]
public void Modules_only_talk_through_their_public_contracts()
{
    var result = Types.InAssembly(typeof(BillingModule).Assembly)
        .That().ResideInNamespace("Product.Modules.Billing.Internal")
        .Should().NotBeCalledFromOutside("Product.Modules.Billing")

The result after two quarters: CI down to 7 minutes (mostly by parallelising module test projects and moving to Testcontainers per module), deploys from weekly to roughly 30 a week behind feature flags, and one service running independently. Eighteen months later two more modules were extracted — and because the seams were already enforced, each extraction was a week, not a quarter. That was the real payoff: the modular monolith is not the opposite of microservices, it is the cheapest way to learn where the service boundaries actually are before you pay for them.

What I would do differently: I under-invested in the messaging seam early. Modules called each other through in-process interfaces, which made the eventual extraction of Notifications harder than it needed to be because callers assumed synchronous success. If a module is a candidate for extraction, make its inbound contract asynchronous from day one — an in-process message bus with the same delivery semantics you would get over the wire, including "may be delivered twice".

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can make an expensive architectural decision on evidence rather than fashion, and whether you can say no to the exciting option. They want to hear the specific criteria you used (deploy independence, scaling profile, failure isolation, team topology), the cost you priced in, and how you made the decision reversible. At principal level they also listen for how you brought a team that wanted microservices along with you.

The mistakes: arguing from taste ("microservices are overkill") with no numbers; presenting the decision as obvious in hindsight; or taking sole credit for a team decision. Name who disagreed and what would have changed your mind — a decision you cannot falsify is not a decision, it is a preference.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

What is the most expensive architectural decision you have influenced, and what were the criteria?

Hint: expensive means hard to reverse — a datastore choice, a boundary, a protocol, a framework, a build-vs-buy. Try to reconstruct the criteria you actually used at the time, including the ones you would not put in a design doc (team skills, hiring, politics). Interviewers reward honesty about the non-technical constraints.

When did you argue against the more exciting option?

Hint: look for the times you said "not yet" rather than "no". What evidence moved the room? If nothing moved and you were overruled, that is still a story — how you committed to a decision you argued against is a strong principal-level signal.

Where in your current system is a boundary enforced only by convention?

Hint: shared tables, a "common" project everyone depends on, a service that queries another service's database, a nullable field two teams interpret differently. What would it take to make that boundary mechanical — an architecture test, a schema grant, a separate connection string?

Have you ever split or merged a service? What did it actually cost?

Hint: count the things nobody budgets for — pipelines, dashboards, alerts, runbooks, auth between the halves, a new failure mode in the caller, the data migration, and the six weeks of double-running. Being able to quote that list is what makes the "we chose not to" version credible.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

What would have made you choose microservices instead?

Three things, any one of which is close to decisive. First, deploy independence that is genuinely blocked — if two sub-teams cannot release without each other and the release cadence is the business constraint, that is worth paying for. Second, materially different scaling or cost profiles, like our renderer, where scaling the whole monolith to serve one hot path wastes real money. Third, a compliance or blast-radius boundary — data that must live in a different region or under different access control. Team growth alone would have moved me too: past roughly 25–30 engineers, the coordination cost inside one artefact starts to exceed the operational cost of separate ones.

How did you handle the engineers who wanted the split?

I gave their concern a real home rather than out-arguing it. The concern was "we cannot ship independently", so we made that measurable — deploy frequency and change-failure rate on the dashboard — and agreed up front that if the modular approach did not move those numbers in two quarters, we would revisit with data instead of opinion. Two of the strongest advocates owned the module seams, which meant they got the architectural work they wanted and were the first to feel it if the seams leaked. I also wrote the ADR to state their position fairly, including what it would have bought us; a decision record that strawmans the losing option poisons the next one.

At what scale does a modular monolith break?

Usually not at request volume — a single .NET process handles a great deal, and you can run many replicas of the same artefact behind a load balancer. It breaks on three axes instead: build and test time as the codebase grows (we bought headroom by parallelising, but there is a wall); the shared database becoming the coupling you thought you had removed, especially when one module's write pattern degrades another's query plans; and deployment risk, because one bad module takes down every module in the process. The honest framing is that the monolith buys you simplicity per unit of team size, and the moment team size or blast radius crosses your tolerance you start extracting — from seams you already enforced.

You kept one database. Isn't that the classic mistake?

The classic mistake is one schema that every module reads and writes. We used separate schemas in one instance, with each module's DbContext granted rights only to its own — so cross-module reads had to go through the owning module's contract, and a violation failed at the database, not at review. That keeps the operational simplicity of one instance (one backup, one failover, one connection pool budget) while preserving the ownership property that makes extraction possible. It is not free: noisy-neighbour effects are real, and a runaway query in one schema can still starve another, which is exactly the risk that later justified moving the reporting module to its own instance.

What did you trade away by not splitting?

Independent deployability for most of the system, and the freedom to use a different runtime or datastore per module. We accepted that a bad deploy affects everything, and paid it down with feature flags, canary releases and a fast rollback path rather than with process isolation. We also gave up the organisational forcing function: separate services make ownership unavoidable, whereas in a monolith you have to create ownership deliberately, with codeowners and an on-call rota per module. If I had a team that would not maintain those disciplines, splitting would look better despite the cost.

Would you make the same call today, in 2026?

More confidently, because the tooling has moved in the modular direction. Aspire (13.5 as of August 2026) makes running a handful of processes plus their dependencies locally a one-command affair, which used to be the strongest practical argument for keeping everything in one process, so I would judge the extraction question on architecture rather than developer ergonomics. .NET 10's build and startup improvements also shorten the CI feedback loop that was our real pain. The decision rule I would use is unchanged: extract when deploy independence, scaling profile or blast radius demands it, and never because a diagram looks tidier.

03The retry storm that took us down

tier · staff production incident / outage

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

It started as a 40-second blip in a downstream pricing service and became a 51-minute full outage of our checkout, which is the part that still stings: the dependency recovered on its own after less than a minute, and we kept ourselves down for another fifty. That is the anatomy of a retry storm. When pricing slowed, every one of our API instances retried three times with a fixed 200 ms delay, each retry holding a request thread and a connection; queue depth grew, our own latency crossed the load balancer's health-check timeout, instances were marked unhealthy and recycled, and the survivors took the load of the dead ones and fell over faster. The failure was ours: we had built an amplifier.

I was incident commander, so my first job was not to fix it but to structure it — one commander, one comms person posting to the status page every 10 minutes, one scribe, and everyone else off the call. We stopped the bleeding in three moves: shed load by disabling the price-refresh path behind a feature flag so checkout used the last known price for up to 15 minutes; drop the retry count to zero via configuration; and raise the health-check timeout so healthy instances stopped being killed for being slow. Recovery took nine minutes once we stopped fighting ourselves. Root-causing came after, in daylight.

The permanent fix was a policy, not a patch. Every outbound dependency call now goes through a named HttpClient with a standard resilience pipeline: a total request budget rather than per-hop timeouts, exponential backoff with jitter, a retry budget capped at 10% of successful traffic so retries can never more than decuple load, a circuit breaker per dependency, and a bulkhead that limits concurrent calls so one slow dependency cannot consume every thread. .NET 8 added Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience on top of Polly v8, and .NET 10 keeps it as the recommended path, so this is a dozen lines rather than a framework.

builder.Services.AddHttpClient<PricingClient>(c => c.BaseAddress = new(cfg["Pricing:Url"]!))
    .AddStandardResilienceHandler(o =>
    {
        o.TotalRequestTimeout.Timeout   = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2);   // the budget for all attempts
        o.AttemptTimeout.Timeout        = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(600);
        o.Retry.MaxRetryAttempts        = 2;
        o.Retry.UseJitter               = true;                       // no synchronised herds
        o.CircuitBreaker.FailureRatio   = 0.3;                        // trip at 30% failures
        o.CircuitBreaker.SamplingDuration = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);
    });

The subtlest lesson was the health check. Ours called the pricing dependency, so a slow dependency made every instance report unhealthy simultaneously — the orchestrator dutifully killed the entire fleet. Liveness must answer "is this process wedged", readiness "can this instance take traffic right now", and neither should fail because a downstream is sad; that is what degradation is for. What I would do differently: I would have run a game day on this exact scenario. We had the resilience library and never tested the failure mode, and an untested fallback is a hypothesis, not a control.

What the interviewer is really probing

Two things at once: incident-command judgment (did you stabilise before diagnosing, did you structure the response, did you communicate) and systems thinking (do you understand that retries, health checks and timeouts are feedback loops that can amplify a small fault into an outage). At staff and above they want the follow-through: the policy, the guardrail, the game day — evidence you fixed the class of problem rather than the instance.

The mistakes: blaming the downstream service ("their outage"), telling it as a heroic solo debugging session, or skipping the customer impact. Say the duration, the blast radius and what customers experienced, and own the amplifier as your design flaw — it is the single most credible move in this archetype.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

What is the worst outage you were in the room for, and what was your actual role?

Hint: write the timeline first — detection, mitigation, resolution, with times. Then mark which decisions were yours. Interviewers accept a supporting role told precisely far more readily than a leading role told vaguely.

Where does your system amplify a fault instead of absorbing it?

Hint: retries without budgets, cron jobs that all start at the top of the hour, a cache that stampedes on expiry, a queue consumer that redelivers poison messages forever, health checks that depend on dependencies, autoscaling that scales on a metric the fault distorts. Pick one you know is still there.

When did a mitigation you chose make things worse?

Hint: restarting instances mid-incident and losing the evidence, scaling up and exhausting a downstream connection limit, rolling back a schema change that had already been consumed. This is the highest-value story in the whole bank if you can tell it without defensiveness.

What can you turn off under load, right now, without a deploy?

Hint: feature flags, a kill switch for a non-essential path, a read-only mode, a queue you can pause. If the answer is "nothing", that is the story — what you did about it, and what it took to get the first flag in.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not simply remove retries altogether?

Because most transient failures are genuinely transient — a TCP reset, a rolling deploy on the other side, a brief garbage-collection pause — and a single retry converts a large slice of user-visible errors into a slightly slower success. The danger is not retrying, it is unbounded retrying with no budget and no idempotency guarantee. So my rule is: retry only idempotent operations, cap attempts, always jitter, and hold retries to a fixed fraction of successful traffic so the total load you can inflict on a sick dependency is bounded. For non-idempotent writes I make them idempotent with a client-supplied key first, then allow retries — otherwise a "safe" retry is a duplicate order.

What if the dependency had stayed down for two hours?

Then the circuit breaker holds it open, and the design question becomes what checkout does without pricing — which is a product decision I want made before the incident, not during. We landed on serving the last known price for up to 15 minutes, then blocking new checkouts for affected items with a clear message while the rest of the catalogue kept working. That is graceful degradation: shrink the failure to the smallest surface that is genuinely dependent. The alternative — queueing orders to price later — was rejected because it creates a reconciliation problem and a customer-trust problem when the deferred price differs.

How do you set the request budget number?

From the user-facing SLO backwards, not from the dependency forwards. If checkout must respond in under 2 seconds at p99, that is the budget at the edge, and each hop gets a deadline out of what remains rather than its own fresh timeout. The dependency's own p99 sets the per-attempt timeout — around 600 ms for pricing, which is roughly its p99 plus headroom — and I keep enough budget for one retry. The failure mode you avoid is per-hop timeouts that multiply: five hops at five seconds is a 25-second worst case nobody waits for.

How did you communicate during the outage?

Cadence over content. A status-page update every 10 minutes even when the update is "still investigating, next update at 10:40", because silence is what turns an outage into a trust incident. Internally, one channel, one commander, decisions written down by a scribe as they were made; anyone with a theory posted it in the channel rather than acting on it. I also called the customer-facing lead early with a plain-language impact statement so support was not inventing one. Afterwards, the postmortem went out within 48 hours, blameless, with the amplifier described honestly as our design flaw.

At what scale does this design still break?

When the fault is in something every request needs and cannot be stale — identity, the primary database write path, the message broker. Resilience policies buy you isolation from a dependency; they do nothing for a dependency with no fallback, and that is where you either build real redundancy (multi-AZ, a replica you can promote) or accept an availability ceiling and say so in the SLO. The other breaking point is correlated failure: if every service retries against the same struggling shared resource, per-service budgets still sum to a herd, which is why the budget belongs at the mesh or gateway layer once you have more than a handful of services.

What would you do differently now?

Run the game day before the incident, and make the resilience policy a platform default rather than a library each team remembers to use. Concretely: one shared extension method that configures every named HttpClient, a build check that fails when a client is registered without it, and a quarterly fault-injection exercise where we make pricing slow in staging and confirm the fallback, the flag and the alerts all behave. I would also separate liveness from readiness on day one — that single mistake turned a degradation into an outage, and it costs nothing to get right.

05Event sourcing vs. outbox: the argument

What I refused to do was win on seniority. (d) Follow-up drill chain

When would you actually choose event sourcing?

When the log is the domain rather than a record of it: ledgers and double-entry accounting, trading and position keeping, anything where regulators ask you to reconstruct state as of a date, or where temporal queries ("what did we believe on the 3rd?") are core features rather than support tooling. It also wins when many consumers need to derive different views of the same truth and you want them independent. What I would not do is adopt it for auditability alone, or in a team that has never run it — the failure mode is not the write model, it is the third year, when you have forty event versions and a projection rebuild that takes eleven hours.

Doesn't the outbox give you at-least-once delivery and therefore duplicates?

Yes, deliberately, and the consumers are where you pay for it. The outbox guarantees the event is never lost and never published without the state change; it cannot guarantee a single delivery, because the dispatcher can crash between publishing and marking the row sent. So every consumer is idempotent — keyed by the message id, with a processed-messages table or a natural upsert — and every handler is written to tolerate reordering. That is a smaller, more local burden than distributed transactions, and it is honest: exactly-once end to end is a marketing phrase, effectively-once through idempotent consumers is a design.

At what scale does the outbox table become the problem?

It becomes the hottest table you own: every business write adds a row, and the dispatcher polls it. Around a few thousand writes a second on Postgres you feel it — index bloat, vacuum pressure, and polling latency you cannot tune away. The fixes in order: partition by day and drop old partitions instead of deleting rows; switch from polling to logical replication or change data capture so the database pushes rather than being asked; and keep payloads small with a pointer to the real record. Time-ordered ids (UUIDv7, native in Postgres 18 and Guid.CreateVersion7() in .NET) matter more than people expect here, because random v4 keys fragment the index that the dispatcher scans.

How did you keep the colleague engaged after losing the argument?

By giving the part of his proposal that was clearly right a real home: he owned the event contracts — naming, versioning rules, the schema registry, the documentation — which was the genuinely valuable half of event sourcing and which we would not have built without him. I also said in the review, on the record, which of my assumptions his spike had disproved. The thing I watch for in myself is winning the decision and losing the person; the tell is whether they bring the next contentious idea to me or route around me.

If the spike had shown that the current-state screen could be served acceptably from a projection with a maintenance cost close to a table — say, a projection framework already in the stack and rebuild times in minutes — the ergonomics argument largely evaporates. I time-box on reversibility. If the decision is cheap to reverse, the debate is worth less than the information you get from shipping, so we pick and move within a day. If it is expensive to reverse — a storage model, a public contract, a boundary — I am willing to spend two weeks and a spike, because that is small against the multi-year cost. What I try never to do is let a decision drift undecided, because an unmade decision is silently made by whoever writes code next, and nobody records why.

06Read-your-writes and the ghost record

tier · senior nasty debugging session

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Support kept forwarding the same complaint: "I created the record, the page said it worked, and then it wasn't there." It never reproduced for us, there was no error, no exception, no 500 — the POST returned 201 with an id and the subsequent GET returned 404. About one report a day out of a few thousand creates, which is exactly the frequency that gets a bug dismissed as user error for three weeks.

The break came from correlating rather than reproducing. I pulled traces for the reported ids and found the pattern in ten minutes once I looked at the right attribute: every failing pair had the POST and the GET land on different database endpoints. We had added read replicas two months earlier and routed GET traffic to them — a change that had shipped smoothly and had nothing to do with this feature. The failures clustered when replication lag exceeded roughly 300 ms, which happened during the nightly bulk import and, more sporadically, whenever a long transaction on the primary held things up. The record existed; the replica just had not seen it yet. A ghost, entirely of our own making.

This is the bit worth internalising: physical replication is asynchronous by default, so a read replica gives you eventual consistency and, worse, non-monotonic reads if a client can hit different replicas on consecutive requests. Nothing about that is a bug in Postgres; it is the trade we made in exchange for read scale. The application has to decide, per read path, which consistency it needs. So we classified them: anything in a user's own post-write flow needs read-your-writes; dashboards and lists tolerate seconds of staleness; reporting tolerates minutes.

The fix had three layers. For the critical path, the API returns the write position (Postgres gives you an LSN via pg_current_wal_lsn()) in a short-lived cookie, and the read router either waits for a replica to reach that position or falls back to the primary — the general pattern of a session consistency token. For the merely-annoying paths, we made the client optimistic: the front end already had the created entity from the 201 response, so it stopped re-fetching it at all. And we put replication lag on the dashboard with an alert at 1 s, plus an automatic circuit that routes all reads back to the primary above 5 s, because at that point the replicas are a liability.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not use synchronous replication and be done with it?

Because you pay for it on every write, forever, to fix a problem that affects a fraction of reads. Synchronous commit means the primary waits for a replica to confirm — write latency then includes a network round trip, and worse, availability drops: if the synchronous replica is unreachable, writes stall unless you have quorum configured carefully. Postgres lets you choose per transaction, which is the useful nuance: you can set synchronous_commit to a stronger level for the handful of transactions that truly need it and leave the rest asynchronous. That targeted version is defensible; flipping it globally trades a read anomaly for a write outage.

At what scale does routing reads to the primary stop working?

When the read-your-writes window multiplied by write rate approaches the primary's read capacity. Ours was fine — 5 seconds of stickiness for users who had just written was a low single-digit percentage of reads — but a system where most sessions write constantly gets no relief from replicas at all. At that point you move to the position-gated version (wait for the replica to catch up to the recorded LSN, primary as fallback), and if that is not enough you are looking at partitioning writes so no single primary carries them, which is a much larger change with its own consistency consequences.

What did you trade away with the cookie approach?

Correctness at the edges, knowingly. A user writing from one device and reading from another is not covered, because the cookie is per browser; a write that takes longer than the window to replicate still shows a ghost; and I added state to a request path that was previously stateless, which makes reasoning marginally harder. I accepted that because the alternative — LSN plumbing through every read — was a week of work for a bug we needed gone that day, and because I could enumerate exactly which cases remained broken. The LSN version shipped six weeks later for the two paths where the residual cases mattered.

How would you have caught this before production?

By testing with lag, not just with a replica. Our integration environment had a replica with sub-millisecond lag on an idle machine, so the anomaly was invisible; injecting 500 ms of artificial delay would have failed the create-then-read test immediately. That generalises: environments that are too healthy hide entire classes of bugs, which is why I like fault injection in staging — latency, packet loss, a paused replica — as a routine part of the pipeline rather than an exotic exercise.

The front end had the created record already. Why was it re-fetching?

Because the data layer's cache invalidation was blunt: creating an entity invalidated the collection query, and the router immediately re-fetched the detail route on navigation. That is a perfectly reasonable default in a client cache, and it is worth saying that the front end was not wrong — it was making an assumption (a successful write is immediately readable) that the backend had quietly stopped honouring. The durable fix was for the API to return the full created entity and for the client to seed its cache from the 201 response, which also removed a round trip. Full-stack bugs usually live in an assumption that neither side wrote down.

How do you decide which read paths get strong consistency?

I ask what a user or a downstream system does with a stale answer. If staleness produces a wrong decision that is expensive or irreversible — a duplicate payment, a second refund, a shipment that should not go — it needs the strong path. If it produces a moment of confusion that self-corrects on refresh, eventual is fine and I say so in the design doc so nobody "fixes" it later. That classification lives next to the endpoints, not in someone's head, and it is the first thing I would hand a new engineer, because the cost of getting it wrong is asymmetric in both directions: too weak gives you ghost records, too strong gives you a primary that cannot scale.

07Cache stampede at 10x traffic

tier · staff scaling challenge

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We planned for a 10x traffic day and it went almost exactly as modelled — except for a 90-second window every five minutes where the site fell over. The pattern on the graph was unmistakable: a sawtooth. Latency flat, then a cliff, then recovery, then flat, precisely on the cache TTL boundary. That is a stampede, and it is one of the few production failures you can identify from the shape of the graph alone.

The mechanism is simple and everyone builds it at least once. A hot key — the homepage product ranking, computed from a 400 ms aggregate query — was cached in Redis with a flat 5-minute TTL. When it expired, every in-flight request missed simultaneously, and all of them ran the 400 ms query. At 8,000 requests per second that is several thousand concurrent aggregate queries against one primary, which saturates it, which makes the query take 4 seconds instead of 400 ms, which means far more requests pile in before the first one can repopulate the cache. The cache was not too small; it had no protection against its own expiry.

Four changes fixed it, and they compose. Coalescing: only one caller per key recomputes, the rest await that same result — this is what HybridCache gives you out of the box, and it shipped with .NET 9 precisely because everyone was hand-rolling it badly. Jitter: TTLs get a random spread (300 s ± 10%) so keys expire in a smear rather than a wave. Stale-while-revalidate: keep serving the expired value for a grace window while one background refresh runs, so expiry stops being a cliff. Negative caching: cache "not found" for a short TTL, because our second-worst hot path was a scraper requesting missing ids and passing straight through to the database.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just extend the TTL to an hour?

Because it changes the frequency of the stampede, not the existence of it — and it makes each occurrence worse, since an hour of accumulated traffic hits an empty key at once. It also worsens staleness in exactly the way users notice: a ranking an hour old on a fast-moving day is visibly wrong. TTL is the wrong knob; the right ones are coalescing (bounding concurrent recomputation) and stale-while-revalidate (removing the cliff entirely). Once those are in place, TTL becomes purely a staleness decision, which is how it should have been framed from the start.

How do you invalidate rather than expire?

For anything a user edits, expiry is too slow and too vague, so we publish an invalidation on the write path — the same outbox that carries domain events — and consumers evict the key. HybridCache supports tag-based invalidation, which matters because one edit usually invalidates a family of keys (the entity, the list it appears in, the search facet). The hard part is not the eviction, it is the race: a reader can repopulate from a replica that has not yet seen the write, putting the stale value straight back. So invalidation-on-write is paired with a short TTL as a backstop, and the write path reads from the primary. Belt and braces, because a permanently stale permission cache is a security bug.

At what scale does the two-tier cache break?

The L1 tier's cost is per-instance staleness and memory, so it breaks when the fleet is large and the item is edited often — with 200 instances and a 20-second L1, a user's edit can look inconsistent across refreshes for 20 seconds, which is unacceptable for anything they typed themselves. It also breaks when the working set outgrows process memory and you start evicting the useful keys. The next step is a smaller L1 restricted to genuinely global, slow-changing data, with per-user data served only from L2, and, if the hot key still saturates a single Redis shard, splitting the key by a random suffix so the load spreads across shards.

What is the staleness budget, and who agreed to it?

Product, explicitly, per item — that conversation is part of the design, not an implementation detail. Ranking: up to five minutes, and nobody outside engineering can tell. Price: 30 seconds and it must never be stale after a user's own change, which is why price goes through invalidation rather than expiry. Permissions and entitlements: seconds, with immediate eviction on revocation, because the failure mode there is a security incident rather than a confused customer. Writing that table down converted a series of ad-hoc arguments into a rule engineers could apply without me.

What happens when Redis itself fails?

We degrade rather than fail: HybridCache's L1 keeps serving the hottest keys, the origin takes the rest, and a concurrency limiter in front of the expensive aggregate query caps how much of that reaches the database — better to shed 5% of requests with a retry-after than to melt the primary and lose everything. We tested this by killing Redis in staging under load; the first attempt failed badly because the client's default timeouts were long enough to consume the request budget, so the app hung rather than degraded. That is the general lesson: the fallback path needs a tighter timeout than the happy path, or your resilience mechanism becomes the outage.

Would a CDN have solved this instead?

For the anonymous case, largely yes, and it is the cheaper answer when it fits: the ranking page is identical for everyone in a market, so an edge cache with a short TTL and stale-while-revalidate absorbs the peak without our servers seeing it at all. We did put the anonymous variant behind the CDN afterwards. It does not solve the authenticated case — personalised content, per-tenant data, anything behind a session — and it introduces its own invalidation problem, where a purge is a global operation with its own propagation delay. So the design ended up layered: CDN for anonymous, HybridCache for authenticated, and the same staleness budget applied to both.

08The tenant that could read another tenant

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A customer emailed support with a screenshot of a CSV export containing rows they did not recognise, with another company's reference numbers in them. That is the worst kind of bug report, because it is simultaneously a data-protection incident, a trust problem and a design failure — and because it means the reporter is a stranger doing your security testing for you.

The mechanism was mundane, which is typical. Our multi-tenant API isolated tenants with an EF Core global query filter: every entity carried TenantId, and HasQueryFilter appended the predicate to every LINQ query automatically. Clean, central, one line per entity. But six months earlier someone had rewritten the export endpoint with raw SQL, for a perfectly good reason — the LINQ version produced an awful plan for large exports. Raw SQL does not go through the query filter. There was no WHERE tenant_id = @tenant, only a filter on a date range and a customer id supplied by the caller, so a caller who guessed or enumerated another tenant's customer id got their rows. Our safety mechanism was invisible, implicit, and silently opt-out.

The response had a sequence I would repeat. Contain first: we shipped a patch within three hours that added the tenant predicate and, more importantly, denied the endpoint entirely if the tenant claim was absent. Then quantify: we replayed access logs for the endpoint over its lifetime, matching requested customer ids against the caller's tenant, and found 14 requests from 2 tenants that had crossed the boundary — all with the same shape, all consistent with users pasting an id from a shared spreadsheet rather than an attacker. Then notify: our privacy and legal people ran the disclosure with the affected customers, because engineers should not improvise that part. Only then did we fix the class of problem.

The class fix was defence in depth, on the principle that a control the application can forget is not a control. We turned on Postgres row-level security so isolation is enforced by the database, with the application setting the tenant for the transaction; even a raw SQL query with no predicate now returns nothing. We added a test that runs every endpoint twice with two seeded tenants and fails the build if tenant B's data appears in tenant A's response — about 200 lines of test infrastructure that would have caught this on the day it was written.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not give each tenant its own database instead?

It is the strongest isolation and I would choose it for a small number of large, regulated tenants — the blast radius of a bug like ours becomes zero, and per-tenant backup, restore and residency get easy. The costs are real, though: migrations must run across every database (thousands of them, with partial-failure handling), connection pooling gets harder because each database wants its own pool, and per-tenant resource waste is significant when most tenants are tiny. The middle option we considered was schema-per-tenant, which shares an instance but separates objects. My rule: shared with row-level security by default, dedicated for tenants whose contract or regulator demands it, and design the tenant resolution so a tenant can be moved between the two without an application change.

Row-level security has a performance cost. How much?

It adds the policy predicate to every query, so the practical requirement is that tenant_id is the leading column of the indexes that matter — with that in place we measured single-digit percentage overhead, which is a bargain. The subtler cost is planning: policies can prevent some plan shapes, and we found one report query that regressed badly until the index was reordered. The failure mode to watch is connection pooling, because the session variable must be set inside the transaction (SET LOCAL) rather than on the connection — otherwise a pooled connection carries one tenant's setting into another tenant's request, which is the very bug you were trying to prevent, now with more confidence behind it.

How did you know 14 requests was the complete number?

I did not know it with certainty, and I said so in the write-up. We had access logs with the caller's tenant and the requested id for the endpoint's whole lifetime, so within log retention the number is sound; before retention we had nothing, and the honest statement was "no evidence of access outside the retained window, which we cannot rule out". That gap drove a change: we now log the resolved tenant and the resource owner on every data-access path and retain it longer, because "we cannot tell" is an unacceptable answer to a regulator or a customer. Being precise about the limits of your evidence is more credible than a confident round number.

What stops the next engineer from disabling row-level security for a batch job?

Nothing technical stops a determined engineer, so the answer is a combination: batch jobs run as a role that has its own explicit policy rather than a bypass, the migration that would grant BYPASSRLS is blocked in review by a codeowner rule on the database role definitions, and the cross-tenant test suite runs against the batch paths too. I also made the legitimate need easy: there was a real reporting job that needed all tenants, and if I had not given it a supported way to do that, someone would have found an unsupported one. Security controls that make the legitimate case hard get routed around, and then you have neither the control nor the visibility.

At what point does this design need re-thinking?

When tenants stop being comparable. Row-level security with a shared schema works while tenants differ by a couple of orders of magnitude; once one tenant is 60% of your data, its queries distort plans and its vacuum load hurts everybody, and you are effectively running a single-tenant system with extra steps. That is the point to move the largest tenants to dedicated instances, keeping the tenant-resolution seam so the application does not care. The other trigger is regulatory: a customer requiring data residency in a specific region cannot be served by a shared instance elsewhere, regardless of how good the isolation is.

How do you test for this class of bug generally?

Two tenants seeded with structurally identical data and a test that walks the route table, calls every endpoint as tenant A with tenant B's identifiers, and asserts a 403 or 404 — never a 200 with data. New endpoints are picked up automatically because the suite enumerates routes rather than listing them, so forgetting to add a test is not a failure mode. On top of that, an authorization check on the read path is written as an explicit test per resource type, and we run a periodic scan for raw SQL that touches tenant-scoped tables without the predicate. None of this replaces a penetration test, but it converts the most common category — broken object-level authorization, consistently at the top of the OWASP API risks — into a build failure.

09Strangling the shared database

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The integration database was the kind of debt that everyone knows about and nobody can price: one SQL Server instance, about 40 tables that mattered, and five applications reading and writing them directly — two ASP.NET Core services, a legacy .NET Framework web app, a reporting tool, and a nightly SSIS package that nobody would admit to owning. Adding a nullable column was a three-team negotiation. Renaming one was impossible. The debt was not the schema, it was that the schema had become an implicit API with five consumers and no contract.

I did not propose a rewrite, because a rewrite of a shared database is a two-year project that gets cancelled at month fourteen. I proposed a strangler: pick one bounded context, put a service in front of it, migrate consumers one at a time, and delete the direct access only when the last consumer is gone. Crucially, we sequenced by consumer risk rather than by table, starting with the reporting tool because it was read-only and its owner was enthusiastic — an early, visible win buys you the political capital for the hard ones.

The mechanics that made it survivable were mostly about compatibility. We used the expand-and-contract pattern for every schema change: add the new shape, dual-write both, migrate readers, then remove the old shape in a later release — never in the same one. For consumers we could not change quickly (the SSIS package, the Framework app), we left a database view with the old table's name and shape, so their SQL kept working while the underlying tables changed beneath them. And we made ownership physical: each application got its own database login with grants only to what it was allowed to touch, which converted "please stop writing to that table" from a plea into a permission error.

How did you stop the migration from stalling at 80%?

By making the old path progressively unavailable rather than merely discouraged. Every compatibility view had an expiry date agreed with its consumer and a log entry on each use, so we could show a team their own usage graph and the date it would stop working; permissions were then revoked on that date, in a scheduled change, not as a surprise. What if you had needed to change a column type, not add one?

Same pattern, more steps: add the new column with the new type, dual-write, backfill in batches sized so no batch holds a long transaction, verify with a comparison query, switch readers, then drop. The two things that bite are the backfill and the constraint: on a large table, a naive UPDATE takes a lock or bloats the table, so it is chunked and resumable with progress recorded; and the new column cannot be made NOT NULL until the backfill completes, which means the not-null constraint is its own later release. On SQL Server I would also watch for the operation being metadata-only versus a full table rebuild, because the difference is between milliseconds and an outage.

Why not put a message broker between them instead of a service?

For write flows, we did — the service publishes domain events and two consumers moved to those instead of polling tables. But events do not replace queries: the reporting tool needed to ask arbitrary questions, and turning that into an event stream would have meant rebuilding its query capability somewhere else. So the split was: commands and notifications go through events, queries go through the service or a documented read replica. Choosing a broker for everything is a common overcorrection, and it produces a system where nobody can answer a simple question without building a projection first.

What did the service in front cost you in latency?

About 6 ms at p50 and 20 ms at p99 for consumers that used to query directly — a real cost, and I would rather name it than pretend the abstraction is free. It was worth it because those consumers were not latency-sensitive, and because the service could cache aggressively in ways five separate consumers never coordinated. Had a latency-critical consumer existed, I would have given it a read replica with a contract-bound view rather than forcing it through the hop, which is the general principle: the boundary is about ownership of writes and schema evolution, and reads can take a shorter path when the contract is explicit.

At what point would you have said "rewrite it" instead?

If the data model itself had been wrong rather than merely shared — if the concepts were incoherent enough that no service could present a sensible contract over them — then a strangler just wraps the confusion in HTTP. The other case is a platform dead end: a datastore going out of support or unable to meet a hard requirement, where you are moving regardless. Even then I would migrate incrementally with dual-writes rather than a flag-day cutover; what changes is that the target is a new model, not a facade over the old one. The rewrite decision is about whether the existing model can be evolved, not about how unpleasant the code is.

10Teaching a team to run design reviews

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I noticed I had become a bottleneck in a way that flattered me and hurt the team: every non-trivial design came to me, we talked it through at a whiteboard, and the engineer implemented what we had agreed. Fast, high quality, and completely unscalable — three teams, one me, and a growing queue. Worse, nobody else was getting better at design, because the hard part (choosing between options under constraints) kept happening in my head.

So I made design a written, reviewable artefact and taught people to run it. The template was deliberately short — one page, five headings: the problem and who has it; the constraints and non-functional requirements with numbers; two or three options with trade-offs; the decision and why; what we will do if we are wrong. That last heading did more work than the rest combined, because it forces the author to think about reversibility, and it makes the review about the decision rather than about the author's taste.

Teaching it went in stages with one engineer at a time. First they wrote a design for something they had already built, which is a low-risk way to learn the shape. Then they wrote one alone and I reviewed it as a peer. Finally — the step people skip — they facilitated someone else's review, because running a review is a distinct skill: keeping it to the decision, protecting the author, and closing with an actual outcome rather than a vague "let's think about it".

The mid-level engineer I started with is the story I would tell. Her first solo design proposed a message queue for a workflow that ran forty times a day; the review's job was not to say no but to ask what the queue was buying at that volume. She came back with a version using a database-backed job table and a note explaining when she would revisit — and that note is exactly the artefact I wanted, because it shows a decision bounded by a condition instead of an opinion. Two quarters later she was reviewing other people's designs and catching things I would have missed about the front end, which was never my strength.

How do you review a design without taking it over?

What if the design is genuinely going to fail?

Then I stop being subtle, and I separate reversible from irreversible. If it is irreversible and I believe it fails — a public contract, a data model, a security boundary — I say clearly that I am blocking, why, and what would unblock it, and I escalate to a decision rather than letting it grind. What I do not do is stay quiet to preserve autonomy and then say "I had concerns" at the postmortem.

Because the behaviour transferred to situations the process did not cover. The artefact can shrink but the decision record cannot disappear. With a resistant team I have used a much lighter form — a template comment on the pull request with three fields, or a fifteen-minute recorded walkthrough with the decision written underneath. What I insist on is that the constraints have numbers and that the decision plus its expiry condition exist in writing somewhere findable, because the point is not the document, it is that six months later a different person can find out why. The failure mode of ceremony-heavy design processes is real: if the template takes longer than the change, people route around it, and then you have neither documents nor reviews.

What did this cost you?

Speed, and some quality in the short term. The designs that came out of stage two and three were not as good as what I would have produced alone, and at least one of them cost us a couple of weeks of rework on a queue we did not need. I chose to pay that because the alternative — a team that produces excellent designs only while I am available — is fragile in a way that does not show up until I am on holiday or on another project. That is the trade at this level: a slightly worse decision made by someone who now owns it beats a better decision they will not be able to make next time.

How would you handle someone who is strong technically but harsh in reviews?

Directly, quickly, and privately, with specifics rather than adjectives — the exact comment, the effect it had, and what I wanted instead. Harsh reviewers are usually optimising for something real (correctness, standards) and have never been told what the collateral cost is: authors who stop circulating early drafts, which is precisely when review is cheapest. I would give them a concrete substitution — questions instead of verdicts, one blocking concern stated explicitly rather than fifteen equal-weight complaints — and I would pair them as a facilitator, because running a review teaches empathy for the author faster than any conversation about tone.

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Nine weeks to a launch that had already been announced to customers, and an honest estimate of about fourteen. Why keep multi-tenancy for a single-tenant launch? Isn't that speculative generality?

By pricing it honestly in their terms: at launch we expected fewer than 20 customers in the first month, ten minutes each is under four hours a week of an operations colleague's time, and the engineering equivalent was two and a half weeks that would otherwise come out of features they cared about more. The guardrail I use is a visible list of deferred items with triggers and owners, reviewed monthly with the same seriousness as incidents, and a rule that a new manual process requires retiring an old one when the list is full. It converts an unbounded accumulation into a budget.

Then my job is to make the trade explicit rather than to absorb it silently, because the fourth option — everything, on time, at quality — does not exist, and pretending it does is how teams end up shipping something broken on the date anyway. I would present the two real options with their consequences: a later date with the full scope, or the date with a defined subset, and I would say which parts of the full-scope-on-time version would be unfinished and what that means for customers. If the decision came back "all of it, on the date", I would document what I expected to break, ask for the rollback and support plan explicitly, and then commit properly rather than sulking. That documentation is not defensive paperwork; it is what makes the postmortem a systems conversation rather than a blame one.

Less than we feared, and the reason is worth generalising: the cut features were the ones nobody could name a customer for. How do you protect quality when you are cutting?

By cutting scope rather than rigour, which sounds like a slogan but has a concrete meaning: fewer features, each finished, tested and observable, rather than all features at 80%. Tests, error handling and telemetry stay in scope because they are what make the manual processes and the deferred items safe — you cannot run an operational workaround on a system you cannot see. The place I do accept less is polish and edge-case coverage on paths with a documented manual fallback: if an unusual case fails, an operator can fix it. That distinction — reduce the surface, keep the depth — is the one I would defend in any room.

12The dual write that lost 4,000 events

tier · staff data-loss / corruption scare

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Finance found it, not us, which is the detail I always lead with when I tell this story. Their month-end reconciliation was short by a few thousand records against our own database: our orders table said 4,127 more shipments had been confirmed than the downstream fulfilment system had ever heard about. Nothing had alerted. No errors, no dead-letter queue, no gap in any metric we had — because we were not measuring the thing that was broken.

The cause was a dual write, the most common distributed-systems bug there is. The handler saved the order to Postgres and then published a message to the broker, two operations with no atomicity between them. If the process died, the pod was evicted, or the broker was briefly unavailable after the commit, the database had the truth and the world never learned it. We had a retry around the publish, which made it rare enough to look fine and rare enough to accumulate quietly over eight months — roughly 0.4% of confirmations, entirely invisible.

Recovery came before redesign. We could identify the missing events because the orders table had the state and the timestamps, so we wrote a reconciliation query, produced the list, and replayed the events through the normal consumer path with their original ids and timestamps — which worked only because the consumers were already idempotent, keyed on order id. That is the point at which I stopped and said out loud that idempotent consumers had just saved us a week of manual data repair; it is the cheapest insurance in event-driven design.

The permanent fix was the transactional outbox: the state change and the outbox row commit in one database transaction, and a dispatcher publishes from the outbox afterwards, retrying until the broker accepts. That converts "lost forever" into "delivered late", which is a category of problem you can monitor and survive. Then the part that mattered as much: a continuous reconciliation job comparing the two systems every hour and alerting on a difference greater than a small threshold, plus an alert on outbox rows older than five minutes.

db.Outbox.Add(OutboxMessage.From(evt)); // same DbContext, same transaction

The fact that it was found externally is fine — how you responded is the story.

The mistakes: describing it as a fluke; skipping the recovery and jumping to the elegant architecture; or claiming exactly-once delivery, which will get you a follow-up you cannot survive. Also, do not hide the duration. "Eight months undetected" is embarrassing and is the entire reason the reconciliation lesson lands.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not use change data capture instead of an outbox?

CDC is genuinely attractive — no application code on the write path, and the guarantee comes from the database's own log, so there is no window at all. The reason I chose the outbox is semantics: CDC gives you row changes, and turning "these three rows changed" back into "an order was confirmed" is inference that lives outside the domain code and breaks whenever the schema changes. The outbox lets the domain say what happened in its own words, versioned, and keeps the coupling at the event contract rather than at the table shape. I would pick CDC when the consumer genuinely wants row-level replication — a data warehouse, a search index — and the outbox when consumers want domain events.

Replaying events changes their order relative to newer ones. Was that safe?

Only because we checked, and it is the question I would want asked. Our consumers were state-convergent for the affected event type: applying "order confirmed" for an order that is already shipped is a no-op rather than a regression, because the handler moves state forward and ignores backward transitions. Where that had not been true, the safe path would have been to replay into a staging consumer, diff the resulting state, and apply corrections deliberately rather than through the live pipeline. The general rule I now write into event contracts is that handlers must be both idempotent and tolerant of out-of-order arrival, with a version or timestamp to decide staleness — because reordering is not an exceptional case in a distributed system, it is Tuesday.

At what scale does the outbox stop being viable?

At high write volumes it becomes the hottest table you own, and polling it adds latency you cannot tune away. The progression is: partition by time and drop partitions rather than deleting rows; move from polling to a push mechanism driven by the replication log; and keep payloads small, storing a pointer rather than the whole document. Past that, you are effectively running CDC and should adopt it deliberately. There is also a latency floor: a polling dispatcher adds a fraction of a second, which is fine for fulfilment and wrong for anything interactive — for those paths I would publish synchronously and rely on the outbox as the guarantee, treating the synchronous publish as an optimisation rather than the mechanism.

How do you decide the reconciliation threshold and cadence?

From the cost of being wrong and the natural latency of the pipeline. Fulfilment tolerates minutes, so hourly reconciliation with an alert on any non-zero difference older than fifteen minutes was right; money movement would get every few minutes with a zero-tolerance threshold, and a warehouse export can be daily. The subtlety is the in-flight window — a naive comparison always shows a difference for records created in the last few seconds, so the query compares a window that closed some time ago, otherwise the alert cries wolf and gets muted, which is worse than not having it. An alert people ignore is not a control.

What did you tell finance, and when?

Immediately, with what we knew and what we did not, and then daily until it closed. Specifically: the discrepancy is real, it is in our favour to correct, here is the count as of now, we do not yet know whether it is bounded to this month, and we will have that answer by Thursday. Data-integrity incidents destroy trust much faster than outages because the other party cannot see the system and has to take your word for it, so precision and cadence matter more than speed. I also gave them the reconciliation report afterwards as a standing artefact, which converted a one-off panic into a routine control they could run themselves.

What would you standardise across the organisation after this?

Three defaults. Any state change that must be observed elsewhere goes through an outbox or CDC — never a publish after a commit — and that is a review checklist item rather than a wiki page. Every consumer is idempotent by construction, with the message id persisted, because that single property is what makes recovery possible at all. And every cross-boundary integration ships with a reconciliation job and an alert before it goes live, treated like a database backup: not optional, and tested. None of that is novel; the reason it does not exist by default is that dual writes work perfectly in every test you write, and only fail in production, rarely, silently.

The sharing model went behind a single authorization function rather than being spread through queries, so "teams" later meant changing one place. (d) Follow-up drill chain

How do you tell "designing for change" from over-engineering?

By what it costs today. A seam — an interface with one implementation, a nullable foreign key, a function that holds a rule in one place — costs close to nothing and buys optionality. A framework — a configurable rules engine, a plugin system, an abstraction over three databases you do not use — costs weeks and buys a future you invented. My test is: can I describe the change it enables in one sentence, and is the change plausible within a year? If the answer is a vague "it might need to be flexible", that is over-engineering. The other tell is whether the abstraction has one implementation or zero real ones; zero means you are designing against imagination.

Why is the time-zone decision irreversible when the sharing model is not?

Because it corrupts data rather than code. Storing a bare local time throws away information you cannot recover: for a row created before a daylight-saving change you cannot reconstruct what instant the user meant, and for cross-zone participants you cannot tell whose local time it was. No amount of later engineering recovers a discarded fact. The sharing model, by contrast, is entirely code and additive tables — the underlying data (who created what, in which tenant) is all still there, so a later change is work rather than archaeology. That is the general rule I use: decisions that discard information are irreversible; decisions that only arrange code are not.

What if the product manager had insisted on designing for external participants up front?

I would have asked for the cheapest thing that keeps the door open and priced the full version so the choice was informed. Concretely: keeping the participant reference nullable and not assuming a participant is always a user in our identity system costs nothing, whereas building account-less invitation flows, external authentication and a separate permission model is a quarter. If they still wanted it, that is a legitimate business call — perhaps a deal depends on it — and my job is to make the cost visible, not to veto it. What I would resist is the middle path where we half-build it: speculative infrastructure that is never exercised is the worst of both, because it carries maintenance cost and still will not fit the real requirement when it arrives.

At what point does the "seams not frameworks" approach fail?

When the change you eventually need cuts across every seam at once — a new dimension in the data model, like adding a hierarchy above tenants, or a change of consistency guarantee. Seams are local; a cross-cutting change touches all of them, and no amount of interface discipline saves you. That is why I spend the design effort disproportionately on the dimensions of the model (what entities exist, what owns what, what is identified by what) and comparatively little on the code structure: getting the dimensions wrong is the expensive failure, and it is the one seams cannot rescue.

How did you keep the team from thrashing on each change?

By making the open questions visible and unsurprising. The design document had a section listing what we knew we did not know, so when "teams" arrived it was already on the list with a note about how we had prepared — the change felt like a resolution rather than a reversal. I also protected the team from mid-sprint churn by batching requirement changes into the next planning boundary unless something was genuinely urgent, which cost a little latency and saved a lot of morale. The thing that damages a team is not change, it is the feeling that the change was random and that their work is disposable; showing that the design anticipated the shape of the change fixes most of that.

Would you have done anything differently if this had been a public API from day one?

Yes — the calculus changes completely, because a public contract is irreversible in the same way as stored data. I would have shipped a deliberately smaller surface, versioned it from the first release, avoided exposing anything I was unsure about (recurrence, participant shapes) even as a nullable field, and used an explicit extension point rather than optional properties that consumers would start depending on. With internal consumers you can change a shape with a coordinated deploy; with external ones you own that shape for years. That is why I treat "who consumes this" as the first question in any design that has an interface, ahead of storage or scale.

A logistics partner added a new value to a status enum on a Tuesday afternoon. No notice, no version bump — from their point of view an additive change, which is a defensible position. But the design question that mattered was more interesting: what should a consumer do with information it does not understand? Failing closed is right when the unknown value could mean something dangerous — a payment status you cannot interpret should never be treated as "paid". Failing open is right when the record is informational and stopping the pipeline is worse than being briefly ignorant. We had never made that choice deliberately; the deserialiser had made it for us.

The durable fix was an anti-corruption layer. Their model stopped leaking into ours: one translation module owned the mapping from their payload to our domain events, and every unmapped value produced a metric and a review item rather than an exception. We generated our client from their OpenAPI document and diffed that document nightly, so a schema change showed up as a pull request the morning it happened rather than as a dead-letter queue. And I did the unglamorous relationship work — found the engineer on their side, got onto their release announcement list, and gave them our integration's contact so a change like this had somewhere to land.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Should the partner have versioned the change?

By the letter of most versioning policies, adding an enum value is additive and does not require a new version — and I think that is the right policy, because the alternative makes evolution impossible. The obligation it creates is on the consumer: if you model an open set as a closed enum, you have made an assumption the contract never gave you. The mature framing, which is also what I would say to their engineer, is that enums in a public contract should be documented as extensible and consumers should be told to tolerate unknown values, ideally with an example in the documentation. Where I would hold them to account is a semantic change to an existing value, which is genuinely breaking regardless of the schema.

Fail-open sounds risky. When would you refuse it?

Whenever an unknown value could carry a meaning that permits something. Payment and entitlement statuses fail closed: an unrecognised value is treated as "not paid", "not entitled", and the request stops. Anything that triggers an irreversible physical action — dispatch, refund, deletion — fails closed too. Delivery tracking is informational and reversible, so failing open with a flagged review is the better trade. What I insist on is that the decision is per field with a written reason, because the default chosen by whatever serialiser you happen to use is not a decision, and it will be wrong in one direction or the other.

How do contract tests work when you cannot run their service?

Two layers. Against their sandbox, a small nightly suite that exercises the calls we depend on and asserts the shapes and error behaviours we rely on — it is a real integration test, so it is slow, flaky by nature and deliberately kept tiny. Locally, the same expectations are encoded as a fake built from their published examples, which every one of our tests runs against, so the fast tests and the nightly ones assert the same contract. Where a partner supports consumer-driven contracts properly you can publish your expectations to them and they run them in their pipeline, which is the ideal; in practice most partners do not, so owning both halves is the pragmatic version.

At what point would you abstract over multiple partners?

When there is a second one, and not before — the anti-corruption layer already gives you the seam, so adding a second implementation is a day rather than a redesign. What I would avoid is the speculative "carrier-agnostic" abstraction built on one example, because an abstraction derived from a single case invariably encodes that case's quirks and then fights the second one. With two real partners you can see which differences are essential (identifier formats, status vocabularies, webhook signing) and which are incidental. The exception is when the business has explicitly committed to multi-partner — a tender in progress, a regulatory requirement — in which case the second implementation is not speculation, it is scheduled work.

What if their sandbox does not match production?

It usually does not, and that has to be assumed rather than discovered. My defences are: keep the raw payload of every inbound message for a retention window, so a production-only discrepancy can be diagnosed and replayed; alert on unknown values and on schema drift from the production traffic itself, not only from the sandbox; and treat sandbox tests as detecting changes rather than proving correctness. The raw payload retention has paid for itself repeatedly — when a partner disputes what they sent, the argument lasts one message instead of a week, and when you need to reprocess, you can.

How would you design your own API so consumers do not suffer this?

Document extensibility explicitly: state which fields are open sets, tell consumers to tolerate unknown values, and provide an example of doing so. Version the contract, publish an OpenAPI document as a build artefact with a diff gate so nobody changes it accidentally, and hold a deprecation window measured in releases with usage telemetry per version so you know who is still on the old one. Announce changes on a channel consumers actually read, and give them a sandbox that mirrors production behaviour including errors and rate limits. Finally, prefer additive change and tolerate reading — the robustness principle is imperfect advice in general, but for an API with consumers you cannot deploy, it is close to right.

15The active-active project I killed

EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We had committed to multi-region active-active. The trigger was a customer contract asking for 99.99% availability, the design was mine, and after six weeks and roughly two engineer-quarters of work I recommended we stop. That recommendation was the most useful thing I did that year, and it was also the most uncomfortable, because I was arguing against my own design in front of people who had rearranged their roadmap for it.

What changed my mind was measuring where our downtime actually came from. I pulled eighteen months of incidents and classified the minutes: about 55% from bad deploys and configuration changes, 25% from a single dependency (the identity provider) failing, 15% from database maintenance and failovers within one region, and about 5% from anything a second region would have helped with. We were building a very expensive answer to 5% of the problem. Worse, active-active would have increased the 55%, because every deploy would now be a multi-region orchestration with more states to get wrong.

The second problem was conflict resolution, and it is the one people underestimate. Active-active with writes in both regions means either a globally distributed database with the latency and cost that implies, or application-level conflict resolution for every entity — and when I actually enumerated our entities, several had no defensible merge semantics. Two regions accepting a booking for the same resource is not a conflict you resolve, it is a promise you break. The honest options were active-passive with a fast failover, or partitioning customers by region so each entity has one home — the latter being a genuinely good design that the contract did not require.

So I wrote a two-page recommendation: stop, and spend a fraction of the remaining budget on the actual causes. Deploy safety (canary releases, automatic rollback on error-budget burn), removing the identity provider as a single point of failure with cached tokens and a degraded mode, and a tested, automated database failover with a measured recovery time objective. The measured result over the following year was better availability than the active-active plan would plausibly have delivered, at roughly a fifth of the cost — and, crucially, we could tell the customer a true story with numbers, which is what they actually wanted.

We would have failed over in a measured 11 minutes with a recovery point objective under 30 seconds, because we practised it quarterly with real traffic — and the practising is the whole answer. An untested failover has an effective recovery time of "unknown, probably hours", which is precisely how organisations discover that their standby database was missing an extension or that a DNS record has a two-hour TTL. By reframing the question as forward-looking and making it concrete: the six weeks are spent regardless, so the only decision is what the next two quarters buy. When the workload is genuinely partitionable so writes have a home region — a per-customer or per-region data model — or when the data has conflict-free semantics, like append-only telemetry or counters that can be merged. Regulatory data residency is another honest driver, since it forces regional homes anyway and active-active becomes a small increment. And there are businesses where a region-scale outage is existential rather than expensive, where the cost is simply justified. What I would not do again is choose it as a general availability strategy without first proving that region failure is a meaningful share of the downtime you actually experience.

Credibility with a couple of people, temporarily, and a visible reversal on something I had advocated for. What repaired it was making the deploy-safety and failover work substantial rather than a consolation prize, and giving him the failover automation to own, which turned out to be the piece leadership talked about most that year. The cost was real, and I would pay it again: an engineering organisation where nobody can stop their own project accumulates zombie work faster than it ships.

How do you decide when a spike has told you enough?

I set the decision criteria before the spike starts — what would make us proceed, what would make us stop — and a time box. In this case the stated criterion was whether we could define conflict resolution for every entity that accepts writes; when the answer for bookings turned out to be "there is no correct merge", that was the stop condition firing, not a matter of taste. Without pre-agreed criteria, spikes either run until someone loses patience or produce a conclusion shaped by whoever is most senior in the room. Writing them down in advance is also what makes it possible to stop a project without it feeling like a judgment on the people doing the work.

Because they were 12% of the bill and 100% of the risk. The disciplined move is to rank by expected value: big share, low risk first, and stop when the remaining items are small or dangerous. I did make one database change, moving old partitions to cheaper storage, because it was reversible and touched nothing in the hot path. Knowing when to stop optimising is part of the skill; the last 5% of a cost programme usually costs more engineering time than it saves.

At larger scale the levers invert. You also start needing chargeback so teams see their own costs, because central optimisation does not scale past a few dozen services. And the commitment instruments (reserved capacity, savings plans) become significant, which introduces a forecasting problem — you are now betting on your own growth curve, and an architecture change that reduces usage can strand a commitment you already bought.

And a quarterly fifteen-minute review of the top ten line items with the team, which is short enough that people actually attend. The failure mode of cost programmes is that they are heroic quarterly efforts followed by a year of drift; making it a monitored signal converts it into maintenance.

I accepted that because we measured the autoscaling response time and it was within our latency budget, and because the alternative — paying 3x for permanent capacity to make the first two minutes of rare events smoother — is a poor trade. The co-location change is the one I keep under review, because it does concentrate a failure mode I deliberately un-spread.

Isn't Aspire lock-in to a Microsoft way of doing things?

By treating attributes as a schema with a budget. We also review new instrumentation the way we review a database index: someone asks what question it answers, and if nobody can name the question, it does not ship. The semantic conventions for HTTP and database attributes are stable in OpenTelemetry, so most of this is following the standard rather than inventing one.

The failure mode at that scale is a platform team that mandates without supporting — my rule would be that the golden path must be genuinely easier than doing it yourself, measured by how many teams choose it when they are not required to.

A service mesh, twice. The page came in at 03:12: error rate above threshold on the write path. Our application was not healthy until 03:41, and the difference between those two numbers is the entire story: the failover worked exactly as designed, and we were the ones who could not cope with it.

Half-asleep, my sequence was deliberately mechanical, because 3 a.m. judgment is bad judgment. Confirm the alert is real (yes — errors, and a customer-visible one). Check the obvious external event before theorising (the provider's status page showed the failover). Only then, awake and with a colleague, work out why the applications did not recover on their own.

The reason was three small things compounding. Npgsql's pool was holding connections to the old primary that were not dead in a way it noticed quickly — no keepalives, so a broken connection looked idle rather than broken. The DNS record for the writer endpoint was cached in the process for longer than its TTL suggested, so even new connections went to the old address for several minutes. And our EF Core setup had no retry policy on the write path, so the first failure after the failover surfaced to the user rather than being retried a second later against the new primary. Each individually is a minor omission; together they turned a 40-second infrastructure event into 28 minutes of downtime.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not retry everything automatically? What could go wrong?

Duplicates on the write path, primarily. A retry after a command times out is genuinely ambiguous: the transaction may have committed and the acknowledgement been lost, so a naive retry can double-apply an operation. That is why the execution strategy must wrap the whole transaction rather than an individual command, and why non-idempotent operations need an idempotency key or a natural uniqueness constraint before you enable retries. The second risk is amplification — retries against a database that is genuinely overloaded make it worse — which is why the retry count is small, the delays back off, and there is a circuit breaker above it. Retrying is a correctness question first and a resilience question second.

Would connection pooling in front of the database have helped or hurt?

Both, and it is worth being precise. A pooler such as PgBouncer absorbs the reconnection storm after a failover, which helps, and it gives you a single place to point at the new primary. But it also becomes a component with its own failure and failover behaviour, and in transaction-pooling mode it breaks anything relying on session state — prepared statements, session variables, advisory locks — which is exactly the mechanism we used for tenant isolation elsewhere. So it would have helped this incident and introduced a constraint I would have to design around. I would adopt it for connection-count pressure, not for failover behaviour alone.

How do you decide what is page-worthy at 3 a.m.?

Customer impact that is happening now and needs a human. Everything else is a ticket for the morning, and I would rather under-page and be slightly slower on a Saturday than have a rota that stops reading alerts. Concretely: symptom-based alerts on the user-facing SLOs (error rate, latency, checkout success) page; cause-based alerts (CPU, disk, queue depth) are dashboards and tickets unless they predict imminent customer impact. Every page also has to have an action — if the runbook says "observe", it is not a page. Reviewing the pages that fired each month and deleting or downgrading the useless ones is unglamorous work that does more for reliability culture than most tooling.

At what scale does "roll the pods" stop being an acceptable mitigation?

Quickly, on two axes. If restarting drops in-flight work — long-running requests, uncommitted batch progress, WebSocket connections — then a restart is itself an incident, and you need graceful shutdown with connection draining before it is safe. And at larger fleet sizes a simultaneous restart creates a thundering herd against the database and any warm caches, so the restart has to be staged, which takes minutes you may not have. That is why the durable fix was client-side recovery rather than a better restart procedure: the mitigation that scales is the one where nothing needs restarting at all.

What did you tell the team the next morning?

The timeline, the mechanism, and explicitly that the provider did nothing wrong — because the first instinct in the room was to blame the maintenance window, and a team that blames its dependencies stops improving its own resilience. I also said out loud that I had not read the failover documentation, because a blameless culture is built by senior people modelling the admission rather than by a policy document. The action items were assigned with names and dates in that meeting, and the drill went on the calendar before we left the room; action items that leave a postmortem without a date are decoration.

How do you keep failover drills from becoming a checkbox?

Vary them and make them slightly uncomfortable. A rehearsed drill at a known time with the expert present proves very little; the value comes from running it during business hours with the normal on-call person leading and the expert silent, and from occasionally injecting a second complication. I also treat the runbook as the artefact under test — if the person following it gets stuck, the runbook failed, not them. And the results get recorded: measured recovery time per drill, tracked over quarters, so the exercise produces a trend rather than a feeling. When a drill stops finding anything for a year, that is the signal to make it harder, not to stop doing it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just retry failing tests automatically?

Because it hides the class of bug that flakiness usually indicates. A test that fails one time in six because it waits a fixed 200 ms for an asynchronous operation is telling you about a real race — sometimes in the test, sometimes in the code — and auto-retry converts that signal into silence until it appears in production as an intermittent bug. I will accept a single retry for a genuinely environmental class (a container image pull, a network blip in CI infrastructure) provided the retry is recorded and trended, because a rising retry rate is itself a signal. What I refuse is a blanket retry policy on the whole suite, which is how teams end up with a green pipeline and no idea whether it means anything.

Canary at 5% for ten minutes — is that enough to catch anything?

It catches the large, fast failures, which are most of them: a bad configuration, a broken dependency injection registration, a serialisation change, a 5xx spike. It does not catch slow burns — a memory leak, a query plan that degrades as the cache warms, a bug that only fires at month-end — so the canary is paired with the error budget and with alerting that persists after the rollout. For higher-risk changes we extend the window and the traffic share deliberately, and for data-shape changes we run a shadow comparison instead. The honest framing: a canary reduces blast radius and time to detect, it does not prove correctness, and treating it as proof is how teams get complacent about testing.

How do feature flags avoid becoming their own debt?

An expiry date and an owner per flag, a dashboard of flags older than 90 days, and a rule that a release flag is deleted in the sprint after it reaches 100%. Without that you get combinatorial state that nobody can reason about, code paths that are never exercised, and eventually an incident caused by an old flag flipped by accident. I also distinguish release flags (short-lived, delete them) from operational kill switches and experiment flags (long-lived by design, but owned and reviewed). The debt is real and I would rather name it than pretend flags are free — but the alternative, coupling code deployment to feature release, costs far more.

At what scale does this pipeline design break?

When the repository or the test suite grows past what can be run in full on every change. At that point you need test selection based on the change's dependency graph, a merge queue so batches are tested in the order they will land, and probably a monorepo build tool that understands affected targets. The other wall is the integration environment: with dozens of services you cannot spin up the whole world per pull request, so you move to contract tests plus ephemeral environments for the few flows that genuinely need end-to-end coverage. What does not change is the principle — the pipeline's job is a trustworthy answer fast enough that people wait for it.

How did you get the team to care about the DORA-style metrics?

By using them as diagnostics rather than targets, and by never showing them per person. The framing that worked was "these four numbers describe how painful our week is", and the first improvement — deploying without a Thursday ritual — was felt immediately, which bought patience for the rest. I was careful about the failure mode: metrics like deploy frequency become gameable and demoralising the moment they are used to compare teams or appear in performance reviews. They belong to the team, reviewed by the team, and the moment leadership wanted them as a scorecard I pushed back and offered change failure rate and time to restore as the ones that actually map to customer experience.

What did all this cost?

Roughly one engineer's time for a quarter, spread across the team rather than concentrated, plus a modest increase in CI compute from parallelisation and Testcontainers — real money, and cheaper than the incident load it removed. The bigger cost was attention: for six weeks, fixing flaky tests competed with features, and I had to defend that trade repeatedly with the flake-rate chart. I would defend it again, because the compounding is unusually strong here — every subsequent change is cheaper to verify, and the team's willingness to refactor went up noticeably once they trusted the suite, which is a benefit I did not predict and cannot measure.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can turn a vague, expensive request into a specific, cheap one without damaging the relationship — and whether you can be persuaded when the requirement turns out to be real. They listen for the question you asked (what decision changes?), the honest pricing of options, and the fact that you said yes to something. At principal level, the durable artefact — a written criterion others reuse — is the strongest part.

The mistakes: a story where you simply refused; treating the stakeholder as foolish; or agreeing to everything and then quietly under-delivering. Also, if you cannot articulate the technical differences between polling, SSE and WebSockets crisply, the push-back reads as avoidance rather than judgment.

(b) STAR / CARL scaffold

Situation
A powerful stakeholder asking for a system-wide property in one word. Quote the request — the vagueness is the point.
Task
Find the real requirement hiding inside it, and deliver that without committing the organisation to the expensive version.

(c) Recall prompts — find your own version

When did you say no to someone with more organisational power than you?

Hint: the useful version is a "yes, and here is the cost" rather than a flat refusal. What did you offer instead, and how quickly did you deliver it? Speed on the smaller thing is what makes the push-back land.

What request have you received that was a solution in disguise?

Hint: "make it real-time", "we need microservices", "put it in the cloud", "add AI", "make it scalable". What was the underlying need, and how did you get to it without making the asker feel corrected?

When were you wrong to push back?

Hint: a requirement you dismissed as gold-plating that turned out to matter — a compliance need, a sales blocker, an accessibility requirement. This is a strong story because it proves your push-back is a considered practice rather than a reflex.

What written decision of yours is still being used by people you do not talk to?

Hint: an ADR, a design principle, a checklist, a criterion. Scaled influence through artefacts is the clearest evidence of principal-level impact, and it is easy to forget you have it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why SSE rather than SignalR, given the team already knew SignalR?

Because the requirement was one-way and SSE is dramatically less machinery for that case: plain HTTP, no protocol negotiation, automatic reconnection with last-event-id built into the browser's EventSource, and nothing special at the proxy or load balancer beyond disabling response buffering. SignalR is excellent and I would reach for it the moment the client needs to send — collaborative editing, presence, acknowledgements — or when I need transport fallback for hostile networks. Choosing the smaller mechanism also kept the operational surface honest: no backplane, no sticky sessions, and a failure mode (the stream drops, the client reconnects and re-reads state) that anyone on the team can reason about at 3 a.m.

At what scale does the SSE design break?

At the point where connection count rather than request rate becomes the constraint. Each client holds an open connection, so 40 users is nothing and 200,000 is a different architecture — you need connection-terminating infrastructure separate from your application servers, a fan-out mechanism so a single event does not become 200,000 individual database reads, and a deploy strategy that does not reconnect everyone at once. Roughly: below a few thousand concurrent connections per instance you can ignore this; above that, budget for a dedicated push tier. The other break is per-client data: the moment each connection needs a personalised stream, the fan-out cost grows with users rather than with events, which is a much worse curve.

What if the stakeholder had insisted on the full version anyway?

I would have asked for it to be sequenced rather than argued about: build the one screen now, measure whether it changes the operations team's behaviour, and revisit the rest in a quarter with evidence. If they still insisted after that, it is a legitimate business decision made with the cost visible, and my job is to implement it well rather than to relitigate — while making sure the plan includes the operational pieces (connection budget, deploy strategy, mobile behaviour) that make it survivable. What I would not do is agree publicly and slow-walk it privately, which is the most corrosive behaviour a senior engineer can adopt and is usually transparent to everyone anyway.

How do you make "what decision changes?" work without sounding obstructive?

Tone and sequencing. I ask it as curiosity about their work rather than as a challenge — "walk me through what someone is doing when they are looking at this screen" — and I ask it before I express any opinion about the technology, so it does not read as a rhetorical trap. It also helps to bring the answer they might want: showing the operations lead a rough version at five-second refresh and asking whether that solves it converts an abstract debate into a concrete reaction. The version that sounds obstructive is when the question arrives after you have already decided the answer, which people detect instantly.

Where does the polling default itself become the wrong call?

When polling cost exceeds push cost, which happens earlier than people think: 20,000 clients polling every five seconds is 4,000 requests per second of mostly-unchanged responses, each with authentication, database work and serialisation, and that is more expensive than 20,000 idle connections. Conditional requests with ETags and edge caching push that crossover point out considerably, which is why polling scales further than its reputation suggests. My rule of thumb is that polling wins while the interval is comfortably longer than the tolerable staleness and the response is cacheable; once you find yourself shortening the interval to satisfy a latency requirement, you are paying push prices for pull semantics and should switch.

How do you keep this kind of criterion alive after you leave the team?

Write it where decisions are actually made rather than in a wiki nobody opens — in our case, a line in the design template asking "what is the freshness requirement, and what decision depends on it", plus the one-page note linked from it. Criteria survive when they are embedded in a workflow and when someone other than the author has defended one successfully; the three times it settled an argument without me were the real test. I also stated the conditions under which it should be revisited, because a rule with no expiry becomes dogma, and the next person deserves to know what evidence would justify changing it rather than being told what a previous principal engineer once thought.

21The big behavioural Q&A

cross-cutting prompts 21 questions deploy a prepared story

These are the prompts that are not tied to one incident. Each answer says which story to reach for and what the interviewer is listening for — the point is that you should almost never need a story you have not prepared. Two rules for all of them: lead with one sentence of situation, and never spend more than 90 seconds before the first result.

Failure & ownership

Tell me about a time you caused a production incident.

Deploy story 03 (the retry storm, where the amplifier was our design) or 12 (the dual write). Own the mechanism in the first two sentences — "we had built an amplifier" — because the interviewer is listening for whether you take responsibility before being asked. Give impact in customer terms, the mitigation, and the class-level fix. The failure mode is a story where the cause was someone else's; if your best example genuinely was, say so and describe your part of the response honestly.

What is the biggest mistake you have made as an engineer?

Story 15 (committing to active-active before measuring where downtime came from) is the strongest option because the mistake is judgment, not typing. Pick something with real consequences that you can describe without defensiveness, and finish with the specific habit that changed — for that one, "measure the gap before choosing the solution". Avoid humble-brag mistakes ("I cared too much about quality"); interviewers hear them constantly and discount the whole answer.

Tell me about a time you missed a deadline.

Use 11 inverted: the estimate exceeded the date, and the story is what you did with that fact and when. What matters is the timing of the disclosure — early, in writing, with options — and what you protected. If you have a genuine miss, describe how you kept the stakeholder informed as it slipped rather than presenting a surprise at the deadline; silence is the failure, not the slip.

Tell me about a decision you reversed.

Story 15 again, or 05 if the reversal was being persuaded by a colleague's evidence. The signal is intellectual honesty plus the ability to stop work already in flight. Say what the evidence was, how long you resisted it, and how you handled the people affected — the "how you killed it" part is what distinguishes a senior answer from a principal one.

Conflict & influence

Tell me about a disagreement with a colleague.

Story 05 (event sourcing versus outbox). Steelman their position in one sentence before describing yours — that single move signals maturity more than the outcome does. Then the mechanism: agreed criteria, spikes owned by each advocate, decision on evidence, and adopting their best idea. Close with the relationship outcome, because the interviewer is quietly assessing whether they would want to disagree with you.

Tell me about a time you influenced without authority.

Story 17 (the pilot-led tool adoption) or 09 (getting five consumers off a shared database). The pattern to make explicit: evidence first, a volunteer rather than a mandate, someone else presenting the result, and adoption spreading by request. Interviewers are checking whether you can move an organisation you do not manage — name the person whose support unlocked it.

Tell me about pushing back on a manager or executive.

Story 20 ("make it real-time") or 11 (scope). The structure that works: ask what decision changes, price the options honestly, say yes to the real need fast, and write down the criterion. Never characterise the stakeholder as naive — the answer should make the interviewer think you would handle them respectfully.

How do you handle a code review disagreement that is going nowhere?

Move it out of the thread and into a call or a design note within two rounds, because long review threads are where disagreements calcify. Separate blocking concerns from preferences explicitly and only block on correctness, security or an irreversible decision; if it is style, defer to a linter or the author. Referencing 10 is useful here — the review culture you built, and the specific coaching you gave a harsh reviewer.

Tell me about working with a difficult stakeholder or team.

Story 14 (the partner whose API changed) is the safest, because the resolution is systemic — controls you own, plus a working human channel — rather than a personality contest. Avoid framing anyone as difficult; describe the structural reason for the friction (different incentives, no shared release calendar, no contract) and what you changed about the structure.

Leadership & mentoring

Tell me about someone you developed.

Story 10. Name one anonymised person, their starting point, your intervention, and evidence of change — ideally something they did independently that you would not have done. The strongest single detail is a case where they caught something you missed. Avoid claiming their growth as your achievement; describe what you provided and let the interviewer infer.

How do you set technical direction across teams?

Combine 10 (the design review practice), 20 (a written criterion others reuse) and 02 (an architectural decision with recorded reasoning). The principal-level answer is about artefacts and mechanisms that work without you in the room — templates, criteria, ADRs, golden paths — not about being consulted on everything. Give an example of your influence operating in your absence.

How do you make sure your team's on-call is sustainable?

Story 18. Grade every page by "could the system have done this itself?", delete or downgrade alerts that produce no action, and treat recurring pages as a backlog with owners. Add the human side: rota fairness, time back after a bad night, and a postmortem culture where senior people admit their own gaps first. Interviewers hear a lot about tooling here and very little about the people, so lead with the people.

Tell me about a time you had to deliver difficult feedback.

The harsh-reviewer thread in 10 works well. Be specific about what you said, not just that you said it — the exact behaviour, its effect, and the substitution you offered. Include the outcome honestly, including if it took more than one conversation. Vague answers ("I gave direct feedback and they improved") read as invented.

Ambiguity & prioritisation

Tell me about a project with unclear requirements.

Story 13. Lead with the actual sentence you were given, then the method: separate unknown from unstated, settle the irreversible modelling decisions immediately, leave seams rather than frameworks, and use shipping as research. The distinguishing detail is naming a requirement you deliberately did not build that never arrived.

How do you prioritise when everything is urgent?

Reversibility and blast radius, as in 11: protect the decisions that cannot be undone, defer anything with a manual workaround, and attach a trigger and an owner to everything deferred. Say who you involve in the trade-off — this is a question about whether you decide alone or make the cost visible to the people who own the outcome.

How do you decide what to work on when nobody tells you?

Point at the measurement habit that recurs across 01, 16 and 19: find the number that describes the pain (p99, cost per thousand requests, change failure rate), show it to people, and let it argue. At principal level, add how you distinguish work that is genuinely under-owned from work that belongs to a team you should be enabling instead.

Tell me about a time you had to say no.

20 for a stakeholder request, 17 for the tools you declined (the service mesh, twice), 15 for stopping your own project. The best version includes what you said yes to instead, delivered quickly. A candidate who only has "no" stories reads as an obstacle; one who has none reads as a pushover.

Technical judgment & depth

Walk me through a system you designed end to end.

Use 02 as the spine and pull in 12 (event delivery), 06 (consistency per read path) and 08 (tenant isolation). Structure it as requirements and constraints with numbers, then the decisions, then the trade-offs you knowingly accepted, then what you would change. Interviewers rate the trade-off narration above the diagram; if you present a design with no downsides, they assume you have not run it in production.

What is the most technically complex thing you have worked on?

Choose by explainability, not by prestige: 12 (exactly-once semantics and recovery) and 07 (caching at 10x) are both complex and narratable in three minutes. Make the complexity concrete — the failure mode that made it hard — rather than adjectival. Then be ready to go two levels deeper than your summary, because "complex" is an invitation to drill.

How do you keep up with technology?

Answer with a mechanism plus a recent specific: release notes for the platforms you run, one deep source per area, and a habit of upgrading deliberately (04). Then prove it with something current and dated — .NET 10 as the LTS from November 2025, ASP.NET Core 10 defaulting to OpenAPI 3.1, EF Core 10 requiring .NET 10, .NET 11 due November 2026 with runtime async. One dated, checkable fact does more than a list of newsletters.

How do you balance speed and quality?

Reject the framing gently and use 11 and 19: cut scope, not rigour, and note that a trustworthy pipeline makes you faster rather than slower. Give the concrete rule — fewer features, each finished, observable and reversible — and name the place you accept less (polish on paths with a documented manual fallback). The measurable version is change failure rate falling while deploy frequency rises.

Values & motivation

Why do you want to work here, and what are you looking for?

Not a story question, but the one most often answered badly by strong engineers. Tie it to the work you have just described: if your best stories are about making systems legible and delivery boring, say that is what you want to do at more scale, and name something specific about their system or product that makes that interesting. Vague enthusiasm reads as a mass application; a specific, verifiable reason reads as intent.

22Night-before cheat sheet

read once then sleep

Ten sentences to read the evening before. They are about delivery, not content — the content is above, and cramming it tonight will not help.

  1. Open with one sentence of situation, then stop. "We ran an ordering system for about 400 requests a second on .NET, and one endpoint went from 140 milliseconds to four seconds at p99." Context, scale, symptom — then let them steer. Anything longer and you have spent your attention budget before the interesting part.
  2. Reach the result inside 90 seconds. Interviewers decide early whether you are a rambler. If the story is long, give the result early and offer the detail: "we cut p99 by 95%; the interesting part is how we found it — want that?"
  3. Quantify with the metric that matters, not the one you remember. p99 rather than average, change failure rate rather than deploy count, cost per thousand requests rather than the bill. If you do not have the number, say "roughly" and give a range — invented precision collapses under one follow-up.
  4. Separate "I" from "we" honestly and continuously. "The team migrated eleven services; I designed the sequencing and ran the pilot." Taking credit for team work is the single most common thing that sinks otherwise strong candidates, and interviewers probe it deliberately with "what was your specific contribution?"
  5. Own the failure before you are asked. "The amplifier was our design" beats being walked into it. Every story in this bank has a "what I would do differently" for exactly this reason — deliver it unprompted, and make it a habit change rather than a regret.
  6. Tie every decision back to a real trade-off. Name what you gave up: staleness for load, latency for ownership, cost for headroom, deploy independence for simplicity. A decision with no downside is a decision you have not examined, and that is what the drill-downs are testing.
  7. Have a scale answer ready for every story. "This holds to roughly X; past that the next constraint is Y." It converts a war story into evidence of design thinking, and it pre-empts the "at what scale does this break?" follow-up that is coming anyway.
  8. Be precise about versions and dates when you claim currency. .NET 10 is the LTS released 11 November 2025; EF Core 10 requires it; ASP.NET Core 10 emits OpenAPI 3.1 by default; .NET 11 is due November 2026. One dated fact signals you are current; a vague "the latest version" signals the opposite.
  9. When you do not know, say so and then reason. "I have not run that at that scale — here is how I would find out, and here is what I would expect to bite first." Interviewers are far more comfortable with a calibrated engineer than a confident one, and inventing an answer is unrecoverable.
  10. End each story with what it changed in how you work. The habit, the checklist item, the metric you now add on day one. It converts an anecdote into evidence of learning, which is the actual thing being assessed — and it hands the interviewer a natural place to go next.
