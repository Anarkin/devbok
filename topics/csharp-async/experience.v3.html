01The health check that could not get a thread

Senior Production incident / outage

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Fourteen pods of an ASP.NET Core service on .NET 10, four vCPUs each, doing a boring 2,000 requests per second at a p99 of 80 ms. On a Tuesday morning the p99 went to nine seconds in under three minutes, CPU on every pod sat at 15%, and the load balancer started pulling pods out because /healthz — an endpoint that returns a literal 200 and touches nothing — was timing out too. That last detail is the whole diagnosis in one observation: an endpoint that does no work cannot be slow because a dependency is slow. It can only be slow because no thread is free to run it.

I took incident command. The first thing I did was stop the scale-out someone had kicked off, because adding pods to a starvation incident gives the same bug more places to happen and destroys the signal you are trying to read. Then I attached dotnet-counters monitor -p <pid> --counters System.Runtime to one pod and watched threadpool-thread-count climb by roughly one per second — 34, 35, 36 — while threadpool-queue-length sat in the thousands. That slow ramp is the thread pool's hill-climbing injection heuristic behaving exactly as designed: above the minimum (the processor count by default, so 4 here) it adds threads cautiously, one or two a second, because a new thread costs a megabyte of stack and is usually the wrong answer. It is the correct policy for a CPU-bound pool and the worst possible policy when every thread is parked in a synchronous wait.

A dotnet-dump collect and then clrstack -all in dotnet-dump analyze showed 33 of 36 threads under the same frame: an internal claims library whose public API was synchronous and which called _cache.GetOrCreateAsync(...).GetAwaiter().GetResult() inside. It had shipped that way two years earlier and had been fine, because the cache answered in under a millisecond and a thread blocked for a millisecond is invisible. That morning the cache had a failover, its latency went to two seconds, and every request thread that entered the claims path blocked for two seconds while the continuation of the async cache call sat in the same pool's queue waiting for a thread that would never come free. That is the entire mechanism of sync-over-async starvation: the blocked thread is waiting on work that needs the thread it is blocking.

// The offending shape: a synchronous public API over an async implementation.
public ClaimsPrincipal GetClaims(string token)
{
    // Blocks a pool thread. The continuation of GetOrCreateAsync also needs
    // a pool thread. Under latency this is deadlock by starvation, not a slow call.
    var entry = _cache.GetOrCreateAsync(token, _ => FetchAsync(token)).GetAwaiter().GetResult();
    return Build(entry);
}

// The fix: async all the way down, with the caller doing the awaiting.
public async ValueTask<ClaimsPrincipal> GetClaimsAsync(string token, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var entry = await _cache.GetOrCreateAsync(token, _ => FetchAsync(token, ct));
    return Build(entry);
}

The immediate mitigation was ugly and correct: I raised ThreadPool.SetMinThreads to 200 on the affected pods via a config flag we already had, which trades memory and context switches for surviving the next ten minutes, and I said out loud in the incident channel that this was a tourniquet and not a fix so nobody would file it as the resolution. Within twenty minutes the p99 was back under 200 ms. The real fix landed over the following two weeks: an async overload on the claims library, callers migrated, and the synchronous method marked [Obsolete] with a Roslyn banned-API rule so it could not come back. What I would do differently: I would not have needed the dump. If we had had a threadpool-queue-length alert — queue depth above 100 for 60 seconds — we would have known it was starvation from the alert text, and the two-year-old blocking call would have been visible in a lint rule long before it was visible in an outage.

(c) Recall prompts

When did a service you owned go slow while its CPU stayed flat?

Flat CPU with rising latency is nearly always a queue: thread pool, connection pool, semaphore, or a channel with a slow consumer. Which one was yours? If you have never had the thread-pool version, the connection-pool version tells the same story and probes the same judgment.

Where in your codebase does a synchronous API sit on top of an async implementation?

Look for .Result, .Wait(), .GetAwaiter().GetResult(), and any Task.Run(...).Result. Legacy authentication, configuration providers, and old caching wrappers are the usual homes. You do not need an outage to make this a story — "I found it before it fired" is a strong prevention story.

What is the ugliest mitigation you have deployed on purpose?

Raising MinThreads, doubling a pool, disabling a feature flag, shedding load. The interesting part is not the hack, it is that you named it as a hack in the channel and tracked the real fix to done.

Have you ever stopped someone from doing the "obvious" remediation during an incident?

Scaling out, restarting, rolling back the wrong deploy. Recall how you said it — commanding is mostly the skill of saying "hold that, here is why" without freezing the room.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does raising MinThreads help at all, if the code is still blocking?

Below the minimum, the pool creates threads on demand with no delay; above it, injection is throttled to roughly one or two threads per second by the hill-climbing heuristic. Raising the minimum removes the throttle, so blocked threads are replaced immediately instead of at one per second, and the queue drains. It does not fix anything: you are paying about a megabyte of stack per thread plus context-switching, and if the blocked call is unbounded you simply starve at a higher number. I treat it as a tourniquet with an expiry date, and I say so in the incident channel so it does not get recorded as the resolution.

What if the blocking call is in a third-party package you cannot change?

Then you isolate it instead of fixing it. The options are a dedicated bulkhead — run those calls on a bounded, separate scheduler or a semaphore-limited queue so they can only ever consume N threads — or move the dependency out of process behind an async API you control. I would take the bulkhead first because it is a day's work and it caps the blast radius, then push the vendor and plan the extraction. The trade-off is that the bulkhead adds a queue, so you have to decide what happens when it is full: reject fast with a 503, or wait and risk the same latency by another route.

At what scale does this stop being survivable?

It depends on the ratio of blocked duration to arrival rate, not on the traffic alone. With a two-second block and 2,000 rps, you need roughly 4,000 concurrent threads to keep up, which is not a thing the pool will ever give you — so the system is unsurvivable at any traffic where blocked-seconds-per-second exceeds the thread count you can afford. That framing is useful in review: I ask people to multiply the expected latency of the blocking call by the request rate, and if the product is bigger than a few hundred, the call must be async.

What did you trade away by fixing it async-all-the-way instead of another route?

Two weeks of a senior engineer's time and a breaking change for every caller of a widely used internal library, including two teams who had to take an unplanned release. The alternative — leaving the sync API and bulkheading it — would have been three days and no coordination cost, but it would have left the trap in place for the next caller. I chose the migration because the library was on the hot path of every request, and I paid for it by writing the analyzer so nobody would have to relitigate it.

How would you prevent this class of bug in an organisation, not just this service?

Three layers, cheapest first. A banned-API analyzer so .Result, .Wait() and GetAwaiter().GetResult() fail the build outside of Main and tests. A standing alert on threadpool-queue-length and on the delta between thread count and processor count, so the shape is named in the alert. And a review norm that a synchronous public API wrapping an async implementation is a design defect, not a convenience. The first two are mechanical and durable; the third is the one that needs a principal engineer to keep saying it.

Would .NET 11 runtime async have changed anything here?

Not the outage. Runtime async changes how suspension is implemented, not what happens when you block a thread — a blocked thread is still a blocked thread. What it would have changed is the diagnosis: with runtime-async=on the live stack traces are the real call chain rather than a pile of AsyncMethodBuilderCore.Start frames, so the dump would have been quicker to read. It is a preview feature in .NET 11, opted in per project, so I would not have had it in production in any case.

021.2 GB a minute on the cache-hit path

Senior Performance problem diagnosed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A pricing service, .NET 10, roughly 9,000 requests per second per pod, spending 11% of wall-clock time in gen0 garbage collection with a collection every 40 milliseconds. Nothing was failing; it was just expensive, and the fleet was 60 pods. I started from allocation rather than CPU because the GC counters told me where to look: dotnet-counters showed gc-heap-size flat but alloc-rate at about 1.2 GB per minute, which is the signature of a lot of short-lived garbage on a hot path, not a leak.

An hour with dotnet-trace collect --profile gc-verbose and then the allocation view in PerfView pointed at a single method: a cache lookup that returned Task<Quote> and hit the in-memory cache 98% of the time. Every one of those hits allocated a Task<Quote> object plus, because the method was async and had an await in the miss branch, the boxed state machine. That is the ValueTask case in its purest form: an async method whose result is usually already available pays a heap allocation per call for a result it did not have to await. At 9,000 rps and about 120 bytes per call pair, that is the 1.2 GB.

// Before: Task<T> allocated on every call, including the 98% cache hits.
public async Task<Quote> GetQuoteAsync(string sym, CancellationToken ct)
{
    if (_cache.TryGetValue(sym, out Quote q)) return q;   // still allocates a Task
    q = await _upstream.FetchAsync(sym, ct);
    _cache.Set(sym, q, TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5));
    return q;
}

// After: the synchronous fast path is allocation-free; the slow path is unchanged.
public ValueTask<Quote> GetQuoteAsync(string sym, CancellationToken ct)
{
    if (_cache.TryGetValue(sym, out Quote q)) return new ValueTask<Quote>(q);
    return new ValueTask<Quote>(SlowAsync(sym, ct));

    async Task<Quote> SlowAsync(string s, CancellationToken t)
    {
        var v = await _upstream.FetchAsync(s, t);
        _cache.Set(s, v, TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5));
        return v;
    }
}

The split into a synchronous outer method plus a local async function matters: if you simply mark the whole method async ValueTask<Quote>, the state machine still gets boxed when it completes asynchronously, and on the synchronous path you save the Task but not the builder work. Keeping the fast path out of the async machinery is what makes it free. Allocation on the hit path went to zero, alloc-rate dropped to about 90 MB per minute, gen0 collections went from every 40 ms to roughly every 600 ms, and p99 improved 14% — which paid for six pods.

The part I would do differently is the rollout. I changed the return type of a public method on a shared library, which is a binary-breaking change even though it compiles, and one downstream service that had not recompiled started throwing MissingMethodException in staging. I should have shipped the ValueTask overload alongside the Task one for a release, then removed the old one. I also learned to be strict about the rule that a ValueTask may be awaited exactly once and never after that: I added an analyzer for it, because the second person to touch that method tried to await the same ValueTask twice in a retry loop, which is undefined behaviour and, with a pooled source behind it, would have handed them somebody else's result.

(c) Recall prompts

What is the last thing you optimised where you had a measurement before you had an opinion?

The measurement is the story. If you cannot name the tool and the counter, pick a different example — interviewers at this level probe the diagnosis harder than the fix.

Where in your code does an async method usually complete without ever awaiting anything?

Cache lookups, buffered stream reads, feature-flag checks, validation that only sometimes calls out. These are the natural ValueTask sites; they are also the sites where the "await once" rule gets broken.

Have you ever made a change that was source-compatible but binary-breaking?

Return type changes, adding a parameter with a default, changing a struct's layout. This is a good "what I would do differently" even when the optimisation itself went well.

When did you decide not to optimise something?

Saying no to a micro-optimisation on a cold path — with the measurement that showed it was cold — is a stronger senior signal than the optimisation itself.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just make every method return ValueTask?

Because you would be trading a cheap allocation for a class of correctness bugs. A ValueTask can be awaited only once, must not be awaited concurrently, cannot be stored and re-read, and needs AsTask() before it can go into WhenAll — and AsTask() allocates, so on any path that combines tasks you pay anyway. It is also a two-word struct, so passing it around is more copying than passing a reference. My rule is: Task by default on public APIs, ValueTask where a measurement shows a hot path that usually completes synchronously.

What if the same hot path also completes asynchronously often — say 50/50?

Then plain ValueTask buys you very little, because the async path still allocates. That is where IValueTaskSource comes in: you implement the source yourself and pool the state, which is what Socket and the Channels readers do internally, or you enable PoolingAsyncValueTaskMethodBuilder on the method. Both are real complexity — you now own object lifetime and the token dance — so I would only do it in a library on a measured critical path, never in application code.

At what scale does this optimisation stop mattering?

Below a few thousand calls per second it is noise; gen0 collections are cheap and the allocations die in the nursery. It starts to matter when the allocation rate pushes gen0 collections into the tens per second, or when the objects survive long enough to be promoted, because gen1 and gen2 are where the real cost is. I would rather spend the change budget on the promotion problem than on gen0 churn, unless the profile shows the churn is what is stalling the thread.

What did you trade away?

API ergonomics and a bit of safety. Callers can no longer casually cache the returned task, and I had to write down a rule the team did not previously need. I also spent a week on a rollback and a staged re-release after the binary-break, which is a week I did not spend on the upstream latency that was the actual reason for the cache.

How would you have caught the double-await in review rather than in production?

Analyzers, and a habit. CA2012 flags a ValueTask that is not awaited directly, which catches most of the storage cases, and I turned it into an error rather than a warning in that repository. The habit is that any method returning ValueTask gets a one-line XML comment saying "await exactly once", so the rule shows up in IntelliSense at the call site rather than in a wiki nobody reads.

03One library, three hosts: the ConfigureAwait policy

Staff Hard architectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We owned a domain library — about 40,000 lines, roughly 900 await sites — consumed by three hosts: an ASP.NET Core API, a WPF desktop client, and a batch worker running as a console app. The desktop team kept filing intermittent hangs. The API team kept filing "why is this library slower than it should be". Both were right, and both were downstream of one decision nobody had ever made deliberately: what the library does with the synchronization context.

The mechanism matters here and it is the thing interviewers drill. By default, await captures the current SynchronizationContext (or the current TaskScheduler if there is no context) and posts the continuation back to it. ASP.NET Core has no SynchronizationContext, so on the API the capture is a null check and the continuation runs on whatever pool thread finishes the I/O. WPF has one, pinned to the UI thread, so every continuation in our library was being marshalled back to the UI thread — which is both slow, because it queues a message per await, and lethal if anything up the stack blocks that thread. The desktop hangs were classic single-threaded-context deadlocks: UI thread blocks on a task, the task's continuation needs the UI thread, nobody wins.

The debate was whether to put ConfigureAwait(false) on all 900 awaits. It is the textbook answer and I did not like it: it is 900 lines of noise, it is trivially forgotten on the 901st await, and it hides the actual design question. I made a call that the library is context-agnostic — it is domain logic, it never touches UI, it never reads HttpContext — and therefore it should never capture. We enforced that mechanically rather than by discipline: the ConfigureAwait.Fody-style approach was one option, but we went with the analyzer route, turning CA2007 on as an error in that project only, so a missing ConfigureAwait(false) fails the build. Application projects kept it off, because in an app you often want the context.

// Library code: context-agnostic, enforced by CA2007-as-error in this project only.
public async Task<Invoice> RenderAsync(Guid id, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var order = await _orders.GetAsync(id, ct).ConfigureAwait(false);
    var rates = await _fx.GetRatesAsync(order.Currency, ct).ConfigureAwait(false);
    return Compose(order, rates);
}

// .NET 8+ gives a richer switch than the boolean. Suppressing the throw on a
// cancelled task is occasionally what you want in cleanup paths:
await stream.WriteAsync(buffer, ct)
    .ConfigureAwait(ConfigureAwaitOptions.SuppressThrowing | ConfigureAwaitOptions.ForceYielding);

The result was that the desktop hangs stopped completely, and a benchmark of the batch path improved about 6% because we were no longer paying for context checks in a tight loop. The cost I accepted: 900 diff lines, a noisier codebase, and a rule that new engineers find annoying for their first month. What I would do differently is the communication. I announced the decision in an architecture note and assumed it was settled; the desktop team read it as "the library team is making our problem our problem". I should have paired with one of their engineers on the first fifty conversions, so the decision arrived as a joint fix rather than a memo. The technical call was right; the rollout cost me three weeks of goodwill I did not need to spend.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

ASP.NET Core has no SynchronizationContext, so why bother in the library at all?

Because the library does not get to choose its host. The API is one of three consumers today and there will be a fourth — a Blazor Server front end, a MAUI client, a test host with its own context — and each of those brings a context back. There is also a second capture nobody thinks about: if there is no SynchronizationContext, await falls back to the current TaskScheduler, so code running under a custom scheduler or inside a Parallel body still gets a non-default capture. ConfigureAwait(false) opts out of both.

Why not use a post-build weaver and keep the source clean?

I considered it and rejected it on debuggability. A weaver means the IL does not match the source, so a stack trace, a decompiled assembly and a source-linked debug session tell three slightly different stories, and the next person to debug an async hang has to know the weaver exists. The analyzer route costs 900 visible lines but every one of them is honest. If the library had been 400,000 lines rather than 40,000 I might have made the opposite call and paid for it with documentation.

What breaks if a caller depends on the continuation running on their context?

Then they have a bug in their code, not in mine — a library must not promise thread affinity it never documented. Concretely, a WPF caller who did await lib.RenderAsync(); label.Text = … was previously getting away with touching the UI from a continuation that happened to be marshalled back. After the change they get an InvalidOperationException on the wrong thread. We found four of those before release with a targeted search, and the fix is one Dispatcher.Invoke at the boundary, which is where it belonged.

At what scale does the per-await capture cost actually show up?

Not at web scale on Kestrel — it is a null check, and you will never see it. It shows up in two places: a UI context, where each capture is a real message post and you can watch a tight loop of awaits stutter the frame rate, and in very hot library loops where the extra branch and the ExecutionContext work per await add up. That second one shrank in .NET 11: continuations now skip the ExecutionContext capture and restore entirely when there is nothing to restore, so code that uses ConfigureAwait(false) and few AsyncLocal<T> values gets that overhead back for free.

How would you brief a team taking over this library?

One page: the library is context-agnostic by design, the analyzer is the contract, and here is the mechanism in five sentences so you can defend it when someone asks. I would explicitly tell them the rule they are allowed to break — application-level code and Main — because a rule with no stated boundary gets applied everywhere and then resented. And I would hand them the four call sites we fixed at the boundary as the worked example of what the rule pushes onto callers.

04The deadlock that only happened in the desktop shell

Mid Nasty debugging session

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A WPF admin tool froze when you clicked Export — not slowly, completely: the window stopped painting, Windows greyed it out, and you had to kill it. It never reproduced in the unit tests, never in the integration tests, and never when I ran the same code path from a console harness. That asymmetry was the clue I sat on for two days before I understood it.

The chain was three methods long. The click handler was synchronous and called a service method that returned a Task, and because someone had wanted to "keep the handler simple" it ended in .Result. That service method awaited an HTTP call. On a UI thread there is a SynchronizationContext with exactly one thread behind it. The await captured it. The HTTP call completed on a pool thread, the continuation was posted to the UI context's queue, and the UI thread was sitting inside .Result waiting for the task to finish. The continuation could not run until the UI thread was free; the UI thread could not become free until the continuation ran. That is the whole deadlock, and it is not a race — it is deterministic, which is why it happened every single time in the app and never in a test host with no context.

// The freeze, in three lines.
private void OnExport(object s, RoutedEventArgs e)
{
    var csv = _export.BuildAsync().Result;   // UI thread blocks here...
    File.WriteAllText(path, csv);
}

private async Task<string> BuildAsync()
{
    var rows = await _api.GetRowsAsync();    // ...continuation is posted to the UI thread
    return Render(rows);                      //    which is blocked in .Result. Deadlock.
}

// The fix: let the handler be async, and never block the context.
private async void OnExport(object s, RoutedEventArgs e)   // async void is correct for
{                                                          // an event handler, and only there
    try { await File.WriteAllTextAsync(path, await _export.BuildAsync()); }
    catch (Exception ex) { ShowError(ex); }   // async void needs its own catch
}

What actually broke the two days loose was not cleverness, it was changing the question. Instead of "why does it hang" I asked "what is different between the app and the harness", wrote both differences on the whiteboard — a UI thread, and a synchronization context — and then attached the debugger, froze the process, and looked at the Parallel Stacks window: the UI thread was in Task.Result under OnExport, and there were no other managed frames doing anything. A blocked main thread and no runnable work is the fingerprint of a context deadlock, not of a slow server.

Two things I would do differently. First, I would have reached for the debugger's Parallel Stacks and the Tasks window on hour one instead of on day two; the answer was visible in fifteen seconds once I looked in the right window. Second, I fixed only that handler at first, and three weeks later a colleague filed the identical bug on a different button. The real fix was the analyzer rule that made .Result and .Wait() build errors in that project, plus the note in the code review checklist that an event handler is the one place async void is correct — and that it must contain its own try/catch, because an exception from an async void method is raised on the captured context and takes the process down rather than surfacing as a faulted task.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the same code not deadlock in ASP.NET Core?

Because ASP.NET Core has no SynchronizationContext. The continuation goes to the thread pool instead of being posted back to a single thread, so a blocked request thread does not prevent it from running. You do not get a deadlock — you get thread-pool starvation, which is worse in a way, because it is load-dependent and therefore intermittent rather than deterministic. Same root cause, completely different failure mode, and that is why "it works on the server" is not evidence that blocking is safe.

Is async void ever correct?

Yes, in exactly one place: an event handler with a signature you do not control. Everywhere else it is a bug, because the method returns to the caller at the first await with no task to observe, so the caller cannot await it, cannot know when it finished, and cannot catch its exceptions — an unhandled exception in an async void method is re-raised on the captured context and usually kills the process. When I use it in a handler I put the whole body in a try/catch and delegate immediately to an async Task method, so the async void surface is one line long.

What if you genuinely cannot make the caller async — a constructor, or an interface you do not own?

Then you change the shape rather than blocking. For a constructor, use an async factory method and make the constructor private. For an interface you do not own, do the async work outside and pass the result in, or accept that you are blocking and isolate it: run it on a dedicated thread, not a pool thread, so you are not consuming the pool. The one thing I will not do is Task.Run(() => X()).GetAwaiter().GetResult() and call it safe — it dodges the UI deadlock but it still consumes two threads and it hides the design problem.

At what scale does the ASP.NET Core version of this become an outage?

It scales with blocked-seconds per second. If the blocked call takes 50 ms and you do 100 rps you need five threads and nobody notices; at 2,000 rps with a 2-second call you need 4,000 and the pool never gets there. So the bug is invisible until the dependency slows down, which means the incident always coincides with someone else's degradation and gets misattributed to them. That is the argument I use to get blocking calls banned rather than merely discouraged.

How do you teach a mid-level engineer to see this before it ships?

Not with the rule — the rule is a one-liner and they already know it. I get them to draw the two threads and the queue on a whiteboard, then ask them who runs the continuation. Once someone has drawn the arrow from the pool thread to the UI queue and back, they never write .Result in a handler again. The rule tells them what to do; the drawing tells them why, and the why is what transfers to the next situation, like a Lock held across an await or a custom scheduler.

The cause is a good thing that arrives as a breaking change. Why did Microsoft put LINQ for IAsyncEnumerable in the box if a good package existed?

Because the type is in the box and the operators were not, which meant every consumer of an async stream took a third-party dependency to do anything with it — awkward for the framework's own libraries, and a supply-chain and versioning cost for everyone else. Default to LTS for anything with an operations cost — three years of support means one upgrade every two to three years rather than one a year. A week of feature work, and we took the risk of being early on an LTS rather than letting others find the sharp edges. I would make the same trade again; the thing I would not do again is start it in the same sprint as a release.

06"Just wrap it in Task.Run"

Senior Technical disagreement with a colleague

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A pull request from a capable engineer I liked working with. An endpoint was taking 400 ms because it made a slow call to an internal service, and their change was to wrap the whole handler body in Task.Run. Their argument, in the PR: "this makes it asynchronous so the request thread is freed". It is a reasonable-sounding sentence and it is wrong, and the disagreement got sharp because I first replied with the rule — "don't use Task.Run for I/O" — which is exactly the reply that makes a good engineer dig in.

Task.Run does not make anything asynchronous. It moves work to a thread-pool thread. If the work is I/O and already asynchronous, you have added a hop and consumed a second thread while the first one awaits; if the work is synchronous and blocking, you have moved the block from a request thread to a pool thread, which is the same pool ASP.NET Core draws request threads from, so you have moved the problem two feet to the left and made it harder to see. The only case where it earns its keep on a server is genuinely CPU-bound work you want off the current thread — and even then, on a web server, you usually just want to do it inline, because you have no UI to keep responsive and every thread is fungible.

I rewrote my review comment. Instead of the rule I asked a question: "How many threads are involved in this request before and after your change, and which one is blocked while the internal service is thinking?" That reframed it from "whose opinion wins" to "let's count", and we counted together on a call. Before: one thread, blocked for 400 ms. After: two threads, one blocked for 400 ms and one awaiting. Then we looked at the actual internal call and found it was a synchronous HTTP client method — the real fix was to use the async client method and thread the token through, which took the handler to zero blocked threads.

// Their change: adds a thread, blocks the same total time.
public async Task<IResult> Handle(Query q) =>
    await Task.Run(() => Results.Ok(_client.GetReport(q.Id)));   // GetReport blocks a pool thread

// The fix: no thread is blocked while the network is thinking.
public async Task<IResult> Handle(Query q, CancellationToken ct) =>
    Results.Ok(await _client.GetReportAsync(q.Id, ct));

Result: the endpoint went from 400 ms with a blocked thread to 400 ms with no blocked thread, which sounds like nothing until you look at the load test — throughput at the same pod count went up about 3.5x before the latency knee, because we stopped consuming a pool thread per in-flight request. The colleague wrote the follow-up guidance in our engineering handbook, which mattered more than the fix: it stopped being my rule and became our shared understanding.

What I would do differently: I would skip the first comment entirely. Leading with a rule in a written channel is the least effective way to change a good engineer's mind, because it invites them to defend their reasoning rather than examine it. The counting question would have worked on comment one and saved us a day of friction.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Is Task.Run ever right on a server?

Yes, in two situations. One is genuinely CPU-bound work you want to overlap with I/O — computing two independent things at once, where the parallelism is real. By looking at the test. A single-request local test cannot show thread consumption, because one thread out of many is free. Staff Scaling challenge

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A nightly reconciliation job that had been fine at 4,000 accounts started failing at 60,000. The code was the natural thing to write: project the accounts to tasks, await Task.WhenAll. At 4,000 that is merely rude; at 60,000 it produced a pod that allocated 60,000 state machines up front, opened as many database connections as the pool would give it, and then spent forty minutes throwing InvalidOperationException: Timeout expired. The timeout period elapsed prior to obtaining a connection from the pool for most of them.

The thing worth understanding — and the thing interviewers dig into — is that WhenAll is not a concurrency control. It is a join. The concurrency was decided the moment the Select materialised, because an async method starts running when you call it, not when you await it. By the time WhenAll sees the array, all 60,000 operations are already in flight and racing for a pool of 100 connections. The database was not the bottleneck; our unbounded fan-out was, and the database was the first place it showed.

// The shape that fails: concurrency is whatever the collection size happens to be.
var results = await Task.WhenAll(accounts.Select(a => ReconcileAsync(a, ct)));

// The shape that scales: concurrency is a decision, made once, with a number behind it.
await Parallel.ForEachAsync(
    accounts,
    new ParallelOptions { MaxDegreeOfParallelism = 64, CancellationToken = ct },
    async (a, token) => await ReconcileAsync(a, token));

// When the source is itself a stream and you want back-pressure end to end:
await foreach (var batch in ReadAccountsAsync(ct).Chunk(500).WithCancellation(ct))
    await Parallel.ForEachAsync(batch, opts, ReconcileAsync);

I set the degree of parallelism to 64 rather than to a round number by measuring: I ran the job at 16, 32, 64, 128 and 256 against a copy of the production database and plotted completion time against pool wait time. Throughput climbed to about 64 and then went flat, and past 128 it got worse because connection acquisition started queueing — the classic universal scalability curve, where added concurrency buys less and less until coherence costs make it negative. 64 sat just under the knee with headroom for the other services sharing that database. I wrote that number in a comment with the date and the measurement, because a magic constant with no provenance is the next person's mystery.

Runtime went from "fails after forty minutes" to eleven minutes, memory peak dropped from 2.1 GB to 190 MB, and the database's p99 for other tenants stopped spiking at 2 a.m. What I would do differently: I fixed this job, and six months later the same pattern bit us in a different service. I should have treated Task.WhenAll over an unbounded collection as an organisational smell immediately — a Roslyn analyzer that flags WhenAll(collection.Select(...)) and asks for an explicit degree of parallelism would have cost a day and caught three more sites.

Why not just increase the connection pool to 60,000?

Because the pool is protecting the database, not limiting you. Each connection is a server-side session with memory and a worker; databases have a concurrency sweet spot in the low hundreds and get slower past it, so raising the pool converts a fast failure at the client into a slow collapse at the server, plus you would be stealing capacity from every other service on that instance. The right question is not "how do I get more connections" but "what is the highest concurrency at which total throughput still increases", and that is a measurement.

When would SemaphoreSlim be the better tool than Parallel.ForEachAsync?

When the concurrency limit needs to span more than one loop — a shared limit across several call sites, or a limit on a resource rather than on an iteration. Parallel.ForEachAsync bounds one loop; a semaphore held in a service can bound everything that touches a particular downstream. The trap with the semaphore-plus-WhenAll pattern is that it throttles execution but not creation: you still allocate every task and every closure up front, so it fixes the connection pressure and not the memory. If memory is the problem, you need the source to be pulled, not pushed.

At what scale does even the bounded version break?

When the work no longer fits in one process's runtime window — for us, somewhere past a few million accounts, where eleven minutes becomes eleven hours and a single pod restart loses all of it. At that point the answer is not more concurrency, it is partitioning and checkpointing: shard the accounts across N workers, make each unit idempotent, and record progress so a restart resumes rather than repeats. I would also expect the database to stop being the constraint and the upstream API's rate limit to take over, which is a different bound with a different fix.

Best-case wall-clock. At 128 the job would sometimes finish two minutes sooner on an idle database. I gave that up deliberately for predictability and for the neighbours, and I documented the trade so that the next person who wants it faster knows what they are spending. If the job ever became time-critical I would rather add a second worker on a read replica than push the number up on the primary.

08The cancellation that never reached the database

Senior Security issue found and fixed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We had a search endpoint with an expensive query behind it. A penetration test filed it as a denial-of-service finding, and the finding was accurate in a way that surprised the team: a client could open a request, wait 50 ms, abort the connection, and repeat. The cause was mundane and extremely common. ASP.NET Core gives you HttpContext.RequestAborted, and minimal APIs and controllers will bind a CancellationToken parameter to it for free. Our handler took the token, logged it, and then called into a repository whose method signature had no token at all — so at the first layer boundary the cancellation stopped. Below that, EF Core happily executed the query to completion and the result was thrown away by a caller who had already gone. The token existed, was correct, and was dropped one method call in. That is the failure mode: cancellation is not a runtime feature, it is a parameter you have to keep passing, and it silently degrades to nothing at the first method that forgets.

// The hole: the token is accepted and then not passed on.
public async Task<IResult> Search(SearchQuery q, CancellationToken ct)
{
    _log.LogInformation("search {Q}", q.Text);
    var hits = await _repo.SearchAsync(q);     // no token: runs to completion regardless
    return Results.Ok(hits);
}

// The fix: token to the leaf, plus a server-side budget the client cannot extend.
public async Task<IResult> Search(SearchQuery q, CancellationToken ct)
{
    using var budget = CancellationTokenSource.CreateLinkedTokenSource(ct);
    budget.CancelAfter(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5));          // our limit, not theirs
    var hits = await _repo.SearchAsync(q, budget.Token);  // reaches the DbCommand
    return Results.Ok(hits);
}

I audited every public entry point with a Roslyn analyzer rather than by reading: a rule that flags a method with a CancellationToken parameter that calls another token-accepting overload without passing it. That found 47 sites in a week, of which about a dozen were on paths expensive enough to matter. The two structural changes were more valuable than the 47 fixes. First, every repository method got a required (non-defaulted) token parameter — a default of default is how these holes get created, because forgetting compiles. Second, we added the linked-token budget at the edge so the server always owns an upper bound on work per request, whether or not the client sticks around.

Verification mattered: I reproduced the attack with a small script that opened and aborted 200 connections a second, watched sys.dm_exec_requests before and after, and confirmed queries were now being cancelled at the database rather than completing. Before: 1,900 concurrent server-side queries after a minute. After: never more than about 90.

What I would do differently: I initially framed it to the team as a performance issue, and it got prioritised like one — behind two features. It moved the same day I re-framed it as "an unauthenticated client can take down the database from a laptop, and here is the recording". That was not spin; it was the accurate framing, and I had been the one under-selling it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is a server-side budget necessary if you already forward the client's token?

Because the client's token is the client's decision, and I do not want an anonymous caller deciding how long my database works. A linked token source gives me both: cancel when the client leaves or when my own budget expires, whichever comes first. It also protects the other direction — a client that never disconnects and a dependency that never answers would otherwise hold the request open indefinitely. The trade-off is that a legitimate slow query now fails at five seconds, so the budget has to be chosen from the latency distribution, not invented.

What is wrong with CancellationToken ct = default on internal APIs?

It makes the unsafe call compile. Cancellation holes are always omissions, never wrong values, so anything that lets an omission pass silently is the actual defect. Making the parameter required turns a runtime availability risk into a compile error, which is the trade I want. I keep the default on genuinely public library APIs where source compatibility matters, and even there I pair it with the analyzer that flags a caller who has a token in scope and does not pass it.

What must you not cancel?

Anything whose partial completion leaves state inconsistent, and anything in a cleanup path. Concretely: do not pass the request token to the write that finalises a transaction, and never pass an already-cancelled token to the disposal or compensation call in your finally — that is how you get a half-written record plus a skipped rollback. For the second case .NET 8 added ConfigureAwaitOptions.SuppressThrowing, which lets you await a cleanup operation without it throwing on an already-cancelled token. The general rule is that cancellation is safe at operation boundaries and dangerous in the middle of one.

At what scale does the linked-token approach itself become a cost?

Each CreateLinkedTokenSource allocates and registers a callback on the parent, and registrations on a long-lived token accumulate if you forget to dispose. On a hot path at tens of thousands of requests per second that shows up in allocation profiles, and a leaked registration on an application-lifetime token is a genuine memory leak I have seen in the wild. The mitigations are to dispose with using — which is why the example does — and to prefer a single budget at the edge over one per layer.

How do you know cancellation actually reached the database?

You check on the database, not in your code. For SQL Server I query sys.dm_exec_requests during the reproduction and count sessions running the query; for PostgreSQL, pg_stat_activity. Proper cancellation shows up as the query disappearing within milliseconds of the abort, and as an OperationCanceledException in the application log rather than a completed query with a discarded result. If the count keeps climbing, something in the chain is still swallowing the token — usually a retry policy that creates its own uncancelled context.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is async "viral", and what does that mean for planning?

Because there is no correct way to await a task from a synchronous method: your options are to block, which is the bug you are removing, or to make the caller async too. So converting a leaf pushes the change up through every caller to an entry point that can be asynchronous — a controller action, a hosted service, Main. For planning this means the unit of work is a vertical slice, and the cost is driven by the fan-in of the leaf. A leaf with 200 callers is a quarter's work; a leaf with three is an afternoon. I estimate by counting callers, not lines.

Is blocking ever acceptable in the final state?

Yes, in three places. Main and application start-up, where there is nobody to await you and the thread pool is idle. Console tools and tests, where the process exists to do one thing. And a genuinely synchronous entry point you do not control, such as an old plugin interface — and there I would isolate it on a dedicated thread rather than a pool thread. What is never acceptable is blocking on a request path or inside a library, because in both cases you are spending a resource that belongs to someone else.

How did you keep a fourteen-month effort alive through re-prioritisation?

By making it small enough that it never needed defending. One slice per sprint is a fifth of one engineer, which no planning meeting fights about, and each slice shipped a visible improvement so it kept earning its place. The moment a paydown becomes a project with a Gantt chart it becomes cancellable; keeping it as a standing tax kept it safe.

Fourteen months of a slow drip instead of a fast fix, which meant we lived with the risk on those 180 sites for longer than a big-bang would have. I accepted that because a big-bang merge across eleven years of code has a realistic chance of a multi-day outage, and because the ratchet had already capped the downside. Three measures agreed in advance: starvation-shaped incidents per year, the count of blocking calls on request paths, and the p99 of the two services that had been worst affected. I care most about the first, because it is the outcome and the others are proxies. 10Teaching the state machine, not the rule

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A mid-level engineer joined the team, strong in C# generally, and kept producing async code that was subtly wrong in a different way each time: a Task stored in a field and awaited twice, an async void helper, a foreach that awaited sequentially where the calls were independent, a lock that could not compile once the body needed an await and was replaced with a SemaphoreSlim that was never released on the exception path. I gave rule-shaped review feedback for three weeks and the error rate did not fall — it just moved. That is the signal that you are teaching symptoms.

So I stopped reviewing and spent an hour at a whiteboard. I wrote one small async method and asked them to hand-compile it: what does the compiler generate, where does the method actually return, who holds the state, who calls MoveNext. Once they had drawn the state machine struct with the state field and the awaiter, every one of their bugs became a consequence they could derive rather than a rule they had to remember. A Task is a handle to a completed or pending result, so awaiting it twice is fine and it does not re-run — but a ValueTask may be backed by a pooled source, so awaiting it twice is undefined. async void has no builder to put the result in, so nobody can observe it. Sequential awaits in a loop are sequential because await means "suspend until this finishes", and if you want overlap you have to start the operations first.

// The exercise: what does the compiler actually do with this?
async Task<int> SumAsync(HttpClient c)
{
    var a = await GetAsync(c, "/a");   // suspension point 1: state = 0
    var b = await GetAsync(c, "/b");   // suspension point 2: state = 1
    return a + b;                       // builder.SetResult(a + b)
}

// Once you can see the two suspension points, this becomes obvious rather than a rule:
async Task<int> SumOverlappedAsync(HttpClient c)
{
    var ta = GetAsync(c, "/a");        // started, not awaited
    var tb = GetAsync(c, "/b");        // started, not awaited
    var r = await Task.WhenAll(ta, tb); // one suspension point, two operations in flight
    return r[0] + r[1];
}

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Explain the state machine in ninety seconds, as you would to the mentee.

The compiler rewrites the method body into a struct with a state field, one field per local that survives an await, and a MoveNext method containing your code split into segments at the awaits. Calling the method runs the first segment synchronously up to the first incomplete await; at that point the struct is boxed to the heap, a continuation is hooked onto the awaited task, and the method returns a Task to the caller — it has returned, not blocked. When the awaited operation completes, whatever completes it calls the continuation, which calls MoveNext again, which jumps to the next segment via the state field. The result or exception goes into the builder, which completes the task the caller is holding.

How is that different under .NET 11 runtime async?

The observable semantics are the same; the implementation moves from the compiler to the runtime. Instead of a compiler-generated state-machine struct, the runtime itself suspends and resumes the method, which is why the runtime libraries in .NET 11 ship with no compiler-generated state machines at all. Practically it means cleaner live stack traces — the docs' example goes from thirteen frames to five, because the AsyncMethodBuilderCore.Start scaffolding is gone — better breakpoint and step behaviour across awaits, and lower overhead from cached continuations and tiered compilation of async bodies. It is preview in .NET 11 and opted in with <Features>runtime-async=on</Features>, so it is a thing to understand, not a thing to depend on.

How do you know they actually learned it rather than pattern-matched your feedback?

By asking them to apply it somewhere I did not teach. My check was to hand them a bug in a part of the system neither of us had touched — a Channel consumer that was silently dropping work — and see whether they reached for the mechanism. They did: they asked who was running the consumer loop and what happened to its exception. Watching the next PR is a much weaker check, because a PR in the same area rewards memorisation.

What would you do if the whiteboard session had not worked either?

Change the modality again, and then be honest about fit. Some people learn from writing rather than drawing, so I would have had them write the guidance page for the team. If two genuinely different approaches over a couple of months do not move it, the conversation stops being about async and becomes about whether this role, on this system, is the right one — and that is a manager conversation I would start early rather than let it drift into a performance surprise.

How does this scale past one person?

It does not, directly — an hour of whiteboard per engineer is not a strategy for forty people. What scales is the artefact and the norm. The ninety-second explanation became a page in our onboarding with the same exercise, the "where does this suspend" question became a line in the review checklist, and the brown-bag was recorded. That gets most people most of the way; the individual session is what you spend on the ones the artefact does not reach.

11Graceful shutdown before the 30-second deadline

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We were moving a payment-adjacent worker onto a platform where pods were routinely rescheduled — spot capacity, node upgrades, autoscaling — which meant a SIGTERM followed by a hard kill 30 seconds later, several times a day rather than at deploys only. Our worker processed jobs that took between 2 and 400 seconds. The product ask was "make shutdown graceful" with two weeks before the platform migration date. The honest answer was that "graceful" for a 400-second job inside a 30-second window is not achievable, and the negotiation was the work.

I split the ask into three things that had been travelling as one. Stop accepting: on SIGTERM, immediately stop pulling new messages — an hour of work, and it removes most of the problem because most jobs are short. Finish what is in flight: possible for anything under about 25 seconds, which was 94% of jobs by count. Never lose work: achievable for 100% of jobs, but only by making them resumable, which is a data-model change and a month of work. I took the first two into the two weeks and made the third a separate, funded piece of work, with the explicit statement that until it landed, 6% of jobs would be killed mid-flight and reprocessed from the start.

The implementation is standard .NET hosting once you know the pieces, and the piece people miss is that ExecuteAsync's token and StopAsync's token are different things: the first is signalled when shutdown begins, the second carries the host's shutdown timeout. Both matter, and the host's ShutdownTimeout has to be set below the platform's kill deadline or the runtime never gets to run your cleanup at all.

builder.Services.Configure<HostOptions>(o =>
    o.ShutdownTimeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(25));   // below the platform's 30s SIGKILL

protected override async Task ExecuteAsync(CancellationToken stopping)
{
    await foreach (var msg in _queue.ReadAllAsync(stopping))   // stops pulling on SIGTERM
    {
        using var job = CancellationTokenSource.CreateLinkedTokenSource(stopping);
        try { await ProcessAsync(msg, job.Token); await msg.CompleteAsync(); }
        catch (OperationCanceledException) when (stopping.IsCancellationRequested)
        {
            await msg.AbandonAsync(CancellationToken.None);   // fresh token: cleanup must run
            break;
        }
    }
}

We shipped on time with 94% of jobs finishing cleanly and 100% of the remainder abandoned back to the queue rather than lost, because the messages were only completed after success. The resumability work landed seven weeks later and took the mid-flight kills to zero. (d) Follow-up drill chain

What is the difference between the token in ExecuteAsync and the one in StopAsync?

ExecuteAsync's token is signalled the moment shutdown starts — it is your cue to stop taking new work and start winding down. StopAsync's token is bounded by the host's ShutdownTimeout, and when it fires the host stops waiting for you and continues with disposal. So the first says "begin" and the second says "you are out of time". The mistake I see most is code that passes the stopping token into its cleanup calls, so the cleanup is cancelled at exactly the moment it is needed; cleanup should get a fresh token or CancellationToken.None with its own short timeout.

Why not just make ShutdownTimeout longer than 30 seconds?

Because the platform is not asking. After 30 seconds it sends SIGKILL and the process ends mid-instruction with no cleanup at all, so a longer host timeout guarantees the worst outcome rather than a better one. The host timeout must be strictly inside the platform's deadline, with margin for the runtime's own shutdown work — I used 25 against 30. If we genuinely needed longer we would have to negotiate the platform's terminationGracePeriodSeconds, which is a different conversation with a different team, and it does not help with spot preemption where the notice is fixed.

How do you make a job resumable without a rewrite?

Checkpointing at natural boundaries, plus idempotency at the sink. Concretely: split the 400-second job into steps, persist "completed step N for job X" transactionally with the step's effect, and on restart skip to N+1. That does not need exactly-once delivery — it needs each step to be safe to repeat, which for us meant a natural key on the write so a repeat became an upsert. The reason it took a month is that two of our steps called an external API that was not idempotent, and we had to add a request-id and a dedupe table on our side.

At what point does 6% become unacceptable?

When the reprocessing is not free — either because it is visible to a customer, or because the volume times the cost of a repeat exceeds the cost of fixing it. At our volume, 6% of a few thousand jobs a day, all idempotent and invisible, was cheap for seven weeks. If those had been payment captures rather than adjustments, or if the pod churn had been hourly rather than a few times a day, I would have argued to delay the platform migration instead — the correct answer would have been to move the deadline, not the scope.

What would you have done if the product owner had refused the trade?

If the answer had been "all of it, on the date", my job is to state the consequence in their terms — jobs lost, not "technical risk" — and escalate once, calmly, with the same measured numbers. What I would not do is agree and then quietly deliver something that looks graceful and is not, because the failure would arrive later and with less context.

What actually happens to an exception in an un-awaited Task?

It is captured on the task and, if nothing ever observes it, it becomes an unobserved task exception. So the failure is invisible twice over: no crash, no log. You move it out of the request rather than orphaning it inside one. Why is DbContext not thread-safe, and what would the "correct" concurrent version cost?

It is a unit of work with a change tracker, an identity map and a single connection; making it concurrent would mean locking around every entity operation and either multiplexing or pooling connections underneath, which would slow down the 99% case to support a case the pattern does not want. EF Core makes the choice explicit by detecting concurrent use and throwing, which is the right design: a fast, clear failure beats silent corruption. The practical rule is one DbContext per unit of work, per scope, and if you need concurrency you need more contexts, not a shared one.

It gets worse with connection reuse rate and with tenant density, not with data volume. More requests per second means a shorter window between a connection being returned mid-operation and being handed to someone else, so the race widens; more tenants per pool means the neighbour is more likely to be a different customer. It also gets much worse if you add read replicas or a proxy that multiplexes, because the corrupted state can now propagate to a place your point-in-time restore does not cover. That is why I would not treat "small system, low traffic" as a reason to leave the pattern in place.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is jitter necessary on the retry?

Because without it every client that failed at the same moment retries at the same moment, and you convert one upstream blip into a synchronised thundering herd that keeps it down. Jitter spreads the retries across the backoff window so the upstream sees a smooth ramp instead of a spike. The failure this prevents is specifically a recovery failure: the upstream comes back, is immediately hit by every client at once, and falls over again — which looks like a flapping dependency and is actually us.

What happens if two layers each retry twice?

You get up to nine attempts, not four, because the counts multiply rather than add — and the outer layer's timeout has to be longer than the inner layer's total, so the user's latency multiplies too. This is why I insist retries live at exactly one layer, as close to the call as possible, and that everything above it only times out. When I found the second retry loop in this system, deleting it was what produced the 12% drop in upstream calls; the resilience improved and the load went down at the same time, which is a nice thing to be able to say.

At what scale does the cached fallback become the wrong answer?

When staleness has a cost that grows with the number of users seeing it, or when the thing being cached is per-user rather than shared. For a price feed shared by everyone, a 30-second-old value served to 10,000 users is one decision made 10,000 times, and it is fine if it is labelled. For a per-user balance, the cache hit rate collapses and you are mostly failing anyway, so the fallback adds complexity for nothing. Tail latency. Hedging would have taken the p99 lower still, because on the slowest 5% of calls you would be racing two attempts instead of waiting for one to time out. Our system was async top to bottom. The naive bridge — a TaskCompletionSource per request — worked in testing and then produced a bizarre production symptom: their entire callback dispatch would stall, and every subscription in the process went quiet at once.

The cause is a trap worth knowing. A TaskCompletionSource completes synchronously by default, which means that when the vendor's thread calls tcs.SetResult(...), the continuation of whoever was awaiting that task runs on the vendor's callback thread, inline. Our continuation went on to do more awaiting work, so we were effectively borrowing the vendor's dispatch thread for our business logic — and they had exactly two of them for the whole process. One slow continuation of ours stalled every subscription. The fix is one constructor flag, TaskCreationOptions.RunContinuationsAsynchronously, which makes the completion queue the continuation to the thread pool instead of running it inline.

// The bridge, with the flag that stops us stealing the vendor's threads.
public Task<Quote> RequestAsync(string sym, CancellationToken ct)
{

    var reg = ct.Register(static s => ((TaskCompletionSource<Quote>)s!).TrySetCanceled(), tcs);
    _sdk.Subscribe(sym,
        onData:  q  => { tcs.TrySetResult(q); reg.Dispose(); },
        onError: ex => { tcs.TrySetException(ex); reg.Dispose(); });
    return tcs.Task;
}

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does RunContinuationsAsynchronously matter so much here?

Because without it, SetResult runs the awaiting continuation inline on the completing thread. When the completing thread is yours and idle, that inlining is a performance win — no queue hop. When it belongs to someone else and is scarce, you are silently borrowing their thread to run arbitrary code, and any slowness or blocking in your continuation becomes their stall. The same hazard exists in reverse in your own code: completing a TaskCompletionSource while holding a lock can run a continuation that takes the same lock, which is a deadlock nobody expects. I now use the flag by default and treat inline completion as the special case that needs justification.

Why a bounded channel rather than an unbounded one for the stream?

Because unbounded means the vendor's producer speed sets our memory usage, and a fast market plus a slow consumer is an out-of-memory kill rather than a degradation. Bounded forces a decision about what happens when we cannot keep up, and for market data the correct answer is DropOldest — a stale quote is worthless, so dropping it is better than blocking the producer or dying. For an order feed I would have chosen Wait and applied real back-pressure, and if the producer cannot be slowed, the design has to change upstream. The point is that bounded makes you answer the question.

Because a fork of a vendor library is a permanent liability: you inherit every future security fix and protocol change, and you lose support the moment they can point at your patch. It would have taken a day and cost years. The wrapper cost the same day, is entirely ours, and disappears cleanly when they ship the fix. I would fork only if the vendor were unresponsive, the library were abandoned, or the defect were unfixable by adaptation — none of which was true here.

Past that point I would move to a pooled IValueTaskSource implementation, which is what the BCL does internally for sockets. For the streaming path the channel is cheap; the bottleneck moves to the consumer, and the right answer there is more consumers, not a bigger channel.

Why can a custom TaskScheduler not control an async pipeline?

By asking what the remaining work actually is, not what percentage is left. If the remainder is more of the same, persistence is usually right. When the classes of work stop being separable by a static limit — many classes, changing mix, or a class whose fair share depends on demand rather than on a constant. What breaks when an error occurs halfway through a streamed response?

The status code is already sent, so you cannot turn a 200 into a 500 — the client gets a truncated body and has to detect it. When the export exceeds the time a client is willing to hold a connection, roughly a minute or two, or when it needs to be retried, resumed, or produced on a schedule. Why does AsNoTracking matter so much, mechanically?

Because EF Core's change tracker holds a reference to every entity it materialises so it can detect modifications on SaveChanges. How did you validate this before shipping it?

A load test against a restored copy of the largest tenant's data, not a generated dataset, with the memory counters open — that is how I caught the missing AsNoTracking, because peak memory had barely moved from the buffered version. I also tested the abandoned-client case explicitly, disconnecting mid-stream and confirming the enumeration was cancelled and the connection returned, because a streaming endpoint that keeps reading after the client leaves is the resource-exhaustion bug wearing a different hat.

Our standard response to a hung or stalled service was a restart. It worked, in the sense that the symptom went away, and it destroyed the evidence every time, so the same class of hang recurred roughly monthly for a year with no diagnosis. $ dotnet-dump analyze /dumps/hang.dmp
> clrstack -all            # threads: who is blocked, and on what
> dumpasync -stacks        # async state machines on the heap: who is suspended
> syncblk                  # contended monitors
> dumpheap -stat -type Task # is something accumulating tasks?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is dumpasync necessary — why is a thread dump not enough?

Because a suspended async method is not on any thread. Between the await and the completion, its state lives in a boxed state machine object on the heap with a continuation attached, and no stack anywhere references it. A thread dump of a hung async service can show you an entirely idle process while ten thousand operations are suspended waiting on something that will never complete. dumpasync walks the heap for those state machines and reconstructs the logical async call chains, which is the only view that answers "what are we waiting for".

Does .NET 11 runtime async change this?

It improves the live view. With runtime-async=on the runtime owns suspension, so live stack traces show the real call chain instead of the state-machine scaffolding — the docs' worked example drops from thirteen frames to five — and the async profiler in .NET 11 emits one consistent event model for both runtime-async and compiler-generated methods, so tooling does not have to care which you use. It does not remove the need for heap-based inspection of suspended work, and it is a preview feature you opt into per project, so I would not plan a diagnostic strategy around it yet.

How do you capture a dump from a container without breaking your security posture?

Carefully, and with the security team in the conversation before the incident rather than during it. What we did: the diagnostic tools live in the runtime image but the process needs SYS_PTRACE, which we granted only to a debug sidecar that is scaled to zero by default and requires an approved break-glass action to start. Dumps land on an encrypted volume with a short retention, and they are treated as production data because they contain memory — including secrets and customer records. That last point is the one people miss: a full dump is a copy of everything the process could see.

At what point does this approach not scale?

When the hangs are distributed rather than in one process — a stall caused by a downstream service, a lock in a database, a queue that stopped delivering. A dump tells you what one process is waiting on and nothing about why. At that point the right investment is distributed tracing with the wait represented as a span, so you can see the chain across services, and the dump becomes the tool of last resort for the process at the end of the chain. I would not present dumps as the diagnostic strategy; they are one layer of it.

How did you know adoption had actually happened?

By the incidents I was not in. The signal was an on-call engineer capturing a dump at 2 a.m. and posting the dumpasync output in the channel before I woke up, in a service I had never worked on. Before that I had a document and a game day, which are inputs, not evidence. I also tracked the ratio of hang incidents with a captured dump — it went from zero to about 80% within two months of the incident-template change, and the 20% were mostly cases where restarting really was the right first move.

18Killing flaky time-based tests with TimeProvider

Mid CI / test / reliability improvement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Our CI suite had about 40 tests that failed roughly one run in twelve. They all had the same shape: start something asynchronous, await Task.Delay(200), assert. On a developer laptop that is generous; on a shared CI runner with noisy neighbours and a CPU quota it is a coin flip. The team's coping mechanism was a retry-on-failure setting in the test runner, which meant a genuine regression could pass on the second attempt — we had turned our test suite into a slot machine and then stopped looking at it.

I made the argument that flakiness is worse than failure, because a suite you do not trust is a suite you route around, and I had the data: over the previous quarter, 31% of red builds were re-run without any code change, and two real bugs had reached staging behind a passing second attempt. That was enough to get a week.

The fix is to remove real time from the tests entirely. TimeProvider, added in .NET 8, is the abstraction for that: production code takes a TimeProvider and calls provider.Delay(...), provider.CreateTimer(...) or provider.GetUtcNow() instead of the static equivalents, and tests inject a fake whose clock they advance explicitly. The test then does not wait 200 milliseconds — it advances 200 milliseconds and the delay completes deterministically, which is both reliable and much faster.

// Production: time is a dependency, not an ambient global.
public sealed class RetryPolicy(TimeProvider time)
{
    public async Task<T> RunAsync<T>(Func<CancellationToken, Task<T>> op, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        for (var attempt = 0; ; attempt++)
        {
            try { return await op(ct); }
            catch (TransientException) when (attempt < 3)
            {
                await Task.Delay(Backoff(attempt), time, ct);   // .NET 8+ overload
            }
        }
    }
}

// Test: no wall clock, no sleeps, fully deterministic.
var time = new FakeTimeProvider();                 // Microsoft.Extensions.TimeProvider.Testing
var task = new RetryPolicy(time).RunAsync(Flaky, CancellationToken.None);
time.Advance(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(1));             // first backoff elapses
time.Advance(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2));             // second
Assert.Equal(expected, await task);

The other half was the tests that were not about time at all but were using a delay as a synchronisation primitive — "wait for the background handler to have run". Those got a real signal instead: the code under test exposes a Task or a channel the test can await, so the test waits for the event rather than for a duration. That is the change that matters conceptually, and it usually improves the production code too, because "how do I know this finished" is a question the production code should be able to answer.

Result: 40 flaky tests to zero over a week, suite runtime down from 11 minutes to 6 because the sleeps were gone, and we deleted the retry setting — which was the point, because with retries on you cannot tell whether flakiness is decreasing. Re-run rate on red builds went from 31% to under 2% in the following quarter.

What I would do differently: I fixed the 40 and did not add a guard, so new sleeps started reappearing within two months. An analyzer banning Task.Delay and Thread.Sleep in test projects would have cost an hour and would have held the line.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you treat test reliability as a first-class property or as an annoyance, and whether you can get non-feature work funded with evidence. The best signal here is the argument that a retry setting hides the problem — that is a judgment call about integrity of feedback, and it is more interesting than the technical fix. Weak answers describe fixing flaky tests one at a time forever. Also worth naming: removing the retry was the risky part, and you did it deliberately.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is TimeProvider better than an interface you write yourself?

Because it is in the box from .NET 8 and the framework uses it: Task.Delay, PeriodicTimer, CancellationTokenSource and the resilience libraries all take a TimeProvider overload, so injecting a fake controls time everywhere rather than only in code you remembered to abstract. A hand-rolled IClock only controls the calls that route through it, and the framework's timers keep using the real clock — which is exactly the gap that leaves a test flaky after you thought you had fixed it. Fewer abstractions of my own is also a maintenance win.

What about tests where the concurrency itself is the thing under test?

Those cannot be made deterministic by controlling time, because the non-determinism is real. I handle them differently: make the test drive the interleaving explicitly with a TaskCompletionSource or a barrier so the ordering is chosen rather than raced, and keep genuinely stochastic checks — soak tests, stress runs — out of the per-commit suite and on a schedule where a failure is investigated rather than re-run. The rule I apply is that anything blocking a merge must be deterministic; everything else can be probabilistic if someone owns the results.

Was removing the retry setting risky?

Yes, and I did it after the fixes rather than before, deliberately. If I had removed it first the pipeline would have gone red constantly and the team would have re-enabled it and trusted me less. The sequence was: fix the 40, run both with and without retries for a week to confirm the rate was actually zero, then remove and announce. The residual risk is a genuinely flaky test appearing later and blocking everyone — which is why I paired removal with a quarantine mechanism, so a newly flaky test can be tagged out of the gate within minutes while its owner fixes it, rather than the whole team reaching for retries again.

At what suite size does this approach stop being enough?

Determinism scales fine; wall-clock does not. Past a few thousand tests the constraint becomes parallelism and shared state — a fake clock does not help when two tests share a database. Then the work shifts to isolation: per-test containers or transactions, and making the suite shardable. I would still do the time work first, because a flaky suite cannot be parallelised safely, but I would not claim it as the answer to slow CI beyond a certain size.

How do you convince a team that flaky is worse than failing?

With their own numbers, not with the principle. I showed the re-run rate and, more powerfully, the two bugs that reached staging behind a green second attempt — a concrete escape is worth more than any argument about signal quality. Then I made the first fix cheap and visible so the team could feel the difference within days. The principle lands after the experience, not before it; leading with "flaky tests are technical debt" gets agreement and no behaviour change.

// The shape that survives: the loop owns its own error handling, and
// anything that escapes it kills the process so the platform can restart us.

{
    while (!stopping.IsCancellationRequested)
    {
        try { await ConsumeBatchAsync(stopping); }
        catch (OperationCanceledException) when (stopping.IsCancellationRequested) { break; }
        catch (Exception ex)
        {
            _log.LogError(ex, "batch failed; backing off");
            await Task.Delay(_backoff.Next(), _time, stopping);   // transient: retry
        }
    }
}

The measurement is what ended the conversation, and it is why I profiled before I argued: on our workload, async machinery was about 0.4% of CPU, because we are an I/O-bound system and the overhead runtime async removes is not where our time goes. Presenting "here is what it would buy us, measured" converted the discussion from a disagreement about appetite for risk into a shared reading of a number. They agreed the same day, and asked for the internal-tools pilot to be tracked so it would not quietly disappear — which was fair, and which I then owned.

What I would do differently: my first response, in writing, was a paragraph about preview support policy. It read as bureaucratic and it put us on opposite sides for two days. I should have opened with the question "what problem made you want this", because the honest answer — hang diagnostics — was something I could already give them for free that week.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can hold a technical line against organisational pressure without being obstructive, and whether your objection is grounded in facts you actually verified. The strong pattern is: find the real need under the stated ask, price the ask honestly, offer a dated alternative, and bring a measurement. The failure modes are the engineer who says no on principle ("we don't use preview features") and the engineer who caves and then complains later. Naming the bureaucratic first draft is what stops this sounding smug.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

What actually is runtime async, in two sentences?

Since C# 5 the compiler has rewritten every async method into a state-machine struct that it drives across suspension points. In .NET 11 the runtime takes that over: it suspends and resumes methods itself, so there are no compiler-generated state machines — the .NET 11 runtime libraries ship compiled that way — which gives cleaner live stack traces, correct breakpoint binding across awaits, and lower overhead from cached continuations, tiered compilation of async bodies, and continuations that skip ExecutionContext capture when there is nothing to restore.

What would you have needed to see to say yes?

GA rather than preview, a workload where the overhead it removes is actually material, and a rollback path that is not a rebuild. Concretely: after November GA, a two-week soak in our internal tools with allocation and p99 measured before and after, plus a check that our profiler and APM agent report correctly under runtime async — that last one is a real risk with a change this deep, and it is the thing I would test first. If all three came back clean I would take it to production at the next planned framework move, not as a separate change.

Is your LTS-only policy itself defensible, or is it just conservatism?

It is defensible for our situation and I would not defend it universally. We run about thirty services with a small platform team, so an STS cadence means an upgrade every twelve months across all of them, which is roughly a permanent half-engineer. LTS makes that every two to three years. If we had five services and a strong upgrade pipeline, STS would be reasonable and would keep us closer to the runtime team. The policy is a consequence of our upgrade cost, so the honest way to change it is to reduce that cost, not to make exceptions.

What if they had overruled you?

Then it is their call to make and my job to make it as safe as possible — I would have asked for it in one non-critical service first, with an explicit rollback plan and a date to review, and I would have written down the risk I had named so the decision is legible later. Disagreeing and committing is only meaningful if the commitment is real. What I would not do is implement it half-heartedly or set it up to fail; if it works, I want to have been wrong in a way we can measure.

How do you keep a "we will look at it later" promise from being a brush-off?

By dating it and giving it an owner in the same conversation. The pilot went on the platform team's roadmap for the GA month with my name on it, and the .NET 12 decision point went into our framework-upgrade policy document rather than into a chat message. The stakeholder asked for it to be tracked, which was a fair test of whether I meant it. A deferral with no date is a refusal wearing a polite hat, and people can tell.

21The big behavioural Q&A

mid → principal Cross-cutting

Twenty-two prompts that are not tied to one incident. Each answer names which prepared story to deploy and what the interviewer is listening for. Deploy by mechanism, not by label: if two stories fit, pick the one whose result is closest to the job you are interviewing for.

Failure & ownership

Tell me about a time you caused a production incident.

Deploy 12 (the missing await) if you were the author, or 15 (the cancelled scheduler) if you want the version where the failure was a decision rather than a defect. They are listening for whether "caused" survives contact with the telling — candidates reliably drift into "the system caused". Say what you did, what it cost, what you did in the first ten minutes, and what is different now. Do not offer a mitigating context until they ask.

What is the biggest mistake of your career?

15. A cancelled project you sponsored beats a bug, because it shows judgment failing rather than attention failing, and it has a bigger number attached. Give the weeks, the cost, and the specific reasoning error — validating a prototype against a workload that was not ours — because a mistake with a named reasoning error is one you can prove you will not repeat.

Tell me about a time you were wrong and someone else was right.

06 inverted, or the moment inside 16 where you had to be told twice to translate a result into money. Pick something where being wrong cost you something. Interviewers can tell the difference between a real concession and a humble-brag ("I was wrong to work too hard").

How do you handle it when you do not know the answer?

04 is the honest exhibit: two days, a wrong track, and a reframe. The signal is a method — what you eliminate, what you measure, when you ask for help, and how you time-box. Say the time-box out loud; "I give myself an hour before I pull someone in" is more convincing than a claim of persistence.

Describe a time you had to admit a mistake to a customer or a stakeholder.

12. Three rows, one customer, disclosed the same week. What they want is that you disclosed before you were asked, that you gave a bounded scope you could actually defend, and that you did not minimise. "It took two days to be sure it was only three rows" is the sentence that lands.

Conflict & influence

Tell me about a technical disagreement you lost.

Use the inverse of 03 or 20: a case where you disagreed, committed, and the outcome was fine — or where you were proved wrong. Losing well is a real skill and few candidates prepare it. Name what changed your mind, or, if nothing did, what you did to make the chosen path succeed anyway.

How do you convince someone senior to you that they are wrong?

20. The pattern to articulate: verify the facts first, find the need under the ask, price the ask honestly, bring a measurement, offer a dated alternative. Include the bureaucratic first draft — it shows you know that being right is only half of it.

Tell me about the hardest piece of feedback you have given.

10 escalated, or your own equivalent about performance rather than about code. The signal is specificity and follow-through: what you said, how they reacted, what you did in the following month. Vague "I gave direct feedback and they appreciated it" is a non-answer.

How do you get another team to prioritise something for you?

14. The reproduction is the answer: a runnable failing case crosses organisational boundaries in a way that an argument does not. Add the second half — you shipped your own mitigation so your delivery did not depend on their schedule.

Describe a time you changed your mind based on data.

15 (the trace that killed your own project) or 20 (the profile that showed 0.4%). The best version is data that went against you, not data that confirmed you.

Leadership & mentoring

How do you level up an engineer who is stuck?

10. Lead with the diagnosis — different mistakes in the same area means a missing mental model, not missing knowledge — then the change in approach, then evidence of independence. The closing beat is them teaching someone else.

How do you set technical direction without being the person who writes it all?

03 and 09 together: an analyzer is a decision that keeps deciding after you leave the room. The principle to state is that durable direction is encoded in defaults, required parameters, templates and build failures, not in documents — and that documents are for the why, so people can overturn it deliberately.

Tell me about a standard you introduced that people resisted.

03 (900 ConfigureAwait calls) or 09 (analyzer as an error). Own the friction and the goodwill you spent. The follow-up is usually "would you do it again" — have a real answer, including what you would soften.

How do you decide what not to work on?

09 (ratchet first, paydown ranked by risk) and 11 (three asks priced separately). The transferable idea: separate "stop it getting worse" from "fix it", because the first is usually 1% of the cost and 80% of the value.

What does a healthy on-call rotation look like to you?

19 plus 17. Concretely: alerts that name the layer that is broken rather than the symptom, health checks that assert the work and not the process, a runbook that leads with commands, and post-mortems whose standing question is "where else does this exist". Say what you have actually changed, not what you believe.

Ambiguity & prioritisation

Tell me about a time the requirements were unclear.

13. The reframe is the answer: the requirement was not shifting, it was under-specified, and each stakeholder was supplying their own missing half. The transferable question — "what should the user see when this fails?" — is a good thing to be able to hand them.

How do you estimate work you have not done before?

05 (the upgrade that took five days instead of two) and 09 (estimating by counting callers, not lines). Show a unit of estimation, an early re-forecast, and a pre-flight step you added afterwards.

Tell me about a time you had to say no to a deadline.

11. Do not tell it as a refusal — tell it as a decomposition with prices, ending in a decision someone else could own. The measured 94/6 split is what makes it a negotiation rather than a complaint.

How do you decide between a quick fix and a proper one?

01 (tourniquet then migration) and 15 (nine days of admission control instead of a scheduler). The rule to state: ship the quick fix when the cost of the outage is now and the cost of the debt is later, but say it is a tourniquet in writing, with an owner and a date, or it becomes the architecture.

What do you do when everything is urgent?

08. The re-framing story: the work moved when it was described in the right units, not when it was pushed harder. Prioritisation is often a communication problem wearing an urgency costume.

Values, motivation & craft

Why do you care about async correctness specifically?

Ground it, do not philosophise. Something like: async bugs fail silently and at the worst time — a blocking call is invisible until a dependency slows down, an un-awaited task swallows its own exception, a dropped token turns into an availability risk. Cite 01, 12 and 08 in one sentence each. The interviewer is checking whether your interest is aesthetic or consequential.

What is the most interesting thing you have learned recently?

20. Runtime async in .NET 11 is genuinely interesting and currently topical: the runtime takes over suspension from the compiler, live stack traces drop from thirteen frames to five in Microsoft's own example, and continuations skip ExecutionContext capture when nothing needs restoring. Say clearly that it is preview and that you have not shipped it — being current and being disciplined are both signals, and claiming production experience you do not have is the one unrecoverable mistake in this answer.

22Night-before cheat sheet

Delivery Read this last

Ten sentences to read the night before, then close the laptop. They are about delivery, not content — the content is in the twenty stories above.

  1. Open with one line of situation, then stop. "An API tier doing 2,000 requests a second went from 80 milliseconds to nine seconds, with CPU flat." If they want more setup they will ask; if you give it unprompted you will spend your best two minutes on context.
  2. Say your role in the second sentence. "I was incident commander", "I was the reviewer", "it was my project and I got it funded". Ambiguous ownership is the single most common reason a good story reads as junior.
  3. Separate "I" from "we" deliberately and generously. Name what someone else did — "Priya pulled the dump, I made the call to stop scaling". Precise credit makes your own claims more believable, not less.
  4. Quantify the result before they ask. Two numbers beat one and three beat two: latency, money, and something the customer noticed. If you genuinely have no number, say what you would have measured and why you could not.
  5. Tie the technical decision back to a real async trade-off. Bounded concurrency against throughput, ValueTask against safety, streaming against clean error handling, cancellation against consistency. "It depends, because…" with a named because is the lead-level register.
  6. Own the "what I would do differently" without flagellating. One sentence, aimed at a system or a decision — a missing alert, a suppressed warning, a bureaucratic first draft — and then move on. Dwelling reads as anxiety; skipping reads as unreflective.
  7. Never present a scaffold from this page as your own history. Every worked example here is fiction; use it to find your equivalent. An invented story collapses on the second drill-down, and interviewers at this level always ask a second drill-down.
  8. Answer the drill in three to six sentences, then stop talking. The follow-up chains in this page are calibrated to that length. Silence after a complete answer is the interviewer's turn, not a gap you must fill.
  9. When you do not know, say the boundary and then reason. "I have not shipped runtime async — it is preview in .NET 11 — but here is what I would test first, and why." Reasoning from a stated boundary scores higher than a confident guess and infinitely higher than an invented one.
  10. Close each story with the systemic change, not the fix. The analyzer, the alert, the required parameter, the incident-template line. Fixing the bug is the mid-level answer; making the class of bug impossible is the one they are hiring for.
