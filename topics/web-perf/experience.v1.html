01 The hero image that made LCP miss by 2.3 seconds

SeniorPerformance problem

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A marketing landing page had an LCP of 4.8 seconds in the field (CrUX, p75) against a 2.5-second "good" bar, and the product team wanted to know why "the page felt slow" before they'd approve a design change nobody had asked me to make. I opened the Performance panel, recorded a throttled trace, and read the LCP breakdown by its four sub-phases instead of guessing: TTFB was fine at ~400ms, but load delay was over 1.8 seconds — the hero <img> wasn't discovered until the render-blocking Google Fonts CSS and a synchronous analytics <script> in <head> had both finished, because the browser's preload scanner had nothing to find until the HTML parser got past them. Load duration added another 900ms because the image was an unoptimized 2400px-wide JPEG served at its native size with no srcset. Fixes, in order of what actually moved the needle: <link rel="preload" as="image"> plus fetchpriority="high" on the hero image so the preload scanner could start the fetch immediately; moved the analytics tag to async; self-hosted the two font weights we actually used instead of a Google Fonts request, cutting a full DNS+TLS round trip; and swapped the JPEG for AVIF with srcset/sizes sized to the actual rendered width. LCP came down to 1.9 seconds in the field within a week of rollout. What I'd do differently: I'd have set an explicit width/height (or aspect-ratio) on the hero image from day one — it happened not to shift layout here because the CSS already reserved space, but that was luck, not design, and I only noticed after the fact.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you diagnose LCP by its sub-phases instead of pattern-matching "images are slow, compress it," and whether you have field data (CrUX/RUM) alongside a lab trace rather than just one or the other. The common failure is naming a fix that sounds right (lazy-load the hero image, ironically making LCP worse) without having looked at where the actual milliseconds went.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does fetchpriority alone not fix load delay if the image is still discovered late?

fetchpriority="high" only raises the priority of a request the browser already knows about — it does nothing if the preload scanner hasn't found the <img> yet because render-blocking resources are still ahead of it in the document. That's why the fix pairs it with an explicit <link rel="preload"> or simply removing what's blocking discovery.

What if the hero image had been injected by client-side JS instead of present in the initial HTML?

Then no static preload scanner trick helps — the browser can't discover it until that script runs and executes. I'd push to render the LCP element server-side or in the initial HTML specifically because it's the one element where discovery time is non-negotiable.

At what scale does self-hosting fonts stop being a clear win?

If you're already on a multi-region CDN and only use one or two weights, self-hosting is close to free. It gets more nuanced with a large variable-font family across many locales, where Google Fonts' shared, highly-cached edge network can occasionally out-cache a smaller origin — I'd measure both rather than assume.

What did you trade away by preloading the hero image with high priority?

Bandwidth contention with whatever else is loading in that same window — on a constrained connection, over-preloading can delay other important requests. I scoped it to exactly one image, not a blanket policy, and watched the network waterfall to confirm nothing else got crowded out.

How would this diagnosis differ on a page using an SSR framework with streaming?

Streaming can push TTFB down and the hero element earlier into the byte stream, which usually helps load delay directly, but if the framework's own bootstrap script sits ahead of the image in the HTML, you can reintroduce the same blocking pattern one layer up — the sub-phase breakdown is the same regardless of framework.

What would you do differently now?

Add an LCP regression check to CI keyed off the sub-phases specifically, not just the aggregate number, so a future change that trades load delay for render delay doesn't silently cancel out and hide a regression behind a flat total.

02 SSR, SSG, or islands: choosing a rendering strategy

StaffArchitectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A content-heavy marketing and docs site was a fully client-rendered SPA: fast to build features on, but TTFB-to-interactive was dominated by shipping and hydrating a JS bundle for pages that were, structurally, mostly static text. I owned the call on where to take it, for a team of five with no dedicated infra engineer. I ran a two-week spike comparing three shapes on the same three representative pages: static-site generation (build-time HTML, zero client JS by default), server-side rendering with the existing framework's hydration model, and an islands architecture (static HTML with small, independently hydrated interactive components — a search box, a feedback widget). SSG won on every Core Web Vital by a wide margin — there's no hydration cost at all for a page that's 95% static content — but it meant a content change needed a full rebuild-and-deploy, which our CMS team's editors weren' t going to tolerate for typo fixes. Full SSR with framework hydration gave us live content but paid the hydration tax on every page even where nothing was interactive. Islands gave us static-by-default pages with the two or three genuinely interactive widgets hydrating independently and lazily, at the cost of a less mature ecosystem and a real learning curve for a team used to one rendering model everywhere. I chose islands, prototyped the CMS-triggered incremental rebuild path to satisfy the editors' turnaround requirement, and wrote down the decision with the actual trace numbers. What I'd do differently: I underestimated the ecosystem cost — finding component libraries that played well with partial, framework-agnostic hydration took longer than the technical spike suggested it would.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does hydration cost scale with component tree size rather than page complexity as perceived by a user?

Hydration re-runs the framework's reconciliation against server-rendered markup for every hydrated component, so the cost is proportional to how much of the tree is marked interactive, not how the page looks. A visually simple page with one deeply nested interactive widget can hydrate more expensively than a visually busy but fully static one.

What if the CMS team's rebuild-turnaround requirement had been non-negotiable and incremental rebuilds weren't feasible?

Then SSG would have been ruled out regardless of its metrics win, and I'd have picked SSR with aggressive edge caching and short-TTL revalidation instead — the content-freshness constraint dominates the decision once it's truly non-negotiable.

At what scale does an islands architecture's tooling immaturity stop being worth the metrics win?

If the team is large and needs a wide, mature component ecosystem with predictable framework support, the integration tax can exceed the hydration savings. It paid off here specifically because we had few enough interactive widgets that hand-rolling islands support for them was a bounded, one-time cost.

What did you trade away choosing islands over plain SSG?

Some simplicity — the team now reasons about two rendering models (static shell, independently hydrated islands) instead of one, which is a real ongoing cognitive cost even though it's smaller than the alternative's hydration cost.

How would resumability (skipping hydration's re-execution step entirely) change this trade-off?

A resumable framework serializes enough state to resume interactivity without replaying component logic on the client, which narrows the CSR/SSR hydration-cost gap considerably. I'd re-run the spike against it before assuming the original conclusion still holds if the team were choosing today.

What would you do differently now?

Timebox a "day in the life of a content editor" trial during the spike itself, not just a technical benchmark — the requirement that actually decided the fine details only surfaced because an editor complained after the fact, not during planning.

03 The dashboard tab that ate 4GB by lunchtime

SeniorProduction incident

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Support tickets described the same pattern: an internal analytics dashboard, left open in a tab all day, would slow to a crawl and eventually crash the tab by early afternoon. I reproduced it by leaving the dashboard open with DevTools' Memory panel recording, and took heap snapshots roughly every 20 minutes rather than trusting a single snapshot. The three-snapshot technique — take one, do a normal work cycle, take another, do it again, then diff snapshot two against snapshot three for objects that only ever grow — pointed at a detached DOM tree count climbing steadily: a chart-redraw function on a setInterval was adding a new resize listener on the window on every redraw without ever removing the previous one, and each listener's closure held a reference to the old chart container element, which kept the whole detached subtree alive even after the chart itself had been replaced. Nothing was visibly wrong from the outside for the first hour — the leak was small per cycle — which is exactly why nobody had noticed it until a full day of uptime made it obvious. Fix: store the listener reference and call removeEventListener before attaching a new one, and switch the redraw trigger from setInterval to a ResizeObserver so there was one listener for the container's lifetime instead of one per redraw. Memory growth flattened from roughly 40MB/hour to near zero after a day. What I'd do differently: I'd have added a memory-growth check to our own internal-tools monitoring long before a support ticket found it — nobody owns "the dashboard felt slow by 3pm" as a metric unless someone deliberately decides to.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you actually know the retained-size diffing technique (comparing two snapshots taken during steady-state activity, not one snapshot at startup) rather than saying "we found a memory leak" with no method behind it. The mistake is stopping at "we added removeEventListener" without explaining why the DOM subtree stayed detached-but-alive in the first place — that's the part that demonstrates you understand the GC's reachability model, not just the symptom.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does a detached DOM node stay in memory at all if it's no longer in the document?

The garbage collector only reclaims objects that are unreachable from a GC root. A closure captured by a still-registered event listener holding a reference to that node keeps it reachable — "detached" describes its relationship to the document, not its reachability to the collector, and that distinction is exactly what the fix depends on.

What if the leak had been in a third-party charting library's internals rather than our own callback?

I'd isolate it with the same snapshot-diffing technique to confirm it's genuinely upstream, then check the library's issue tracker and changelog before assuming I need a workaround — a surprising number of "our code" leaks turn out to be a documented, already-fixed issue in a version behind current.

At what point does a small per-cycle leak become worth root-causing versus just recommending a periodic tab refresh?

A workaround is reasonable as an immediate mitigation for a long-lived internal tool, but I wouldn't stop there — 40MB/hour compounding for a full workday is a multi-gigabyte problem, and "just refresh the tab" doesn't scale to more dashboards or a version where the interval is shorter.

What did you trade away switching from setInterval to ResizeObserver?

A small amount of redraw-timing predictability — a ResizeObserver fires on actual size changes rather than a fixed cadence, so any code that assumed a steady tick needed to be checked for hidden dependence on that assumption.

How would this differ debugging a memory leak in a single-page app that never does a full page reload?

The stakes are higher precisely because there's no reload to reset accumulated state — every route change that doesn't fully tear down its previous view's subscriptions and closures compounds for the entire session, which is why SPA teardown logic deserves the same scrutiny as this dashboard's redraw loop.

What would you do differently now?

Add a synthetic long-running session to our internal monitoring that just leaves the dashboard open and graphs its own memory over hours, so growth shows up as a metric trend before it shows up as a support ticket.

04 Cutting the build over from Webpack to Vite

StaffMigration / upgrade

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Local dev server startup on a mid-sized app had crept to nearly a minute, and a hot-module reload after a one-line change took 4-8 seconds — long enough that engineers had started batching changes to amortize the wait, which is its own quiet productivity tax. I owned migrating the build tooling from a Webpack config that had accreted loaders and plugins over several years to Vite, whose dev server serves native ESM directly to the browser and only bundles for production, using esbuild for fast dependency pre-bundling. I didn't do a big-bang cutover: I ran Vite alongside the existing Webpack config behind a feature flag for local dev only, kept the production build on Webpack until dev parity was proven, and migrated loader-equivalent behavior (SVG-as-component, CSS modules, a legacy require.context glob import with no direct Vite equivalent) one at a time, each with its own before/after check against real pages. Dev server startup dropped from ~55 seconds to under 2 seconds, and HMR became near-instant, because the browser only re-requests and re-evaluates the one changed module instead of Webpack re-bundling a dependency graph around it. The production build cutover came last and needed the most care: Rollup (Vite's production bundler) has different default chunking heuristics than Webpack, so our route-based code-splitting boundaries shifted slightly and I had to re-verify bundle sizes per route rather than assume the aggregate number told the whole story. What I'd do differently: I'd have inventoried every Webpack-specific plugin behavior (loader ordering quirks especially) into an explicit checklist before starting, instead of discovering a couple of them as broken pages during migration — nothing was lost permanently, but it cost debugging time that a checklist would have caught up front.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does native ESM serving make HMR faster mechanically, not just "because Vite is newer"?

Webpack's dev server re-bundles a dependency graph around a changed module because it ships one (or a few) bundle files; Vite serves each module as its own request, so on a change the browser only re-requests and re-evaluates that one module via the existing module graph already in memory — there's no bundling step in the loop at all during dev.

What if a legacy loader had no Vite-ecosystem equivalent at all?

I'd write a small custom Vite plugin replicating just the transform we needed rather than searching indefinitely for a drop-in replacement — Vite's plugin API is intentionally close to Rollup's, so a narrow, purpose-built plugin is usually a bounded, low-risk piece of work.

At what point would you not recommend this migration to another team?

If the app is small enough that Webpack's cold start was never actually painful, or if the team depends heavily on a Webpack-specific plugin ecosystem with no Rollup equivalent, the migration cost isn't worth the dev-experience win — I'd say so directly rather than push a fashionable tool where it doesn't pay off.

What did you trade away moving the production bundler to Rollup?

Some familiarity — a few engineers had built a mental model of Webpack's chunk-splitting behavior over years, and that model doesn't transfer directly, so there was a real (if temporary) drop in "I know exactly why this chunk looks like this" confidence across the team.

How does this migration interact with a monorepo using multiple frameworks?

Vite's per-framework plugins (React, Vue, etc.) can coexist in one config, but shared internal packages need to publish ESM-compatible output or you reintroduce the same resolution headaches Webpack had — I'd audit the monorepo's internal package formats before migrating, not after.

What would you do differently now?

Build the loader-equivalence checklist first, as a shared doc the whole team could add entries to during their own testing, rather than relying on me alone to enumerate every custom Webpack behavior from memory.

05 The lazy-load-everything argument

SeniorTechnical disagreement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A well-meaning teammate proposed a blanket rule during a perf sprint: add loading="lazy" to every <img> tag in the codebase via a codemod, on the theory that lazy-loading is strictly better for performance. I pushed back, and it turned into a real disagreement in review rather than a quiet override, because the rule sounded obviously correct and I needed to explain the exception clearly rather than just say "no." The loading="lazy" attribute defers a <img>'s fetch until it's near the viewport, which is exactly right for below-the-fold images — but applying it to an above-the-fold hero image (frequently the LCP element) actively delays the one fetch you want the browser's preload scanner to start immediately, turning a fast image into a slow LCP. I asked for the codemod to explicitly exclude anything in the first viewport-height of a page template, backed by a quick A/B on our two highest-traffic templates showing LCP regressing by several hundred milliseconds when the hero image was included versus excluded. We shipped the codemod with that carve-out, and I wrote a one-paragraph rule of thumb into our frontend guidelines doc so the next person wouldn't need to relearn it in a code review. What I'd do differently: I'd have proposed the carve-out proactively in the first review comment instead of only after a full back-and-forth — the actual technical point was simple, and I let the disagreement run longer than it needed to.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you can disagree with a plausible-sounding but wrong generalization using a concrete mechanism (preload scanner discovery) rather than just asserting seniority, and whether the disagreement stayed collaborative and ended in a durable fix (the guideline doc) rather than a one-off override. The mistake is a story where you were simply right and everyone conceded immediately — real disagreements have friction, and naming it is more credible than pretending there wasn't any.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does the preload scanner not "just figure out" that the hero image is high priority?

The preload scanner discovers resources by speculatively parsing the HTML ahead of the main parser, but loading="lazy" is an explicit signal telling the browser to defer the fetch regardless of position — it overrides the scanner's normal eager behavior for images, which is precisely why the attribute is wrong for anything that might be the LCP element.

What if the teammate had pushed back on your carve-out as "premature optimization for an edge case"?

I'd have pointed to the A/B data directly — a several-hundred-millisecond LCP regression on our two highest-traffic templates isn't an edge case, it's the majority of real traffic, which is exactly the kind of claim that needs a number rather than a debate about principle.

At what point would a blanket lazy-load rule actually be fine?

On a template with no image in the first viewport at all — a text-only article listing, say — the blanket rule is harmless because there's nothing above the fold to protect. The exception is about position, not a universal "never lazy-load" rule.

What did the guideline doc trade away versus a lint rule that enforces it automatically?

Enforcement — a doc relies on someone reading it at the right moment, where a lint rule (flagging loading="lazy" on anything matched by a known "likely-hero" selector) would catch a regression automatically. I'd consider that a follow-up if the mistake recurred even once after the doc shipped.

How does this generalize past images — what's the same mistake applied to JS?

Deferring or lazily importing a script that's actually needed for the initial interactive state (a route's main bundle, say) causes the same category of problem: a general "defer things" instinct applied to the one thing that needs to be eager.

What would you do differently now?

Lead the review comment with the carve-out proposal itself, framed as "this rule is right except for one case, here's why," instead of disagreeing first and only supplying the fix once asked — it would have been the same outcome with less friction.

06 The layout shift that only happened on slow connections

MidNasty debugging session

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Our CLS metric looked fine in every local test I ran, but the field data (real-user CLS in the p75) was well above the 0.1 "good" threshold, and I couldn't reproduce it on my own fast office connection no matter how many times I loaded the page. I switched Chrome DevTools' network throttling to "Slow 3G" instead of the default "no throttling," and the shift appeared immediately and repeatably: a promo banner injected by an ad-tech script was arriving late enough on a slow connection that it pushed the entire article body down after the reader had already started reading, whereas on a fast connection it arrived early enough to slot in before anyone noticed. The fix was to reserve the banner's space with a fixed-height container (using the ad slot's known dimensions) regardless of whether the script had loaded yet, so its eventual insertion filled a space that already existed instead of pushing content. What would you do differently now?

Build a standing audit (even a simple script scanning our served HTML for script tags with neither async nor defer) rather than relying on catching it during an incident review of one specific outage.

19 Lighthouse CI was too noisy to trust

SeniorCI / reliability improvement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A Lighthouse CI check that another engineer had introduced a year earlier had, over time, become something most of the team clicked past without reading — it failed often enough on unrelated pull requests that "just re-run it, it's probably flaky" had become the default response, which meant it was providing essentially zero real signal by the time I looked into it despite still gating merges nominally. I instrumented the check's own history: pulled the last few months of CI run results and found the failure rate was over 30% on PRs that made no performance-relevant change at all, which confirmed the "it's flaky" folk wisdom was actually correct and not just an excuse. The root causes were mundane once measured rather than assumed: our CI runners were shared, variably-loaded machines with no CPU throttling normalization, so the same code could measure as fast or slow depending on what else happened to be running on that runner at that moment, and the check compared against a single baseline run rather than a distribution. I moved the check to a dedicated, consistently-provisioned runner pool, switched to a median-of-five-runs comparison with a wider tolerance band tuned against several weeks of the new setup's own noise floor, and added a clear, specific failure message naming which metric regressed by how much instead of a generic "performance check failed" that told nobody anything actionable. False-positive rate dropped to under 3%, and — more importantly than the number itself — the team's own trust in the check visibly recovered; people started actually reading the failure messages again instead of reflexively re-running. What I'd do differently: I'd have measured the actual false-positive rate the moment the "just re-run it" pattern first became common team folklore, rather than letting a whole year pass with a nominally-blocking check that had, in practice, already stopped meaningfully blocking anything.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you diagnose "this check is unreliable" with actual historical failure-rate data rather than accepting team folklore at face value, and whether you understand the specific infrastructure causes of CI performance-measurement noise (shared runners, single-run comparisons). The mistake is a story that just says "we made it more reliable" without the mechanism — a vague fix reads as having gotten lucky rather than having understood the problem.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does a shared, variably-loaded CI runner specifically break performance measurements more than it breaks, say, a unit test?

A unit test's pass/fail is generally independent of how fast it runs, so contention just makes it slower without changing the result — a performance measurement's result is the timing, so the same contention that only costs a unit test some wall-clock time directly corrupts a performance check's actual signal.

What if moving to a dedicated runner pool wasn't within budget?

At what point would you recommend removing a check like this entirely rather than trying to fix it?

If, after genuinely trying to fix the noise sources, the false-positive rate stayed high enough that the team's trust couldn't realistically recover, I'd rather remove the gate and rely on the field-data review cadence than keep a nominally-blocking check that everyone has learned to ignore — a check nobody trusts is worse than no check, because it creates a false sense of coverage.

What did the wider tolerance band trade away?

Some sensitivity — a genuinely small regression is now more likely to slip through unflagged than it would with a tighter band. I judged that trade worth making because a check that's too sensitive to catch everything, but is trusted by nobody, catches less in practice than a slightly less sensitive check people actually read.

How does this generalize to other kinds of flaky CI checks beyond performance?

The same diagnostic pattern — measure the actual historical failure rate instead of trusting folklore, find the specific infrastructure or test-design root cause, fix that root cause rather than just retrying more — applies just as directly to flaky integration or end-to-end tests, which suffer from analogous environmental noise sources.

What would you do differently now?

Set a standing quarterly check on every blocking CI gate's actual false-positive rate, rather than waiting for folklore to build up and then reacting to it once someone happens to investigate.

20 Saying no to an autoplay hero video

PrincipalStakeholder pushback

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A newly-hired CMO, coming from a brand-marketing background, wanted the homepage redesigned around a full-bleed, autoplaying background video in the hero section, modeled on a competitor's site they admired. I'd already seen this exact pattern cost a previous team dearly — autoplaying video is a significant, sustained main-thread and network cost that actively fights good LCP and INP, and on mobile networks (where a meaningful share of our traffic came from) it could add real, measurable cost to users on metered data plans. Rather than simply refusing a VP-level, brand-driven request — which would have been both organizationally unwise and not actually in the company's interest, since the underlying goal (an impactful, premium-feeling hero) was completely legitimate — I asked for a short discovery conversation to understand what specifically about the competitor's video the CMO found compelling, and it turned out to be the sense of motion and production quality, not literally "video" as a technical requirement. I proposed and prototyped an alternative: a short, muted, <video> fallback-to-static-poster-image approach for capable connections, using the prefers-reduced-motion media query to skip video entirely for users who'd expressed that preference, lazy-initializing the video only after the LCP-critical poster image had already painted so the video was strictly additive rather than something the page waited on, and capping it to a connection-quality check (navigator.connection.effectiveType where available) that fell back to the static poster image entirely on a slow connection. I walked the CMO through both versions side by side rather than just describing the difference, and they preferred the prototype once they saw it looked and felt like what they wanted, at a fraction of the performance cost of true unconditional autoplay. What I'd do differently: I'd have framed the initial conversation entirely around their actual goal (impact, motion, premium feel) from my very first sentence, instead of opening with a technical objection to their proposed literal implementation — leading with the shared goal would likely have gotten to the same collaborative outcome with noticeably less initial friction.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you can push back on a senior stakeholder's specific request without dismissing their underlying (legitimate) goal, by separating "what they asked for" from "what they actually need" and prototyping an alternative rather than just arguing in the abstract. The mistake at principal level is a story where the stakeholder is simply overruled by technical authority — the strongest version finds and delivers what they actually wanted, at a cost they're happy to pay.

Why does deferring the video's initialization until after the poster image paints protect LCP specifically?

If the LCP element is the poster image, its paint timing is what's measured — as long as the video initialization happens strictly after that paint and doesn't block or compete with the resources needed for it, the video's own cost falls outside the LCP window entirely rather than delaying it.

What if the CMO had insisted on true unconditional autoplay even after seeing the prototype?

I'd present the actual metric cost in business terms (a specific projected LCP/INP impact on the mobile traffic segment, and what that segment's conversion sensitivity to speed has historically looked like) and let them make the final call as the accountable stakeholder — at that point it's a legitimate business trade-off for them to own, not a technical decision for me to make unilaterally.

At what point would you not push back on a similar request at all?

If the page in question were low-traffic or not performance-sensitive by design (an internal-only marketing preview page, say), the same request wouldn't warrant the same pushback — the stakes here came specifically from this being the public homepage carrying real traffic and real conversion sensitivity.

What did the connection-quality fallback trade away?

Some consistency of experience — users on the same page can see meaningfully different heroes (video vs. static poster) depending on their measured connection quality, which is a real design compromise. I judged it worth accepting because a uniformly bad experience for slow-connection users is a worse outcome than an inconsistent-but-locally-optimized one.

How does the Speculation Rules API or the Long Animation Frames API relate to a decision like this one?

Neither directly — Speculation Rules is about prerendering a likely next navigation and Long Animation Frames is a diagnostic API for attributing main-thread jank, and this decision was about avoiding adding the jank-causing cost in the first place. They're complementary tools for a different stage of the same overall performance practice, not alternatives to this specific fix.

What would you do differently now?

Open the very first conversation with "help me understand what feeling you want the hero to create" before I'd said anything about performance cost at all — starting from their goal rather than my objection would likely have reached the same prototype with a shorter, less adversarial path there.

22 Night-before cheat sheet

final review

Ten sentences to read once, out loud, the night before. Not new content — a compression of everything above into what you actually need to remember walking in.

  1. Open every story with the situation in one plain sentence before any detail — the interviewer is oriented in the first five seconds or not at all.
  2. Every story needs a number: a percentile, a millisecond figure, a byte count, a percentage. If you don't have one, say the honest qualitative version instead of inventing a precise-sounding fake.
  3. Know your Core Web Vitals cold: LCP ≤2.5s, INP ≤200ms, CLS ≤0.1, all at the 75th percentile of real-user field data — and that INP replaced FID as the official responsiveness metric in March 2024.
  4. Always answer "what would you do differently" with something specific, not "communicate better" — name the concrete check, default, or policy that should have existed.
  5. Distinguish lab data (Lighthouse, a local trace) from field data (CrUX, real-user monitoring) explicitly — conflating them is a common tell that you haven't actually shipped a fix and watched it land.
  6. Every technical decision had a trade-off; name what you gave up, not just what you gained — a story with no downside sounds rehearsed, not real.
  7. Name the actual constraint you were under (a deadline, a legacy dependency, a non-engineering stakeholder's need) — it's what makes a decision legible instead of arbitrary.
  8. For an incident or security story, state what changed in your process afterward, not just that the immediate issue got fixed — a fix without a process change invites the same question twice.
  9. Keep any API or platform specifics you cite honest about how settled they are — say plainly when something (Speculation Rules, Long Animation Frames) is still evolving rather than presenting it as long-established.
  10. Never fake a story you don't have — say plainly you haven't hit that situation yet and reason out loud how you'd approach it; that reads as more senior than a fabricated example.
