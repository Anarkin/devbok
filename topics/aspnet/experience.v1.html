01The 2pm p99 cliff: thread-pool starvation

tier: senior → staff archetype: performance diagnosis

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

We ran a checkout API on ASP.NET Core 8 behind four Linux containers, each capped at 2 vCPU. Median latency was a boring 40 ms all day. Then, most weekdays around 2pm, p99 went from 180 ms to eleven seconds inside about ninety seconds, held there for four or five minutes, and recovered on its own. CPU never went above 45%. Memory was flat. The database showed no slow queries. Every dashboard said "fine", which is exactly why it took us two weeks to catch.

The thing that cracked it was a metric nobody was looking at. I put dotnet-counters on a live pod during a bad window and watched System.Runtime: threadpool-queue-length was sitting at 1,300 and climbing, threadpool-thread-count was creeping up roughly one thread per second, and cpu-usage stayed at 40%. That combination is the signature of thread-pool starvation, and understanding why it looks like that is the whole story. The .NET thread pool does not add a thread the moment work queues up. Past the minimum count it uses a hill-climbing controller that injects roughly one worker per 500 ms and then measures whether throughput actually improved. That pacing is deliberate — spraying threads at a CPU-bound queue makes things worse — but it means that if you block pool threads on I/O, the pool refills at about two threads a second while requests arrive at three hundred a second. You get a queue that grows far faster than the pool can dig out of, low CPU (the threads are parked, not working), and a self-healing recovery once the burst passes.

The blocking came from one line in a code path added six months earlier. A pricing helper called an internal HTTP service, and because the caller was a synchronous ISomething.GetPrice() interface method that three teams already implemented, the author bridged it with .GetAwaiter().GetResult(). That is sync-over-async: it takes a request thread out of the pool and parks it until a network round trip finishes. Under normal load, with a 15 ms upstream, we had maybe two threads parked at any moment and nobody noticed. At 2pm a batch job in another team's system pushed that upstream's p99 to 900 ms, and suddenly we had eighty threads parked. The pool crossed into starvation and everything queued behind it — including health checks, which is why we started getting spurious pod restarts on top of the latency.

// The line that caused it. Looks harmless in review; it is a thread-pool bomb under load.
public decimal GetPrice(string sku) =>
    _http.GetFromJsonAsync<PriceDto>($"/prices/{sku}").GetAwaiter().GetResult().Amount;

// The fix: make the seam async all the way out. The interface change was the real work.
public async ValueTask<decimal> GetPriceAsync(string sku, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var dto = await _http.GetFromJsonAsync<PriceDto>($"/prices/{sku}", ct);
    return dto!.Amount;
}

I did two things in the same week and I would order them the same way again. First, the stopgap: I raised ThreadPool.SetMinThreads to 200 so the pool would not have to hill-climb its way out of a burst, and added a 250 ms timeout plus a Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience handler on that client so a slow upstream degraded into a cached price instead of a parked thread. That killed the incident inside a day. Then the real fix: I made the interface async, which meant touching three teams' implementations, and I paid for that migration by writing the analyzer rule first — Microsoft.VisualStudio.Threading.Analyzers VSTHRD002 as a build error — so the pattern could not come back while we were still removing it.

What I would do differently: I would not have needed two weeks. We had request-duration histograms and nothing about the runtime itself. Today the first thing I add to any new ASP.NET Core service is the runtime meters — on .NET 10 you get System.Runtime, Microsoft.AspNetCore.Hosting, Microsoft.AspNetCore.Server.Kestrel and, new in 10, Microsoft.AspNetCore.MemoryPool — wired into OpenTelemetry from the first commit, with a queue-length alert. The cost is a dashboard nobody looks at for a year; the payoff is that the year you need it, it is a five-minute diagnosis instead of a two-week hunt.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you diagnose from evidence or from pattern-matching. The tell is the order of your sentences: strong candidates state the symptom, then the measurement that narrowed it, then the cause. Weak ones jump straight to "it was sync-over-async" and cannot say how they knew. The other thing being scored is whether you separate the mitigation from the fix and can justify shipping the mitigation first — that is an on-call judgment, not a purity question.

Mistakes candidates actually make on this archetype: narrating the whole two weeks chronologically instead of leading with the outcome; giving no numbers ("it got much faster"); claiming solo credit for a diagnosis that had three people in the war room; and — very common — describing the fix but never saying how they stopped it recurring.

(c) Recall prompts

When did you last see a .NET service with low CPU and terrible latency at the same time?

That combination is nearly always waiting, not working: blocked pool threads, connection-pool exhaustion (SQL Max Pool Size, default 100), lock contention, or an upstream you did not time out. Which one was yours, and what measurement told you?

Have you ever fixed something with a config knob first and the real cause later?

SetMinThreads, a bigger connection pool, a bumped replica count. Interviewers like this story if you can articulate why the knob was correct as a mitigation and wrong as a solution — and if you actually came back for the real fix.

What is the worst sync-over-async you have found in a codebase you inherited?

.Result, .Wait(), .GetAwaiter().GetResult(), Task.Run(...).Result, an async lambda passed to a sync-only API. Bonus: was it ever defensible? (In a console Main before C# 7.1, or in a legacy ASP.NET Framework app, deadlock risk from the sync context was the bigger worry.)

Which diagnostic tool do you reach for first, and can you say why?

dotnet-counters for live, low-overhead runtime state; dotnet-dump plus dumpasync when you need the stacks of parked work; dotnet-trace for an EventPipe trace you can open in PerfView or Speedscope. Being able to say "counters first because it is cheap and answers the pool question in thirty seconds" is a level signal.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does thread-pool starvation show low CPU? Walk me through the mechanism.

Because the threads are blocked, not running. Once the pool exceeds its minimum thread count, the injection rate is governed by a hill-climbing controller that adds roughly one worker every 500 ms and then measures whether throughput improved. When request threads park on a synchronous wait for I/O, they consume a pool slot without consuming CPU, so the scheduler has nothing to run while the queue grows at request rate. That gives you the classic signature: flat CPU, unbounded threadpool-queue-length, and a thread count creeping up about two per second. It also explains the self-healing — when the upstream burst ends, the parked threads unblock all at once and the queue drains.

You raised SetMinThreads to 200. What did that cost you, and when is it the wrong move?

It costs memory and context switching: each thread reserves 1 MB of stack by default, so 200 minimum threads is a couple of hundred megabytes of virtual address space and a fatter scheduling set, on a 2-vCPU container that only ever runs two things at once. It is the right call when the blocking is genuinely I/O — you want threads parked cheaply until the real fix lands. It is the wrong call when the work is CPU-bound, because you are then oversubscribing cores and making every request slower; and it is a trap if you leave it in forever, because it masks the next sync-over-async someone adds. I treated it as a dated mitigation with a ticket, not a setting.

At what scale does this break even after the async fix?

Async removes the thread as the bottleneck, so the next ceiling is usually the outbound connection pool. SocketsHttpHandler defaults to no limit on HTTP/1.1 connections per server in .NET Core-era runtimes, so unbounded concurrency just moves the pain to the upstream or to ephemeral ports; if you set MaxConnectionsPerServer, you have reintroduced a queue with none of the pool's visibility. The honest answer is that async is a prerequisite for scale, not a scaling strategy: past a few thousand concurrent in-flight calls you need explicit admission control — a concurrency limiter, ASP.NET Core's built-in rate limiting middleware, or a bulkhead in the resilience pipeline — so you shed load deliberately instead of discovering the limit as a timeout storm.

What did you trade away by shipping the mitigation first?

Two weeks of urgency. Once p99 was back to normal, the async interface migration went from "incident work" to "a refactor across three teams", and it got harder to fund. I bought the schedule back by landing the analyzer as a build error in the same PR as the mitigation, so the blast radius could not grow while we negotiated. I would still mitigate first — you do not leave customers in an eleven-second checkout to make a purity point — but I would put a date on the follow-up in the incident review, in writing, before the room emptied.

How would you have caught this in code review?

Review does not reliably catch it, which is why I stopped relying on it. .GetAwaiter().GetResult() reads as defensive rather than dangerous, and the reviewer would have to know the call site sits on a request thread. The durable answer is mechanical: the threading analyzers as build errors, an architecture test that fails if a synchronous interface is implemented by something that takes an HttpClient, and — the part people forget — load testing that actually holds concurrency rather than firing a fixed number of requests, because starvation only appears when arrival rate exceeds the injection rate.

Would anything about this be different on .NET 10?

The mechanism is identical — the pool still hill-climbs, sync-over-async still parks a thread. What has improved is the time to diagnose: the ASP.NET Core and runtime meters are richer and OpenTelemetry-native, so queue length and request duration land in the same backend without a custom exporter, and .NET 10 adds a Microsoft.AspNetCore.MemoryPool meter that would have told us the pooled buffers were fine. .NET 11's runtime-async work changes how async methods suspend and resume at the JIT level, which reduces the per-await overhead, but it does not save you from a blocking call: nothing in the runtime can un-block a thread you told to wait.

02Modular monolith vs. services: the call I owned

tier: staff → principal archetype: architectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A twenty-engineer product group had one ASP.NET Core application: 340k lines, one deployment, one database, forty-minute release. The pitch on the table was to split it into nine services along the domain seams we had already drawn in code. I was asked to own the recommendation. I recommended we not do it, and instead spend two quarters turning it into a genuinely modular monolith with one exception carved out as a service. That call was unpopular for about six weeks and correct for about three years, and the reasoning is the part worth rehearsing, not the outcome.

The evidence I actually gathered: deployment coupling, not code coupling, was the pain. Releases were slow because a forty-minute pipeline ran 6,000 tests serially and required a manual DBA sign-off, and because two of the nine modules — pricing and notifications — changed weekly while the rest changed monthly. Splitting into nine services would have addressed a code-ownership problem we did not have and left the pipeline problem untouched, while adding nine sets of the things distribution actually costs: retries and idempotency at every boundary, distributed tracing to answer questions a stack trace used to answer, schema versioning on wire contracts, and nine on-call rotations for a group that had one.

The thing I leaned on technically is that ASP.NET Core is unusually good at the middle option, and that is worth being able to say precisely. A module boundary in-process can be made real with an assembly per module, an internal-by-default surface plus InternalsVisibleTo only for that module's tests, one IServiceCollection extension per module registering its own services, and an architecture test that fails the build when module A references module B's types outside its published contract. Cross-module calls go through an in-process mediator with the same request/response shape a network call would have, so the day you do extract one, the call site does not change — you swap the handler for a typed HttpClient or a gRPC client. That is the cheap option value: you keep the ability to split without paying for the split.

// One composition root per module. The boundary is enforced by the build, not by discipline.
public static class PricingModule
{
    public static IServiceCollection AddPricing(this IServiceCollection services, IConfiguration config)
    {
        services.AddDbContext<PricingDbContext>(o => o.UseNpgsql(config.GetConnectionString("Pricing")));
        services.AddScoped<IPriceQuery, PriceQuery>();   // internal impl, public contract
        return services;
    }

    public static IEndpointRouteBuilder MapPricing(this IEndpointRouteBuilder app)
    {
        var g = app.MapGroup("/pricing").WithTags("Pricing").RequireAuthorization();
        g.MapGet("/{sku}", (string sku, IPriceQuery q, CancellationToken ct) => q.GetAsync(sku, ct));
        return app;
    }
}

The one exception was notifications: it had a genuinely different scaling profile (bursty, 50× traffic during campaigns), a different failure tolerance (best-effort), and a different release rhythm. That is what actually justifies a service — an independent axis of scale, failure, or change — not "the domain diagram has a line here". We extracted it, and because the call site was already a mediator request, the extraction PR was 400 lines.

What I would do differently: I framed the decision as "monolith vs. microservices", which invited an identity argument. The second time I ran this, I framed it as "which of these four axes — deploy cadence, scale profile, failure tolerance, team ownership — actually differs per module?" and put the answer in a table before anyone said the word microservice. Same conclusion, a third of the meetings, and two engineers who had been arguing on principle turned out to be arguing about the forty-minute pipeline all along.

What the interviewer is really probing

At staff and above, this question is not "do you know the trade-offs" — everyone recites them. It is whether you can name the specific evidence that would flip your own decision, and whether you optimised for reversibility. The strongest answers include a decision you would now make differently and a boundary you deliberately left open.

Mistakes candidates make: arguing the general case instead of their case; presenting the outcome as inevitable; no mention of what it cost the people who disagreed; and — the one that ends senior-plus loops — being unable to say what would have made services the right call.

(b) STAR/CARL scaffold

  • SituationSize the system in one line: team count, LOC or service count, deploy cadence, and the pain that put the decision on the table.
  • TaskWere you the decider, the recommender, or the person who had to execute someone else's call? Be exact — inflating this is the fastest way to fail the drill-down.
  • ActionThe evidence you gathered, the options you considered (name three, not two), the criteria you scored them on, and how you brought the dissenters along.
  • ResultWhat measurably changed: deploy frequency, lead time, incident rate, onboarding time. Plus what you deliberately did not get.
  • DifferentlyWhere the reasoning was weakest, and what evidence you would collect first next time.

(c) Recall prompts

Where did you last draw a boundary, and what enforced it after you left?

Enforcement is the tell. Assembly references, internal plus InternalsVisibleTo, an architecture test, a separate schema per module, an ArchUnitNET or NetArchTest rule in CI. "We agreed to keep it clean" is not a boundary.

Have you ever recommended against the exciting option?

These stories score well precisely because they are unpopular. What matters is that you can state the conditions under which you would have said yes, so it does not read as conservatism.

Which distributed-systems cost surprised you most in practice?

Common honest answers: the first time a retry created a duplicate charge; discovering nobody owned the contract; the day a stack trace stopped being enough; the cost of running nine sets of dashboards, alerts and rotations for a team of twenty.

Which module in a system you know is genuinely different on scale, failure tolerance, or cadence?

That module is your extraction story. If you cannot name one, your honest answer is that the split was organisational, which is a legitimate reason — but say so rather than dressing it as technical.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just split it? The team wanted to.

Because the pain they were describing was deployment latency, and a split does not fix a forty-minute pipeline — it gives you nine of them. I measured what people actually waited on: 6,000 serial tests, a manual DBA gate, and two modules changing on a different rhythm from the other seven. Parallelising the suite and automating the schema gate took the pipeline to nine minutes in six weeks, which addressed 80% of the complaint at maybe 5% of the cost. I did commit to extracting notifications, because that one had a real independent scale axis. The general principle I hold is that distribution is a solution to organisational and scaling problems, and you should be able to name which one you have before you pay for it.

What would have changed your mind?

Three things, and I said them at the time so the decision was falsifiable. If team count had gone past roughly thirty engineers with genuinely separate roadmaps, the coordination cost of one artefact would have exceeded the distribution cost. If two modules had needed materially different availability targets — one at four nines with the other allowed to fail — sharing a process becomes indefensible. And if we had needed to scale one module's compute by 10× independently, vertical scaling of the whole app gets expensive fast. None of those were true at the time; two became true later, and we extracted two more modules then, cheaply, because the seams were real.

At what scale does the modular monolith break?

Three places, in my experience. Deploy contention: past roughly forty engineers on one artefact, the release train becomes the constraint and every incident blocks everyone. Failure isolation: one module with a runaway allocation or a blocking call takes the whole process down, and no amount of module hygiene changes that — it is one GC heap and one thread pool. And data: one database is a gift until two modules need conflicting storage, at which point you are running a monolith with a polyglot persistence layer, which is the worst of both. The signal I watch is not LOC, it is how often two teams' changes block each other in a release.

How did you get the people who disagreed to actually commit?

I stopped arguing about the answer and got agreement on the criteria first — deploy cadence, scale profile, failure tolerance, ownership — in a session where nobody was allowed to name an architecture. Once we scored the nine modules against those four axes, one module stood out and eight did not, and the conclusion was the group's rather than mine. The two strongest dissenters got the notifications extraction to own, which mattered: giving people the real work rather than a consolation is how you keep dissent honest. I also wrote the decision record with their objections in it, verbatim, and the conditions that would reopen it.

What did you trade away?

Failure isolation, mainly — and it bit us. Eighteen months later an unbounded IMemoryCache in one module drove the container into a memory limit and killed all nine modules with it. In a service split, that would have been one degraded feature. I traded that away knowingly for a much lower coordination and operational cost, and I would make the same trade, but I would not describe it as free. The mitigation I should have shipped earlier was per-module resource discipline — bounded caches with size limits, and a memory pressure alert per module rather than per process.

Would you make the same call today, on .NET 10?

More confidently, yes, because the middle option has gotten better. .NET Aspire makes a multi-project composition genuinely runnable on a laptop, which historically was the strongest practical argument for staying in one process; that argument is weaker now. On the other side, the modular-monolith path is better supported too — minimal API route groups per module keep the composition root readable, and the source-generated validation in ASP.NET Core 10 makes the module contract enforceable at the edge without reflection. My criteria have not changed; the cost of both options has come down, so the decision is more reversible than it was, which is an argument for deciding later and cheaper.

03Outage: SocketException at 60k connections

tier: senior → staff archetype: production incident

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A Friday evening, forty minutes into a marketing push. An order API started returning 500s at about 8% of requests, climbing. The exception in the logs was SocketException: Cannot assign requested address wrapped in an HttpRequestException from an outbound call to a payment gateway. Restarting a pod fixed it for roughly nine minutes, then it came back. That "restart buys you ten minutes" shape is the fingerprint of resource exhaustion with a slow refill, and it is what let us skip a lot of hypotheses.

The cause was new HttpClient(...) inside a request handler, in a using block, because someone had correctly noticed HttpClient implements IDisposable and incorrectly concluded it should be disposed per use. Disposing an HttpClient disposes its handler, which closes the sockets; a closed TCP socket sits in TIME_WAIT for 60 seconds on Linux by default before its ephemeral port can be reused. At 400 outbound calls per second against one gateway host we were burning ports faster than the kernel returned them, and the ephemeral range (roughly 28k ports on that image) ran dry. Hence the nine minutes: a restart clears the process, but the kernel's TIME_WAIT table is per-host, so the recovery was really just the burst pausing.

The mitigation was a one-line ratio change on the load balancer to shed 30% of traffic to a static "try again shortly" page while we shipped, and the fix was IHttpClientFactory. The part I make sure I can explain, because it is the actual concept, is why the factory is not just a DI convenience: it pools SocketsHttpHandler instances and rotates them on a two-minute default lifetime. A single long-lived static HttpClient also avoids port exhaustion, but it caches DNS forever — so when the gateway failed over to a new IP, a static client would have kept dialling a dead address. The factory's handler rotation is what gets you both properties at once. If you want a single client without the factory, the modern equivalent is setting PooledConnectionLifetime on the handler yourself.

// Wrong in two directions: a new client per request exhausts ports; a static one pins DNS.
using var client = new HttpClient { BaseAddress = new Uri(gatewayUrl) };

// Modern: typed client + factory. Handler pooling and rotation come for free.
builder.Services.AddHttpClient<PaymentClient>(c =>
{
    c.BaseAddress = new Uri(builder.Configuration["Payments:BaseUrl"]!);
    c.Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5);
})
.ConfigurePrimaryHttpMessageHandler(() => new SocketsHttpHandler
{
    PooledConnectionLifetime = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(2),   // DNS refresh
    MaxConnectionsPerServer  = 200                        // explicit bulkhead
})
.AddStandardResilienceHandler();   // Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience: retry, breaker, timeout

Two hours to mitigate, one deploy to fix, and one honest failure on my part in the incident review: our alerting was on HTTP 5xx rate, which fired only after customers were already failing. The leading indicator — outbound connection count, or the socket exception count — existed and was not alerted on. What I changed afterwards was not "add an alert on this exception"; it was to make the outbound HTTP client metrics part of the service template, so every new service got connection-pool visibility without anyone deciding to add it.

What the interviewer is really probing

Incident command, not debugging skill. They want to hear that you stabilised before you understood, that you can say who did what while you were coordinating, and that the review produced a systemic change rather than a ticket. At staff-plus they also listen for whether you resisted the temptation to keep debugging while customers were down.

Mistakes candidates make: no timeline (mitigation at what minute?); heroic solo framing on what was obviously a team incident; blaming the engineer who wrote the using; and an action item that is really just "we'll be more careful", which tells the interviewer nothing changed.

  • SituationImpact first, in customer terms and one number: "8% of orders failing, climbing, during a promotion."
  • TaskYour role in the incident: commander, primary responder, or the person who happened to know the subsystem. Name it plainly.
  • ActionStabilise → diagnose → fix, in that order, with the clock attached. Include the hypothesis you killed and how.

(c) Recall prompts

What is the most misleading error message you have chased in production?

SocketException that was really port exhaustion; TaskCanceledException that was really a timeout, not a cancellation; a 502 from a proxy that was really your app's graceful-shutdown window; ObjectDisposedException on a DbContext that was really a fire-and-forget async void.

When did a restart "fix" something and what did that tell you?

Restart-heals means accumulating state: a leak, an exhausted pool, a poisoned cache, a stuck circuit breaker. How long the fix lasted is a measurement — it gives you the accumulation rate.

Have you ever been the one who shed load deliberately?

Turning off a feature flag, serving stale cache, rate-limiting a tenant, failing a non-critical dependency open. Interviewers value this because it shows you optimise for the customer's outcome rather than for correctness during an incident.

What did your last incident review actually change?

The good answers are structural: a template change, a default in a shared library, a CI check, a paging rule. If your honest answer is "a ticket that never got done", say that and what you learned — it is more credible than a tidy fiction.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why exactly does creating an HttpClient per request exhaust ports, when GC would clean it up anyway?

Because the exhaustion is in the kernel, not the heap. Disposing the client disposes its SocketsHttpHandler, which closes the connection; the local ephemeral port then sits in TIME_WAIT for roughly 60 seconds so that late-arriving packets from the old connection cannot be misdelivered to a new one. The ephemeral range is finite — commonly about 28,000 ports — so a sustained rate of a few hundred new connections per second against one destination outruns the reclaim rate regardless of how promptly the managed object is collected. Not disposing is even worse in a different way: the handler is finalized non-deterministically, so you get the same socket churn with less predictability.

Then why not one static HttpClient for the process? That solves ports.

It does, and for years that was the advice — but it trades port exhaustion for a DNS problem. A single handler holds its connections open indefinitely, and .NET resolves the name when the connection is established, so a static client keeps talking to an IP that may have been failed away hours ago. That is a nasty failure mode because it only appears during someone else's failover. IHttpClientFactory solves both by pooling handlers and retiring them on a lifetime (two minutes by default), so connections are reused but names get re-resolved. If you genuinely want one client, the equivalent is to set PooledConnectionLifetime on the handler yourself — that is the modern, factory-free answer.

At what scale does even the factory approach break?

Two ceilings. First, HTTP/1.1 concurrency: connections are not multiplexed, so concurrency is bounded by connections per server, and if you leave that unbounded you shift the failure to the upstream; if you bound it, you have a hidden queue whose depth nobody is watching. Moving that hop to HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 changes the picture, because one connection multiplexes many streams — at which point the limit becomes the peer's MAX_CONCURRENT_STREAMS. Second, the resilience policy itself: naive retries multiply load exactly when the upstream is already struggling. That is why the standard resilience handler pairs retry with a circuit breaker and a total-request timeout rather than shipping retry alone.

What did you trade away with the load-shedding decision?

Roughly 30% of a promotion's traffic, deliberately, for about ninety minutes — real revenue, and I made that call without asking marketing first because the alternative was an error rate climbing through 8% with no ceiling. What I traded for was a stable system to deploy into and a diagnosis done on a service that was not on fire. I told the stakeholders inside ten minutes, in writing, with the reasoning and the expected duration. If I could redo one thing, it is that the shed threshold should have been a pre-agreed runbook decision with a named owner, not a judgment call at 8pm on a Friday.

How would you catch this before production now?

Load testing that holds sustained concurrency rather than firing a fixed count — port exhaustion needs about a minute at rate to appear, so a short burst test passes cleanly. Beyond that: an analyzer or review rule against new HttpClient outside composition roots, the outbound metrics in the service template so connection counts are visible by default, and — the highest-leverage one — a shared internal package that registers typed clients with sensible handler lifetimes and a resilience pipeline, so the correct thing is also the least typing.

Anything about this specific to a recent .NET version?

The primitives are stable, but the packaging has consolidated. Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience with AddStandardResilienceHandler() is now the recommended path and is built on Polly v8 pipelines — that replaced the older AddTransientHttpErrorPolicy style you will still meet in existing codebases, and hand-rolled retry loops from the .NET Core 2.x era. On the diagnostics side, the System.Net.Http and System.Net.NameResolution meters are standardised, so connection duration and DNS lookups land in OpenTelemetry without custom code — exactly the leading indicator we were missing.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why strangler rather than a parallel rewrite you cut over once?

Because a big-bang cutover concentrates all the risk on one night, and with 210k lines of eleven-year-old behaviour, nobody in the building could enumerate what "working" meant. Strangler let each route group carry its own small risk with a thirty-second rollback, so a bad migration cost us one feature for an hour instead of the business for a day. It also meant we kept shipping features throughout, which is what bought the political runway for three quarters. The cost is real: you run two stacks, two deploy pipelines and a proxy hop for the duration, and you must solve session and auth sharing up front — which is exactly the piece I under-planned.

How did you keep users logged in across two different frameworks?

Shared ASP.NET Core Data Protection: both apps point at the same key ring on a shared path and set the same application name, so the purpose strings that derive the encryption key match and either app can read the other's auth cookie. The Framework side needed the Microsoft.Owin.Security.Interop shim so its cookie format matched what Core expects. The failure modes here are unpleasant and worth naming: if the key ring is not shared, keys are generated per machine and every deploy or scale-out logs users out; if the application name differs by a character, decryption fails silently and reads as an expired cookie. We tested it by scaling to three instances of each app and hammering a login-then-navigate loop.

At what point does the strangler approach stop being worth it?

When the shared state between the two halves is bigger than the halves. Ours worked because the boundary was HTTP routes and one auth cookie. If two systems had to share an in-memory cache, a distributed transaction, or a mutable session object per request, the coordination cost overtakes the rewrite risk and you are better off with a hard cutover on a quiet weekend plus a tested restore. The other stopping condition is time: a strangler that runs longer than about four quarters tends to become permanent, because "finish the migration" never beats a roadmap item. That is why I put a delete date on the legacy app in the plan, with a named owner.

What did you trade away?

Three quarters of four engineers, and a proxy hop that added about 3 ms to every request for the duration — plus a genuine cost I did not anticipate: for two quarters, every bug had two possible homes, and the team's diagnostic instinct got slower. We paid that down by making the YARP routing table the first thing on the incident runbook, so the first question was always "which side served this?". I would still make the trade; I would budget for the cognitive cost explicitly and rotate people so nobody became the only person who understood the seam.

How did you decide what "done" meant?

Not "all code migrated" — that framing is what makes migrations run forever. We defined done as "the Windows VMs are decommissioned and the legacy repository is archived", which is a cost outcome the finance side could verify, and it forced the deletion conversations to happen. Traffic percentage was a progress metric, not the goal. That reframing is also what made it acceptable to rebuild eleven report pages and delete 169 of them: against "migrate everything" that is a failure; against "turn off the VMs" it is the fastest path.

If you started this migration today, what would you target and why?

.NET 10, without hesitation — it is the current LTS, GA in November 2025, supported until November 2028, so a migration finishing in 2027 does not land on a runtime that is already halfway through its life. I would specifically avoid landing on .NET 9: it is STS and goes out of support in November 2026, which would mean doing the upgrade dance again immediately. I would also use the upgrade tooling as an accelerator rather than a decision-maker — it is good at the mechanical .csproj and API-swap work and has no opinion at all about which 169 pages you should delete, which is where the value actually was.

05Disagreement: Native AOT vs. JIT for a hot service

tier: staff archetype: technical disagreement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A colleague I respected proposed publishing our highest-traffic gateway service — an auth-token exchange doing about 4,000 rps across twelve pods — as Native AOT. His case was strong on paper: 30 ms cold start instead of 900 ms, a 38 MB container instead of 190 MB, and about 40 MB of resident memory instead of 130 MB. We had a real cold-start problem during scale-out events. I disagreed, we argued in a design review for an hour and got nowhere, and the way out was that I stopped arguing and asked what measurement would settle it.

My objection was about steady-state throughput, and the reason is worth stating precisely because it is the concept the whole disagreement turns on. Native AOT compiles ahead of time and ships machine code — no JIT at runtime, so no startup compilation, but also no tiered compilation. RyuJIT starts methods at tier 0 (fast to compile, slow code), counts invocations, and re-compiles hot methods at tier 1 with full optimisation including dynamic PGO, which since .NET 8 is on by default and uses runtime profile data — actual type distributions at call sites — to guide devirtualisation and inlining. AOT cannot do that: it has static profile guesses only. So AOT's shape is "fast immediately, then flat", and JIT's is "slow for a second, then faster than AOT can be at steady state". For a pod that lives for hours, the first second is a rounding error.

We ran the benchmark on our real workload. Startup: AOT won overwhelmingly, as predicted. Sustained throughput after a sixty-second warmup: JIT was about 8% ahead on p50 and about 12% ahead on p99. Neither of us had predicted 12%, and it changed the shape of the conversation from opinion to arithmetic — 12% of twelve pods is a pod and a half, against a cold-start problem that cost us roughly ninety seconds of degraded capacity a few times a week.

The resolution was neither of our positions. We kept the gateway on JIT and fixed cold start where it actually hurt, with two changes: ReadyToRun publishing, which pre-compiles to a portable native format so tier-0 JIT work mostly disappears at startup while keeping the JIT available to re-optimise hot paths, and raising the pod's startup CPU allocation so the warmup was not competing with request traffic. Cold start went from 900 ms to about 210 ms — not AOT's 30 ms, but well under the health-check window, which is the number that mattered. And my colleague took Native AOT to two places where it was genuinely right: a queue-consumer sidecar that scaled to zero, and our internal CLI, whose 900 ms startup every engineer paid for dozens of times a day.

The disagreement was not really technical — we were optimising different metrics and had never said so out loud. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is JIT faster than AOT at steady state? That is counter-intuitive.

Because the JIT knows things a compiler cannot. Tiered compilation starts a method in tier 0 to get running quickly, counts calls, and re-jits hot methods at tier 1 with full optimisation. Since .NET 8, dynamic PGO is on by default, so those tier-1 compilations are guided by real runtime profile data: which concrete type actually shows up at an interface call site, which branch actually gets taken. That lets it devirtualise and inline through abstractions that AOT must leave as indirect calls, because AOT only has static guesses. AOT wins where the run is short enough that warmup dominates, or where memory and image size matter more than throughput.

Then what makes Native AOT worth it at all?

Three things that JIT structurally cannot give you. Startup measured in tens of milliseconds, which is decisive for scale-to-zero and serverless where you pay per invocation and cold starts are user-visible. A much smaller resident set — no JIT, no IL metadata it would need — which changes pod density and therefore cost. And a self-contained native binary with no runtime to install, which matters for CLI distribution and constrained images. ASP.NET Core has narrowed the gap on usability, too: the Web API (Native AOT) template ships with OpenAPI support in .NET 10, and the source-generated validation introduced in ASP.NET Core 10 replaced a reflection-based implementation specifically so minimal APIs validate cleanly under AOT.

What breaks under Native AOT that people discover late?

Anything that reflects or emits at runtime. Reflection-based serialization is the classic — you move to System.Text.Json source generation with a JsonSerializerContext. Dynamic proxy generation breaks, which takes out several mocking and interception libraries and some ORM lazy-loading. Assembly.LoadFrom-style plugin models are gone. Then there is the operational half people never budget for: reduced diagnostic surface — profiler attach and Edit-and-Continue are limited, and your dump-analysis muscle memory changes. The build is also slower and needs a native toolchain per target platform, so cross-compiling in CI becomes a real piece of work rather than a flag.

At what scale would you revisit the decision?

Two triggers. If the service moved to a scale-to-zero platform — a per-invocation billing model where cold start is on the critical path of a user request rather than of a deployment — the arithmetic inverts completely and AOT wins outright. And if memory per pod became the binding constraint rather than CPU, since a 3× smaller resident set changes pod density more than 12% CPU does. The measurement I would want before revisiting is the ratio of request-serving time to process lifetime; when that drops below roughly 50%, you are paying for a runtime you barely use.

What did you concede?

That cold start was a real production problem and I had been treating it as an annoyance. His pressure is why we fixed it at all — my instinct was to leave it. I also conceded the framing: I had been arguing "AOT is not suitable here" when the honest position was "AOT is not suitable for this service, and I have not checked the others." That is what led to the sidecar and the CLI, both of which were his wins. I make a point of telling the story with those in it, because a disagreement where the other person's idea shipped somewhere is the only kind that leaves the team better.

How would you run this disagreement differently with a team that had no benchmarking culture?

I would spend the first hour building the harness rather than arguing, and make it crude on purpose — a load generator, sixty seconds of warmup, p50 and p99 out of the existing histograms. The goal is not benchmark rigour, it is converting a values argument into a numbers argument fast enough that nobody has entrenched. Then I would leave the harness in the repository, because the durable outcome is not this decision, it is that the next one costs twenty minutes. Teams that argue about performance in the abstract usually do so because measuring is expensive; make measuring cheap and the arguments mostly evaporate.

06The leak that was a captive dependency

tier: senior → staff archetype: nasty debugging session

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Pods on a document-processing API were being OOM-killed every eleven to fourteen hours against a 1 GB memory limit. Working set climbed steadily from 180 MB after start, sawtoothing upward, and no single deployment correlated with the start of it. Three days of my life, and the interesting part is that two of them were spent proving what it wasn't.

First measurement, always: is this managed or native? dotnet-counters monitor --counters System.Runtime showed gc-heap-size tracking working set closely, so it was managed — if working set had grown while the GC heap stayed flat, I would have been looking at native memory, unmanaged handles, or pinned buffers instead. Second: is it a leak or is it the GC being lazy? That distinction matters more than people think, because on .NET 9 and later, DATAS is enabled by default for Server GC and deliberately keeps the heap proportional to live data, adjusting as workload changes; a heap that grows and never shrinks under DATAS is a real signal, whereas on older Server GC a large flat heap often just meant "there is memory available, why give it back".

The actual method was two dumps and a diff. dotnet-dump collect at hour one and hour six, then in dotnet-dump analyze, dumpheap -stat on both and compare. One type had gone from 4,100 instances to 61,000: our DocumentContext, an EF Core DbContext. gcroot on a random instance walked up through a ConcurrentDictionary to a singleton. There it was: a class registered as a singleton had taken an IServiceProvider — sorry, worse, it had taken a DocumentContext directly in its constructor.

// The bug. A singleton capturing a scoped service pins it — and everything it tracks — forever.
public sealed class TemplateCache
{
    private readonly DocumentContext _db;                 // scoped, captured by a singleton
    private readonly ConcurrentDictionary<string, Template> _cache = new();
    public TemplateCache(DocumentContext db) => _db = db;
}
builder.Services.AddSingleton<TemplateCache>();            // captive dependency

// The fix: resolve a scope per use, and keep the cached values as detached DTOs.
public sealed class TemplateCache(IServiceScopeFactory scopes)
{
    private readonly ConcurrentDictionary<string, TemplateDto> _cache = new();
    public async ValueTask<TemplateDto> GetAsync(string key, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        if (_cache.TryGetValue(key, out var hit)) return hit;
        await using var scope = scopes.CreateAsyncScope();
        var db = scope.ServiceProvider.GetRequiredService<DocumentContext>();
        var dto = await db.Templates.AsNoTracking().Where(t => t.Key == key)
                          .Select(t => new TemplateDto(t.Key, t.Body)).SingleAsync(ct);
        return _cache.GetOrAdd(key, dto);
    }
}

The mechanism, said properly, is the thing a drill-down will chase. A singleton lives for the process; a scoped service lives for one request. When the singleton's constructor takes the scoped service, the container resolves one instance from the root scope and the singleton holds a strong reference to it for the process lifetime. A DbContext has a change tracker that keeps a strong reference to every entity it has loaded, so what looked like one leaked object was a root for an ever-growing object graph. Generational GC makes it worse in the sense that being reachable from a gen-2 singleton means these objects are promoted and then never collected — the collector is doing exactly its job, which is why a "leak" in .NET is almost always an unintended reference, not a failure to free.

The fix was 40 lines. The prevention was more valuable: ValidateScopes and ValidateOnBuild turned on in all environments. The default host enables scope validation in Development only, which is precisely why this shipped — it had been caught locally by nobody, because the singleton was constructed lazily on first use, and in dev nobody used it before the app restarted.

builder.Host.UseDefaultServiceProvider((ctx, o) =>
{
    o.ValidateScopes  = true;   // throws on captive dependencies — in every environment, not just Development
    o.ValidateOnBuild = true;   // fail fast at startup rather than on first resolution
});

Method under uncertainty. They want to hear a hypothesis loop — measure, narrow, discard — not a lucky guess. The strongest signal is that you can say what each measurement ruled out. Second signal: you turned a bug fix into a class-of-bug fix.

Mistakes candidates make: telling it as a mystery story with the reveal at the end (lead with the cause, then explain the hunt); no tool names; "I added logging" as the entire method; and skipping the two days of dead ends, which are the part that demonstrates rigour.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the GC not collect those DbContext instances? Is that not what it is for?

The GC collects unreachable objects, and these were reachable. The singleton is a root that lives for the process; it held a dictionary that transitively held the contexts, and each context's change tracker holds strong references to every entity it loaded. So the collector was correct at every step — this is why "memory leak" in .NET almost always means "unintended reference", not "failure to free". The generational design amplifies the symptom: objects surviving collections get promoted to gen 2, which is collected rarely, so the heap grows in a sawtooth that never returns to baseline. Reading the sawtooth's floor rather than its peak is the trick — a rising floor is a leak; a rising peak alone is just allocation rate.

How would you tell a managed leak from a native one?

Compare working set against gc-heap-size in dotnet-counters. If they track each other, it is managed and a heap diff will find it. If working set climbs while the GC heap is flat, the memory is outside the collector's world: unmanaged handles from an interop library, pinned buffers or an ArrayPool that is being rented and not returned, native allocations in a compression or crypto library, or fragmentation on the loader heap from dynamic assembly generation. On Linux I would also check whether the process is being charged for page cache by the cgroup limit, because containers OOM on the cgroup accounting, not on your heap. .NET 10 helps here specifically: the new Microsoft.AspNetCore.MemoryPool meter makes pooled-buffer behaviour visible, and the pool now evicts memory when load drops, which used to look like a slow native leak.

At what scale would this bug have gone unnoticed forever?

Any deployment cadence faster than the leak rate — which is exactly how it hid for months. We deployed twice a week early on and the pods never lived long enough to hit the limit; the leak only became visible when a feature freeze stretched uptime past two weeks. That is a general lesson I now apply: frequent deploys mask slow leaks, so a change in release cadence is a change in your risk profile. It is also an argument for running a soak test at a fraction of production traffic for 48 hours before a freeze, which we added afterwards and which caught a different, smaller leak within a quarter.

What did you trade away by turning scope validation on everywhere?

Startup time and a bit of risk. ValidateOnBuild constructs the whole graph eagerly, which added about 300 ms to startup on a service with a few hundred registrations — irrelevant for a long-lived pod, but I would think twice on a scale-to-zero function where startup is the product. The risk is that it converts a latent misconfiguration into a hard startup failure, so the first deploy after enabling it can fail loudly. I consider that a feature — I would much rather fail at startup than OOM at 4am — but you introduce it on a quiet day, not the afternoon before a freeze, and you check the other services first.

Two of the three days were dead ends. What were they and would you skip them now?

Day one I was convinced it was EF Core change tracking on a bulk import path, because that is the leak I had seen most recently — availability bias. I spent it adding AsNoTracking() in the wrong places and proved nothing. Day two I chased a native hypothesis after misreading a working-set graph that had a different y-axis scale from the heap graph. I would skip both now by doing the two-dump diff first: it is about twenty minutes of work and it answers "what is actually growing" without requiring me to guess. My rule since then is that any leak investigation past one hour starts with a heap diff, not a hypothesis.

Would DATAS have changed this diagnosis?

It would have made it faster to spot, and it changes the baseline you compare against. DATAS — dynamic adaptation to application sizes — shipped opt-in in .NET 8 and became the default for Server GC in .NET 9, with further tuning in .NET 10; it sizes the heap roughly proportional to live data and gives memory back when the workload lightens. Under classic Server GC, a big heap on a big machine was normal and told you nothing, so the leak hid in the noise. Under DATAS, a heap that only ever grows is a much cleaner signal, because the collector is actively trying to shrink it and failing. The flip side, which I would say in the same breath, is that DATAS is tuned for memory over raw throughput; if you are chasing maximum throughput on a dedicated box, System.GC.DynamicAdaptationMode is the knob, and you should measure rather than assume.

07Scaling SignalR past one node

tier: staff archetype: scaling challenge

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A live collaboration feature — presence, cursors, and document updates — worked beautifully on one node with 400 concurrent users and fell apart the week we scaled to three nodes for a customer with 9,000. Two distinct failures arrived together, which made it look like one confusing problem: users stopped seeing each other's updates roughly two-thirds of the time, and reconnect storms took the pods down every few minutes.

The first failure is the one everyone can predict once they see it. A SignalR hub keeps its connections in memory on the node that owns them, so Clients.Group("doc-42").SendAsync(...) reaches only the connections on that node. With three nodes and no backplane, two-thirds of the group never hears you. The fix is a backplane — we used Redis — but the concept to be precise about is what a backplane actually does: it turns every hub send into a publish on a Redis channel that every node subscribes to and re-fans-out locally. That means your Redis instance now sees every message, so it becomes both a single point of failure and a throughput ceiling, and message ordering is only guaranteed per-publisher.

The second failure was subtler and cost more time. Our transport was falling back to long polling for about a third of clients because a corporate proxy stripped the WebSocket upgrade headers. Long polling with sticky sessions off means each poll can land on a different node, and SignalR's connection state is node-local, so the client would get a 404 on its next poll and reconnect — 3,000 clients doing that in a loop is a self-inflicted denial of service. The correct configuration is either sticky sessions at the load balancer, or SkipNegotiation with WebSockets forced, which requires that WebSockets actually work end to end. We did both: enabled sticky sessions, and made the transport fallback visible so we knew when clients were degraded rather than finding out from a support ticket.

builder.Services.AddSignalR(o =>
    {
        o.MaximumReceiveMessageSize = 64 * 1024;
        o.ClientTimeoutInterval     = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);
        o.KeepAliveInterval         = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(10);   // must be <= half the client timeout
    })
    .AddMessagePackProtocol()                      // ~40% smaller frames than JSON on our payloads
    .AddStackExchangeRedisBackplane(o => o.Configuration.ChannelPrefix =
        StackExchange.Redis.RedisChannel.Literal("collab"));

The change that actually made it scale, though, was not infrastructure — it was reducing what we sent. Cursor movements were being broadcast per mouse event, roughly 30 per second per user, to everyone in the document. At 9,000 users that is arithmetic nobody should accept: we were asking a backplane to carry hundreds of thousands of messages a second to deliver information no human can perceive at that rate. We coalesced cursor updates to 10 Hz per user on the client and moved to MessagePack instead of JSON, which together cut backplane traffic by about 92%. The document updates — the messages that actually mattered — were a rounding error by comparison.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does a backplane become the bottleneck? Explain the arithmetic.

Because it linearises fan-out. Without a backplane, a send to a group of n local connections is n writes on one node. With a backplane, every send is one publish to Redis plus a subscribe-side delivery to every node, and each node then does its own local fan-out — so Redis carries the full message rate of the entire cluster regardless of how many nodes you add. Adding nodes increases the publish rate and does not increase Redis's capacity. That is why the ceiling is messages per second, not connections, and why the winning move is almost always to reduce the message rate at the source. Above a certain scale you stop fanning out through one channel entirely and shard by group, or move to a service designed for it.

Why did long polling without sticky sessions fail, exactly?

SignalR's connection state — the connection id, its groups, its pending messages — lives in memory on the node that handled the negotiate. With long polling, each poll is a separate HTTP request, so a round-robin balancer sends the next poll to a node that has never heard of that connection id and returns a 404. The client treats that as a dropped connection and reconnects, which re-negotiates, which lands somewhere else again. At three thousand affected clients that is a reconnect storm that costs more than the traffic it was carrying. The fixes are sticky sessions, or forcing WebSockets so there is exactly one long-lived request per connection and the affinity problem disappears. Sticky sessions have their own cost: they defeat even load distribution and make node drains during deploys much more disruptive.

At what scale does the Redis backplane approach stop working?

In our shape — small messages, high rate — the practical ceiling was in the low hundreds of thousands of messages per second before Redis CPU became the constraint, and long before that the p99 delivery latency starts drifting in a way users feel as lag. The signals I would watch are Redis CPU, the pub/sub buffer, and delivery latency percentiles rather than averages. Past that you have three options: shard the backplane by group so no single Redis sees all traffic, move to a managed SignalR service that does the fan-out for you and bills per connection, or reconsider whether a hub is the right primitive at all — some of these problems are better served by a log the clients tail than by a broadcast.

What did you trade away with the 10 Hz cursor coalescing?

Fidelity, and it was noticed. Two power users on the design team reported that cursors felt "slightly rubbery" during fast drags, and they were right — we went from about 30 Hz to 10 Hz. We bought that back partly by interpolating between updates on the client, which is a rendering trick rather than a networking one and costs nothing on the wire. The trade I would defend is that we bought a working product for 9,000 users at the cost of a perceptible-but-tolerable degradation for a handful; the trade I would criticise is that I shipped it without telling anyone, so the first feedback came as a bug report rather than a heads-up.

How do you deploy a service with long-lived connections without dropping everyone?

You cannot avoid disconnection entirely, so you make it cheap and staggered. Connection draining on the load balancer so a terminating pod stops receiving negotiates but keeps existing sockets for a grace window; a shutdown timeout longer than the default five seconds so in-flight work completes — HostOptions.ShutdownTimeout, and matched to the orchestrator's termination grace period, which is the mismatch that catches people. On the client, jittered exponential backoff on reconnect so 9,000 clients do not all retry on the same second, and automatic reconnect with state recovery so the user does not lose their place. We also rolled one pod at a time with a pause, which turned a cliff into a ramp.

Anything you would build differently on ASP.NET Core 10?

I would question whether we needed a hub for half of it. Server-Sent Events became a first-class primitive in ASP.NET Core 10 via TypedResults.ServerSentEvents, and for one-way server-to-client streams — presence, notifications, progress — SSE is dramatically simpler: plain HTTP, no negotiate, no backplane semantics, no reconnect protocol to get wrong, and the browser reconnects for you. I would keep SignalR for the bidirectional document editing where it earns its complexity, and move presence to SSE. Splitting a real-time feature by directionality rather than treating it as one lump is the design lesson I took away.

08The JWT that validated everything

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I found this in a code review I nearly rubber-stamped. A partner-facing API had been failing integration tests against a new identity provider, and the fix in the PR was three lines in Program.cs that turned off audience validation and issuer validation "temporarily, for the sandbox environment", guarded by an environment check that read a configuration value which — in the same PR — defaulted to true when unset. Our container images did not set it. The change would have shipped an API that accepted any structurally valid, correctly signed JWT, including tokens issued by that identity provider for entirely different applications.

// The PR. Every one of these three lines removes a distinct check.
options.TokenValidationParameters = new TokenValidationParameters
{
    ValidateIssuer   = false,   // any issuer we trust a key from
    ValidateAudience = false,   // a token minted for another app is accepted here
    ValidateLifetime = false,   // an expired or stolen token never stops working
};

Explaining why each line matters is the part that made the conversation productive rather than a policy fight. Signature validation proves a token was issued by someone holding the signing key — it says nothing about who it was issued for. In a multi-tenant identity provider, thousands of applications may sit behind the same issuer and, with federation, the same key set; the aud claim is the only thing that says "this token is for you". Turning off audience validation while keeping signature validation therefore converts "authenticated as our user" into "authenticated as anyone's user", which is the confused-deputy shape that shows up in real breaches. Lifetime validation is what makes token theft time-boxed; without it, a token leaked in a log line in 2024 still works.

What I did was deliberately not "reject the PR". I approved a version of it that fixed the actual problem, which took twenty minutes of pairing to find: the sandbox IdP was issuing an audience of api://partner-sandbox while our config expected api://partner, and the JWT library's error message had said IDX10214: Audience validation failed with both values in it — the author had not read past the first line. Configuring the correct audience per environment took three lines and no security trade-off at all. That is worth rehearsing as a pattern: most "we had to disable the check" situations are a misconfiguration wearing a costume.

builder.Services.AddAuthentication(JwtBearerDefaults.AuthenticationScheme)
    .AddJwtBearer(o =>
    {
        o.Authority = builder.Configuration["Auth:Authority"];   // drives OIDC discovery + key rotation
        o.TokenValidationParameters = new TokenValidationParameters
        {
            ValidateIssuer           = true,
            ValidIssuer              = builder.Configuration["Auth:Issuer"],
            ValidateAudience         = true,
            ValidAudiences           = builder.Configuration.GetSection("Auth:Audiences").Get<string[]>(),
            ValidateLifetime         = true,
            ClockSkew                = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30),  // default is 5 minutes
            ValidateIssuerSigningKey = true,
        };
    });

builder.Services.AddAuthorization(o => o.FallbackPolicy =
    new AuthorizationPolicyBuilder().RequireAuthenticatedUser().Build());   // secure by default

The durable outcome was two things beyond the fix. First, that FallbackPolicy line, which makes every endpoint require authentication unless it explicitly opts out with [AllowAnonymous] — inverting the default so that forgetting to protect an endpoint is impossible rather than merely unlikely. (d) Follow-up drill chain

The signature was still being validated. Why is disabling audience validation still serious?

Because a signature proves provenance, not intent. In a multi-tenant identity provider a single issuer signs tokens for thousands of applications, so a valid signature only tells you the token came from that IdP — not that it was minted for your API. The aud claim is the sole mechanism that scopes a token to one audience. Without it, anyone who can obtain a token for any application on that tenant can present it to yours and be authenticated; if you then map identity to permissions by subject or email, they arrive as a legitimate-looking user. This is the confused-deputy pattern, and it is more common than people expect precisely because "but the signature is valid" feels reassuring.

How would you have detected exploitation if it had shipped?

Honestly, poorly, which is part of why I pushed for the pipeline check rather than trusting detection. The signal you would look for is authenticated requests whose token aud or iss does not match what you expect, which means logging those claims — carefully, since a token is a credential and must never reach logs whole. ASP.NET Core 10 helps here: authentication and authorization now emit metrics as first-class meters, including authenticated request duration and challenge/forbid counts, so a spike in successful authentications from an unexpected client is visible without custom instrumentation. I would pair that with alerting on new subject identifiers appearing at a rate that does not match onboarding.

At what scale does the FallbackPolicy approach cause problems?

It caused one immediately, which is worth admitting: it broke the health endpoint and the OpenAPI document on the next deploy, because both had been implicitly anonymous. That is the point of the change — you find out — but on a large surface it means an audit of every genuinely public endpoint, and in a big organisation that is a week of work and a few arguments. The failure mode at scale is people adding [AllowAnonymous] to make an error go away without understanding why the endpoint was failing, which quietly restores the old world one attribute at a time. The counter is to make [AllowAnonymous] require a justification comment enforced by an analyzer, and to review its usages periodically rather than never.

What did you trade away?

Twenty minutes of my time and about a day of the author's schedule, on a PR that was already late. That is not nothing when someone is under pressure, and I made a point of pairing on the real fix rather than leaving a comment and walking away — a review comment that says "this is unsafe" and nothing else outsources the whole cost to the author and is how you teach a team to stop raising things. I also traded away some future flexibility with the CI assertion: it fails legitimately when someone adds a genuinely new audience, which happened twice and cost a few minutes each time. I would take that trade every time.

What else in an ASP.NET Core auth setup do you check by reflex?

The default ClockSkew of five minutes, which quietly extends every token's life and surprises people during token-revocation discussions. Whether the authority is used for OIDC discovery so key rotation is automatic rather than a pinned key someone has to remember to update. That authentication and authorization middleware sit in the right order relative to routing, since misordering fails open in specific configurations. CORS with AllowAnyOrigin alongside credentials. And whether refresh tokens are rotated and revocable, because access-token lifetime is only half the story.

Has anything in ASP.NET Core 10 changed how you approach this?

Two things. Cookie authentication no longer redirects unauthenticated API requests to a login page — endpoints identified as APIs now return 401 or 403 — which removes a whole class of confusing behaviour where a JavaScript client received a 200 with an HTML login form and treated it as success. That is a breaking change worth knowing about on any .NET 10 upgrade. And passkeys: ASP.NET Core 10 ships WebAuthn/FIDO2 support with Identity, including passkey management in the Blazor Web App template. That does not change token validation, but it changes the conversation about phishing-resistant credentials at the front door, and it is the kind of current detail that shows you have read the release notes rather than the blog summaries.

Nobody would touch it. The cost was not aesthetic — onboarding a new engineer to the request pipeline took a week, and we had twice shipped a middleware ordering bug that failed open.

The technical work had three parts. First, extract per-feature registration into IServiceCollection extension methods so the composition root became a readable table of contents — this is mechanical and safe, and it was 80% of the line count. Second, and much harder, understand the middleware order and write it down. The pipeline is a chain of delegates where order determines behaviour, and the failure mode of getting it wrong is silence: put UseAuthorization before UseAuthentication and HttpContext.User is unpopulated, so policies evaluate against an anonymous principal; put UseCors after routing without care and preflight breaks for some routes only; put exception handling too late and you leak stack traces on the paths it does not cover.     .AddPlatform(builder.Configuration)      // logging, telemetry, health, resilience defaults

    .AddIdentityAndAccess(builder.Configuration)

    .AddPricing()
    .AddNotifications();

var app = builder.Build();

app.UseHttpsRedirection();

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is middleware order load-bearing at all? Could the framework not sort it out?

Because the pipeline is deliberately a composition of nested delegates rather than a declarative set of concerns. Each component receives the next as a continuation, so it can run code before the rest of the pipeline, after it, or instead of it — that is what lets exception handling wrap everything below it and lets short-circuiting components like static files or a rate limiter skip the rest entirely. Any scheme that inferred order would have to understand those intents, and the cases where two orders are both legitimate are common enough that guessing would be worse than explicit. The design trades a footgun for expressiveness, which I think is right — but it means the order deserves tests, the way any load-bearing configuration does.

Why put the rate limiter after routing but before authentication?

After routing because per-endpoint rate limit policies are endpoint metadata — the limiter needs the selected endpoint to know which policy applies, and if it runs before UseRouting only a global limiter takes effect, silently. Before authentication because the point of shedding load is to shed it cheaply: token validation involves signature verification and possibly a network call for key discovery, and you do not want an attacker to make you do that work for every request in a flood. The trade-off to name is that limiting before authentication means your limits are keyed on something weaker — IP or an API key header — rather than on a verified identity, so a per-user limit still has to sit after auth. In practice you want both: a cheap outer limit and a precise inner one.

At what point does the composition-root refactor stop paying off?

When the extension methods start hiding rather than organising. If AddPlatform() registers forty things across six concerns, you have moved the 2,000 lines rather than structured them, and the next engineer has to open three files to answer what used to be a scroll. My rule is that an extension method should correspond to something a person would name in conversation — a module, a bounded concern — and if you cannot name it without "and", split it. The other stopping point is that the composition root is genuinely allowed to be a long, boring list; there is no prize for a ten-line Program.cs if the registrations are now scattered across the solution in ways that make startup order harder to reason about.

What did you trade away for three weeks of two engineers?

A quarter feature slipped by about two weeks, and I made that trade explicitly with the product manager rather than absorbing it quietly — partly because a refactor funded by stealth is one that gets abandoned the first time priorities move. What I could not buy back was the review load: 180 registrations moving is a lot of diff even when it is mechanical, and reviewer fatigue is real, which is exactly how the two mistakes got through. If I had a fourth week I would have spent it on smaller batches rather than on more scope.

10Levelling someone up on async

tier: staff archetype: mentoring / levelling up

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A mid-level engineer joining from a heavy Node background was producing correct code that made me uneasy, and I could not articulate why for two weeks. The tell, once I saw it, was that every method was async, nothing had a CancellationToken, and there was a Task.Run wrapping database calls "to make them non-blocking". Each piece was defensible in isolation. Together they said the mental model was "async means it runs in the background", which is the JavaScript intuition and is subtly wrong in .NET in a way that produces real production bugs.

What did not work: I explained the mechanism in a code review comment. Three paragraphs about state machines, the thread pool, and continuations. He read it, thanked me, and wrote the same code the next week — because a mechanism explanation without a felt consequence does not move a mental model. What worked was making him produce the bug. We spent forty minutes with a load test against a scratch endpoint containing his Task.Run(() => db.Query()).Result pattern, watched dotnet-counters, and he saw the queue length climb while CPU sat at 30%. He said "oh — the thread is just sitting there", and after that the concepts landed in ten minutes because they were explaining something he had watched happen.

The three corrections, in the order they mattered. Async is not parallelism. await on an I/O operation frees the thread until the operation completes; it does not start a second thread. Task.Run does hand work to a pool thread, which is right for CPU-bound work and actively harmful for I/O — you consume a thread to wait, which is the opposite of what async I/O buys you. Cancellation is cooperative and you have to pass it. ASP.NET Core gives you HttpContext.RequestAborted, and a token that is accepted and ignored is worse than no token, because callers believe cancellation works. The state machine is not free. Every async method allocates when it suspends, so a wrapper method that only awaits one call and returns is usually better written to return the inner task directly, or as ValueTask on a hot path that usually completes synchronously.

// What he was writing: a pool thread consumed to wait for I/O, and cancellation dropped.
public async Task<Order> GetAsync(int id) =>
    await Task.Run(() => _db.Orders.Single(o => o.Id == id));

// What we landed on: no extra thread, cancellation honoured, no redundant state machine.
public Task<Order> GetAsync(int id, CancellationToken ct) =>
    _db.Orders.SingleAsync(o => o.Id == id, ct);

The part I am actually proud of is what happened next. Rather than reviewing his PRs more closely, I asked him to write the team's short guide to async in ASP.NET Core, because teaching it is what fixes a model permanently. He wrote three pages, two other engineers said it answered questions they had been quietly carrying for a year, and it became the thing we hand new joiners. Six months later he caught a sync-over-async call in someone else's PR before I did.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is Task.Run around a database call actively harmful?

Because it converts a thread-free wait into a thread-consuming one. Async I/O in .NET does not need a thread while the operation is outstanding — the request is issued, the thread returns to the pool, and a completion is queued when the I/O finishes. Wrapping the call in Task.Run takes a pool thread and blocks it inside a synchronous database call, so you have paid for a thread and a queue hop to achieve exactly what the synchronous version did, plus you have hidden the blocking from anyone reading the call site. Under load it is a starvation source with camouflage. Task.Run is correct for genuinely CPU-bound work you want off the request thread — image resizing, a heavy parse — and almost nowhere else in a server application.

He accepted the mechanism explanation and kept writing the same code. Why?

Because a mental model is not replaced by information, it is replaced by a contradiction he cannot explain away. His model — "async runs it in the background" — predicted correct behaviour for everything he had personally observed, since none of it had been under load. My explanation added detail without producing a prediction failure, so the old model survived as the default under time pressure, which is where habits actually live. The load test worked because it produced a result his model could not account for. I now reach for "let's make it break" much earlier than "let me explain", especially with experienced engineers, whose models are strong precisely because they have worked for years.

When is a CancellationToken actually worth threading through?

Anywhere the work can outlive the caller's interest — which in a web application is essentially every request-scoped operation, because clients disconnect and browsers cancel. The concrete win is that a cancelled request stops holding a database connection, and under a load spike where clients are timing out at three seconds, honouring cancellation is the difference between shedding work and doing work nobody will read. The nuances worth knowing: never pass a request token to a write you must complete — a cancelled HTTP request should not abandon a half-finished transaction — and be careful in background services, where the correct token is the stopping token, not a request's. Accepting a token and ignoring it is the worst option, because it is a lie in the signature.

At what point does ValueTask stop being worth the complexity?

Almost immediately, for most code. ValueTask avoids an allocation when a method usually completes synchronously — a cache hit, a buffered read — so it pays off on genuinely hot paths measured in millions of calls. In exchange you accept real constraints: you must not await it twice, must not use .Result on an incomplete one, and cannot casually pass it around, and violating those produces bugs that are hard to see in review. My guidance to the team was to use Task by default, reach for ValueTask only with a benchmark in the PR, and know that the runtime has been closing the gap anyway — async method allocation has improved substantially, and .NET 11's runtime-async work targets suspension and resumption cost directly.

How would you have handled it if he had pushed back and not changed?

I would separate the disagreement from the standard. On the disagreement, I would want to understand what he was optimising for — sometimes "every method is async" is a deliberate consistency choice and the conversation is about a style rule, not correctness. On the standard, sync-over-async and Task.Run around I/O are not stylistic, and I would move it out of the interpersonal register into a mechanical one: the threading analyzers as build errors, so it is the build that says no rather than me. That is generally my escalation path for anything I have explained twice — make it a rule the tooling enforces, so disagreement becomes a conversation about the rule rather than about a person's PR.

What did mentoring him cost you, and how did you decide it was worth it?

Perhaps fifteen hours over two months, including the forty-minute load test session and reviewing his guide twice. The honest accounting is that fixing his PRs myself would have cost less in the short term and been strictly worse: I would have had a person who wrote code I quietly rewrote, which is how good engineers get stuck at a level. I judged it worth it because the gap was a model rather than an attitude, and models are cheap to fix once. If someone is not curious about why they were wrong, the same fifteen hours buys much less, and I would spend it on the tooling instead.

11Cutting scope on an API rewrite

tier: staff archetype: scope / deadline negotiation

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A partner integration deadline was fixed by a contract: a new public API had to be live in eleven weeks, and the estimate for what had been scoped was around eighteen. The scoped version included a full GraphQL layer, a new versioning scheme, an SDK in three languages, and a developer portal. I had six weeks of that team's history to go on and I believed the eighteen. The instinct in the room was to add people, which on an eleven-week deadline adds coordination cost and subtracts nothing.

What I did instead was ask what the partner actually needed on day one, and the answer — obtained by getting their integration engineer on a call, which took two days and should have taken two hours — was seven endpoints, OAuth client credentials, a sandbox, and documentation good enough to build against. Not GraphQL. Not three SDKs. They had a code generator and would have preferred an OpenAPI document to any SDK we wrote. That call cut roughly nine weeks of scope and improved the deliverable, which is the uncomfortable lesson: the scope was not too big because someone was gold-plating, it was too big because nobody had asked.

The technical choices followed from the constraint. Minimal APIs rather than controllers, because seven endpoints do not need the MVC pipeline and the reduced ceremony was measurable in review time. OpenAPI generated from the code rather than hand-written, so the document could not drift from the implementation — in ASP.NET Core 10 that story is materially better than it was: the built-in Microsoft.AspNetCore.OpenApi package defaults to OpenAPI 3.1, can serve YAML, and picks up XML documentation comments automatically, so our endpoint summaries became partner-facing documentation with no second source of truth. Validation via the source-generated AddValidation() introduced in ASP.NET Core 10, which meant validation attributes on minimal API parameters worked without a reflection-based layer and without pulling in a third-party validator.

builder.Services.AddValidation();      // ASP.NET Core 10: source-generated, AOT-friendly
builder.Services.AddOpenApi();         // 3.1 by default

var api = app.MapGroup("/v1").RequireAuthorization("partner");

/// <summary>Creates a shipment booking.</summary>   // flows into the OpenAPI document
api.MapPost("/bookings", async (CreateBooking cmd, IBookingService svc, CancellationToken ct)
        => TypedResults.Created($"/v1/bookings/{await svc.CreateAsync(cmd, ct)}"))
   .WithName("CreateBooking")
   .ProducesValidationProblem();

app.MapOpenApi("/openapi/{documentName}.yaml");   // the partner asked for YAML

We shipped in nine weeks with two weeks of buffer that we mostly spent on the sandbox, because that was what the partner's engineers used every day. GraphQL was never built and nobody has asked for it in the two years since. The versioning scheme became "we will add /v2 when we need it", written down, which took an hour instead of three weeks.

What I would do differently: I negotiated scope with our product manager for four days before I talked to the partner. Every one of those days was spent guessing at requirements that a thirty-minute call answered definitively. Now, when scope and deadline collide, my first move is to find the person who will actually use the thing and ask them what breaks if we ship less — before I do any internal negotiation at all.

12Data-loss scare: work lost at shutdown

tier: senior → staff archetype: data-loss / corruption scare

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A support ticket said an invoice had been marked as sent but never emailed. One ticket. Investigating it took an afternoon and turned up 1,340 more over four months — roughly 0.4% of invoices, invisible because nothing had errored. The cause was a pattern that looks harmless and appears in a great many ASP.NET Core codebases: fire-and-forget work started from a request.

// The pattern. Nothing awaits it, nothing owns it, nothing survives a shutdown.
_ = Task.Run(async () => await _emailSender.SendAsync(invoice));
return TypedResults.Accepted($"/invoices/{invoice.Id}");

Three separate ways that loses work, and being able to enumerate them is what turns this from an anecdote into a demonstration of understanding. First, shutdown: when the host stops — a deploy, a scale-in, a node drain — it signals the application stopping token, waits for the shutdown timeout (five seconds by default), and exits. Detached tasks are not tracked by anything, so they are not waited for; the process ends mid-send. We deployed several times a day, and each deploy silently dropped whatever was in flight. Second, unobserved exceptions: an exception inside a detached task is captured in the returned Task, and if nobody awaits it, nobody ever sees it — since .NET 4.5 an unobserved task exception does not even crash the process, it is simply swallowed at finalization. Third, scope: the lambda captured a scoped DbContext from the request, which is disposed when the request completes, so any that ran slowly enough threw ObjectDisposedException into the void.

The fix was to stop treating "later" as "in the background of this process". We wrote the intent to the database in the same transaction as the invoice — the transactional outbox pattern — and a hosted service polled and dispatched it. That changes the failure mode from "lost silently" to "retried until delivered", which is the only durable answer: any in-process background work is lost if the process dies, and processes die routinely and by design.

// Same transaction as the business change: either both happen or neither does.
db.Invoices.Add(invoice);
db.Outbox.Add(new OutboxMessage(nameof(InvoiceIssued), JsonSerializer.Serialize(invoice.Id)));
await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);

// A hosted service drains it. Note the token: this is the stopping token, not a request's.
public sealed class OutboxDispatcher(IServiceScopeFactory scopes) : BackgroundService
{
    protected override async Task ExecuteAsync(CancellationToken stopping)
    {
        while (!stopping.IsCancellationRequested)
        {
            await using var scope = scopes.CreateAsyncScope();
            var pump = scope.ServiceProvider.GetRequiredService<IOutboxPump>();
            try { await pump.DrainAsync(stopping); }
            catch (OperationCanceledException) when (stopping.IsCancellationRequested) { break; }
            catch (Exception ex) { /* log and continue: an unhandled throw here stops the host */ }
            await Task.Delay(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2), stopping);
        }
    }
}

We also fixed the shutdown window, which was a contributing factor for the in-flight work we could not make transactional. The default ShutdownTimeout is five seconds; our orchestrator's termination grace period was thirty. Aligning them — host timeout comfortably under the orchestrator's grace period — is the correct relationship, and getting it backwards means the platform kills you while you are still draining, which produces exactly the "it works except during deploys" bug class.

Recovery: because the invoices were in the database and only the emails were missing, we reconstructed the 1,340 from the invoice records and sent them with a note. No permanent loss, but four months of a customer-visible defect nobody had detected, and that detection gap was the real finding. What I would do differently is the framing I now bring to every design review: for any asynchronous work, ask "if the process dies right now, who retries this?" If the answer is nobody, it is not background work, it is a silent data-loss bug with a delay.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you distinguish "no errors" from "working", and whether your instinct after a scare is detection rather than only prevention. Senior candidates fix the bug; staff-plus candidates ask why four months passed without anyone noticing and fix that.

Mistakes candidates make: focusing on the heroic recovery and skipping the detection gap; not quantifying the blast radius; not mentioning customer communication; and presenting it as a one-off rather than a pattern that certainly exists elsewhere in the codebase.

(b) STAR/CARL scaffold

  • SituationWhat was lost or at risk, how much, for how long, and how you found out. "One ticket" is a powerful opening.
  • TaskContainment, recovery, root cause, customer communication — say which of those you personally owned.
  • ActionHow you bounded the blast radius before fixing anything; the recovery; the structural fix; the detection you added.
  • ResultRecovered vs. permanently lost, honestly. Plus the detection that would now catch it in hours.
  • DifferentlyThe monitoring gap. Four months of silence is the story, not the bug.

(c) Recall prompts

What is the longest a bug has been live before you found it, and how did you find it?

The discovery path matters: a customer, a reconciliation, a stray metric, a migration that surfaced it. A customer finding it first is the most common honest answer and it is fine — what you did about detection is the point.

Where does fire-and-forget exist in a codebase you know?

_ = Task.Run(...), async void event handlers, un-awaited calls the compiler warned about, ContinueWith without a fault path, an IHostedService with no failure handling.

From logs, an audit table, an event stream, an upstream system, a backup. What made it possible — and what would have made it impossible?

Queue depth, a staleness check ("oldest unprocessed row"), reconciliation counts between two systems, a synthetic transaction. Staleness alerts catch a whole class of "nothing errored, nothing happened" bugs.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did nothing error? Surely something threw.

Some did throw, and that is precisely the mechanism. An exception inside a detached Task is stored on the task object rather than propagating; if nobody awaits or inspects it, it is never observed, and since .NET 4.5 an unobserved task exception no longer crashes the process at finalization — it is raised on TaskScheduler.UnobservedTaskException, which by default nobody subscribes to, and then dropped. Combine that with the shutdown case, where the process simply exits with the task mid-flight so there is no exception at all, and you get a failure that produces neither a log line nor a metric. That is why my first structural change was subscribing to UnobservedTaskException and logging it: it turns an invisible class of bug into a noisy one.

Why an outbox rather than just publishing to a queue directly?

Because a direct publish reintroduces the same problem in a more respectable outfit — you now have two systems to update, the database and the broker, with no shared transaction. Publish first and the database write can fail, so you have announced something that did not happen; write first and the publish can fail, so you have the invoice and no email. The outbox collapses it into one atomic write: the intent lands in the same transaction as the business change, and a separate process moves it to the broker with at-least-once delivery. The cost you take on is that consumers must be idempotent, plus polling latency and one more moving part — which is a real cost, and for genuinely best-effort work like a cache warm, I would not pay it.

At what scale does the polling outbox break?

Two ways. Throughput: a single dispatcher polling every couple of seconds is fine into the low thousands per minute, but past that the poll becomes a hot query and you need batching, a covering index on the unprocessed rows, and eventually partitioning or change-data-capture instead of polling. Concurrency: multiple dispatchers competing for the same rows need FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED semantics or a lease column, otherwise you either double-send or serialise on a lock. The one that bit us mildly was table growth — we did not prune dispatched rows, so the query slowed over a quarter until we added a retention job. None of these are hard, but all of them are invisible until the volume arrives.

What did you trade away?

Latency and simplicity. Emails that used to go out within milliseconds of the request now went out within a couple of seconds, which nobody noticed but which I had to confirm was acceptable rather than assume. We also added a table, a background service and an idempotency requirement on the consumer — genuinely more to operate and more to understand for a new joiner. I would defend it because the alternative was a system that silently loses 0.4% of a customer-visible action, and there is no amount of simplicity that buys that back. But I would not apply the pattern uniformly: for work that is genuinely disposable, in-process is fine, and saying so explicitly stops the pattern from becoming cargo cult.

How do you now decide whether background work needs durability?

One question: if this process dies mid-operation, does a person or another system end up with a wrong belief? Sending an email, charging a card, notifying a partner, updating a downstream index — all yes, all need durable intent. Warming a cache, emitting a non-billing metric, pre-computing something that will be recomputed — all no. The trap is the middle ground where the work is "just" a notification, because notifications are exactly where silent loss is invisible to your systems and obvious to your customers. I also ask a second question now: how would we know? If there is no answer, durability is not enough — you need a staleness alert too.

Does anything in recent .NET make this pattern safer?

The primitives have gotten better, though none of them make fire-and-forget durable — nothing can. BackgroundService gained a StopAsync that respects the stopping token properly, and since .NET 8 an unhandled exception in a BackgroundService stops the host by default rather than being swallowed, which is a much better default and is worth knowing about when upgrading, since a previously-invisible crash becomes a loud one. HostOptions.ServicesStartConcurrently and ShutdownTimeout give you explicit control over the drain window. And .NET Aspire makes the outbox-plus-worker shape genuinely easy to run locally, which historically was the main reason teams avoided the pattern.

13Requirements that moved three times

tier: senior → staff archetype: ambiguous / shifting requirements

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A compliance feature landed on us with a regulatory date nine weeks out and a specification that was, honestly, three paragraphs and a legal citation. Which transactions had to be reported, in what format, and to whom, changed three times: once when legal read the guidance more carefully, once when the regulator published a clarification, and once when we discovered our own data did not contain a field the format required. Two of those three changes arrived after we had built to the previous version.

My first instinct was to demand a frozen spec, which is a reasonable-sounding request that would have been refused and would have cost me two weeks of credibility. The requirements were genuinely uncertain — legal did not know, because the regulator had not said. So the design question became: how do we build something whose shape is stable while its details are still moving? That reframing is the whole story.

Concretely, we separated three things that had been one. The selection of which transactions to report became configuration — a set of predicates driven by IOptionsMonitor<T> bound to a configuration section, so a change in scope was a config change with an audit trail rather than a deploy. The format became a versioned mapper with the version in the file name, so we could support two formats simultaneously during the transition the regulator eventually required. The transport — the part nobody would change — was built solidly and once. Then everything went behind a feature flag with a per-environment kill switch, so a wrong interpretation was a five-minute rollback rather than a release.

// IOptionsMonitor, not IOptionsSnapshot: this is consumed by a singleton hosted service,
// and it re-reads on change so a config update takes effect without a restart.
public sealed class ReportSelector(IOptionsMonitor<ReportingOptions> options)
{
    public bool ShouldReport(Transaction t)
    {
        var o = options.CurrentValue;                      // snapshot per call, not per instance
        return t.Amount >= o.MinimumAmount
            && o.IncludedJurisdictions.Contains(t.Jurisdiction)
            && !o.ExcludedProductCodes.Contains(t.ProductCode);
    }
}

The IOptionsMonitor versus IOptionsSnapshot distinction is worth being able to state cleanly, because it is a common interview probe and it mattered here: IOptionsSnapshot<T> is registered as scoped and gives you a value consistent for the lifetime of a request, which is right for request-scoped consumers; IOptionsMonitor<T> is a singleton that exposes CurrentValue and change notifications, which is what a singleton or hosted service needs. Injecting IOptionsSnapshot into a singleton is a captive dependency and will hand you a value frozen at startup — the same class of bug as story 06, arriving through a different door.

The other decision I would repeat was writing down our interpretation. Every time the requirement moved, I updated a one-page document with the current understanding, the source (a paragraph of guidance, a call with legal, an email from the regulator's helpdesk), and what we had built to it. When the third change came, that page let us answer "what did we assume and why" in ten minutes instead of a day of archaeology, and it meant legal could review our interpretation rather than our code.

We shipped four days before the date. Two of the three changes cost us under a day each because they were configuration. The third — the missing data field — cost eleven days, because no amount of architecture saves you from data you never collected; we had to backfill from an upstream system.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can act under uncertainty without either freezing or thrashing. The signal is that you identified which parts were stable and built those firmly, and that you made the uncertain parts cheap to change. They also listen for whether you managed the stakeholder relationship — an engineer who treats moving requirements as bad faith is expensive to work with.

Mistakes candidates make: blaming the business; describing over-engineered generality as if flexibility were free; no example of a change that was actually cheap because of a decision they made; and no acknowledgement of the thing that stayed expensive regardless.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

How did you decide what to make configurable and what to hard-code?

I asked, for each element, "who would change this and how likely are they to change it in the next quarter?" The transport — endpoint, authentication, retry — had no plausible source of change and got built directly. Format was in between, so I versioned rather than parameterised it, because a mapper you can add a version of is easier to reason about than one with fifteen switches. The failure mode I was steering away from is the generalised system with no users: every configuration point is a thing to document, validate, test and get wrong at 2am, so flexibility earns its place per element rather than as a policy.

Why IOptionsMonitor here specifically?

Because the consumer was a singleton hosted service that runs continuously, and the whole point was that a scope change takes effect without a restart. IOptionsSnapshot is registered scoped — it gives a value consistent within a request, which is exactly right for a controller and exactly wrong for a long-lived service, where injecting it is a captive dependency that freezes the value at startup. IOptionsMonitor is a singleton exposing CurrentValue plus OnChange notifications. Two subtleties I would name: you must read CurrentValue at the point of use rather than caching it in a field, or you have reintroduced the freeze; and OnChange can fire more than once for a single file write because of how file watchers coalesce, so any side effect in the handler must be idempotent.

At what point does the "make it configuration" approach become a liability?

When the configuration becomes a programming language. Ours was three lists and a threshold, which a compliance analyst could read; if it had grown to nested boolean expressions, we would have built a rules engine with no type checking, no tests and no review process, deployed by whoever had access to the config store. The specific dangers are that config changes usually bypass code review, that a typo in a jurisdiction code fails silently by matching nothing, and that nobody can answer "what was the configuration on the 14th" unless you version it. We mitigated with schema validation on startup via ValidateDataAnnotations() and ValidateOnStart(), and by keeping the config in the same repository so changes had a pull request.

What did you trade away?

Some directness. A reader of that code cannot tell what gets reported without also reading the configuration for that environment, which is a genuine loss — the code no longer tells the whole story, and a new engineer needs to know that. I offset it with a startup log line that prints the effective selection criteria, and a test that asserts a known set of transactions against a fixed configuration file, so the behaviour is at least pinned somewhere readable. I would make the same trade, but I have stopped pretending configurability is free; it moves complexity from the code to the operational surface, where fewer people are looking.

The data-field problem cost eleven days. Could anything have prevented that?

Yes, and it is the part I actually regret. We spent week one on architecture and week three discovering that a required field did not exist in our data. If I had spent one day in week one producing a single end-to-end report from real production data — even a wrong one, even by hand — the gap would have surfaced immediately. That is now a rule I apply to every integration: build the thinnest possible end-to-end slice against real data before designing anything, because the expensive surprises live in the data, not in the design. Architecture protects you from changes of intent; nothing protects you from information you never collected.

How did you keep legal's trust while telling them their spec kept moving?

By never framing it that way. The one-page interpretation document did the work: each version recorded what we now believed and why, with the source, and I sent it after every change rather than waiting to be asked. That reframed the conversation from "you changed your mind again" to "here is what we currently think, please confirm" — which put the expensive question in front of them early, when they could still answer it cheaply. The unexpected benefit was that legal started sending us the regulator's clarifications directly instead of via product, which cut about a week of latency out of the last two changes.

14The upstream gRPC contract that broke us

tier: staff archetype: cross-team / integration dependency

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

An internal inventory team changed a gRPC field from optional int32 to a required nested message in a minor release. Protobuf is famously tolerant of additive change; this was not additive. Our service started receiving zero-valued quantities where it expected real numbers, and because zero is a perfectly valid quantity, nothing errored — we cheerfully told 4,000 customers a product was out of stock for about two hours before someone in support noticed a pattern.

The immediate response was straightforward: pin the client to the previous proto version and redeploy, which took eighteen minutes. The interesting work was afterwards, and it was mostly organisational. Two teams, no contract test between them, a shared proto file living in the producing team's repository, and a deployment pipeline where their release automatically became our runtime dependency with no gate. Every one of those is a normal arrangement and together they guarantee this outcome eventually.

What I got agreement on, in a meeting I deliberately did not start by describing our outage: consumer-driven contract tests. Our expectations of their service became a test that ran in their pipeline. That is the shift that matters — it moves the feedback from "the consumer breaks in production" to "the producer's build fails", which is where the change is cheapest to fix and where the person who can fix it is already looking. Persuading another team to accept a gate on their pipeline is a real negotiation, and the argument that worked was not about our outage; it was that they had no way of knowing who depended on which fields, which made every change frightening for them too.

// The defensive half, on our side: never let an upstream shape change read as valid data.
public sealed class InventoryClient(Inventory.InventoryClient grpc, ILogger<InventoryClient> log)
{
    public async Task<int> GetAvailableAsync(string sku, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var reply = await grpc.GetStockAsync(new StockRequest { Sku = sku }, cancellationToken: ct);

        // Protobuf gives absent fields their default value. For a quantity, "0" and "absent"
        // mean very different things, so we must ask explicitly rather than trust the value.
        if (!reply.HasQuantity)
            throw new UpstreamContractException($"inventory: quantity absent for {sku}");

        return reply.Quantity;
    }
}

That HasQuantity check is the technical lesson and it generalises well beyond gRPC. Protobuf's wire format does not distinguish "field set to the default" from "field absent" unless the field is declared optional in proto3, which generates the Has property; without it, a missing int is indistinguishable from zero and a missing string from empty. Any place where the zero value is semantically meaningful — a quantity, a price, a count, a boolean flag — needs explicit presence checking, or an upstream mistake becomes silent bad data downstream. The same trap exists in JSON with non-nullable value types, and it is the reason System.Text.Json in .NET 9 added a strict mode option that rejects unmapped members and requires non-optional ones.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did none of your resilience patterns help?

Because they address the wrong failure axis. Retry, timeout and circuit breaking all assume the upstream is unavailable or slow, and they detect that through errors and latency. Ours was fast and returned a 200 with a well-formed response — it was simply wrong. No amount of retrying a correct-looking answer produces a different one. This is a distinction I now make explicitly in design reviews: availability failures are detected by the transport, correctness failures can only be detected by something that knows what the data should look like. That means either a contract test upstream, a validation at the boundary, or a semantic alert on the business outcome — and honestly you want all three, because they fail in different ways.

Why does protobuf let a field silently become zero?

Because proto3 removed required fields deliberately, to make schema evolution safe: absent fields deserialize to their type's default, so adding or removing a field never breaks a parser. That is a good design for compatibility and a trap for semantics, because for an integer quantity, zero is a meaningful value that happens to coincide with "absent". The escape hatch is declaring the field optional in proto3, which restores explicit presence tracking and generates a HasX property in the C# bindings. My rule now is that any field where the default value is semantically meaningful must be optional, and the consumer must check presence — the wire format is not going to protect you and it was never trying to.

At what scale do consumer-driven contract tests stop scaling?

They scale with the number of consumer-producer pairs, not services, so they get uncomfortable somewhere past a few dozen pairs — the producer's build starts running a lot of other teams' expectations and gets slow and flaky, and nobody wants to debug a failure from a team they have never met. Beyond that point the answer is usually a schema registry with automated compatibility checking, where the producer's build validates the new schema against the registered version rather than against every consumer's test. That is less precise — it enforces structural compatibility rather than semantic expectation — but it is O(1) per producer, and precision at that scale is better bought with strong versioning conventions than with more tests.

How did you actually persuade another team to add a gate to their pipeline?

By finding what it did for them. Their real problem was that they had no idea who consumed which fields, so every schema change was a coin flip and they had been sitting on a cleanup for two quarters out of fear. The contract tests gave them a list of what was actually depended on and permission to change everything else — I framed it as a deprecation capability, not a safety net for us. That is the general shape I look for now: influence without authority mostly means finding the version of your proposal that solves the other person's problem. I also made it cheap to say yes by writing the first three tests and the pipeline step myself.

What did you trade away by pinning the client version?

Automatic access to their improvements, and we took on an upgrade obligation that someone has to remember. Within a quarter we were two versions behind and had to do a deliberate catch-up, which cost a day and would have cost nothing if we had stayed current. That is the standard pinning trade: you convert a small continuous risk into a large occasional one. What made it acceptable was pairing the pin with a scheduled upgrade — a monthly dependency-update pull request that runs the contract tests — so the pin is a gate rather than a freeze. A pin without a cadence is just deferred breakage with interest.

Would the same failure be possible with a REST/JSON contract?

Absolutely, and arguably more easily, because JSON deserialization into non-nullable value types has exactly the same "absent becomes default" behaviour with none of protobuf's schema discipline. A missing quantity property deserializes to zero in a non-nullable int and looks identical to a real zero. The mitigations are the same shape: use nullable types at the boundary so absence is representable, validate the deserialized model rather than trusting it, and — newer and genuinely helpful — the strict deserialization option added to System.Text.Json, which rejects unmapped members and can require properties to be present, turning a silent default into a loud failure at the edge.

15The platform migration I cancelled

tier: principal archetype: project that failed or was killed

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I sponsored a service-mesh rollout across fourteen ASP.NET Core services, sold on three promises: mTLS everywhere, uniform retries and circuit breaking, and distributed tracing without touching application code. Five months and roughly two engineer-quarters in, I cancelled it. Two services were migrated, we had spent a lot of goodwill, and calling it was the right decision made about six weeks too late.

What went wrong was not the technology. It was that I had bought a solution for three problems that turned out to have different shapes. Traffic policy and mTLS were genuinely well served by the mesh — that part worked. Resilience was not: the mesh's retries operated at the transport layer with no knowledge of idempotency, so a retried POST could double-charge, and we ended up keeping application-level resilience anyway, which meant two layers of retry multiplying each other during a partial outage. And tracing was the promise that broke the business case, because a mesh gives you span propagation between proxies but cannot see inside a process — you still get one opaque span per service and no idea which database call was slow. To get useful traces we needed in-process instrumentation regardless, which .NET already had.

That last realisation is the technically interesting part and the reason I can tell this story usefully. .NET's System.Diagnostics.Activity and ActivitySource are the native OpenTelemetry tracing primitives, and ASP.NET Core, HttpClient, Entity Framework Core and the SQL client all emit spans natively. Adding AddOpenTelemetry().WithTracing(...) gave us richer traces than the mesh could produce, in a day, because the instrumentation lives where the semantics are. A sidecar can tell you a request took 400 ms; only the process can tell you 380 of those were one N+1 query.

// What we kept. A day's work, and strictly more informative than the mesh's transport spans.
builder.Services.AddOpenTelemetry()
    .ConfigureResource(r => r.AddService(builder.Environment.ApplicationName))
    .WithTracing(t => t
        .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation(o => o.RecordException = true)
        .AddHttpClientInstrumentation()
        .AddEntityFrameworkCoreInstrumentation()
        .AddOtlpExporter())
    .WithMetrics(m => m
        .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation()
        .AddHttpClientInstrumentation()
        .AddRuntimeInstrumentation()        // GC, thread pool, exceptions, JIT
        .AddOtlpExporter());

How I cancelled it mattered more than that I did. I wrote a two-page note: what we believed at the start, what we learned, what we kept (mTLS on the two migrated services, which stayed), what we abandoned, and what I would look for before proposing something like it again. I presented it myself rather than delegating it, and I was explicit that the decision to start had been mine. Then I made sure the two engineers who had done the work were credited for what we learned in their performance reviews — because the fastest way to guarantee nobody ever takes an ambitious swing again is to let a cancelled project quietly damage the people who worked on it.

What I would do differently, and it is the real lesson: I did not define a kill criterion at the start. Every large initiative I sponsor now ships with a written "we will stop if" — a date, a metric, a condition — agreed before anyone is emotionally invested. The six weeks of delay were not analysis, they were sunk-cost reasoning wearing the costume of due diligence, and a pre-agreed criterion is the only defence against that I have found that actually works.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the tracing promise fail? Meshes do propagate traces.

They propagate context between proxies, which gives you a span per hop and a correct parent-child relationship across services — genuinely useful, and it is what people demonstrate. What it cannot give you is anything inside the process, because the sidecar sees bytes on a socket, not your call stack. So a slow request shows as "service B took 400 ms" with no visibility into whether that was one query, forty queries, a lock, or JSON serialisation. Every real debugging session needs the inside view, which means in-process instrumentation, which .NET provides natively through ActivitySource with the framework libraries already emitting spans. Once we accepted we needed that anyway, the mesh's tracing contribution went from "the main benefit" to "a redundant outer span".

Why were the mesh's retries actively dangerous rather than merely redundant?

Because they had no idea what they were retrying. A transport-layer retry sees a failed HTTP request; it does not know whether the operation was idempotent, whether the request actually reached the server and only the response was lost, or whether an idempotency key was present. Retrying a POST that created an order is how you create two orders. Worse, we kept our application-level resilience, so the two layers composed multiplicatively — three application retries times three mesh retries is nine attempts against a service that is already struggling, which turns a brownout into an outage. Layered retry is one of the classic distributed-systems footguns, and the correct discipline is that exactly one layer owns retry policy and everyone else fails fast.

At what scale would the mesh have been the right call?

Fourteen services was too few for the operational overhead, and I would put the threshold somewhere north of fifty, with two additional conditions. First, polyglot: a mesh's biggest genuine win is uniform policy across services in languages that do not share a library, and all fourteen of ours were .NET, so a shared NuGet package achieved most of it for a fraction of the cost. Second, a platform team that owns the mesh as a product — mesh operations are a real specialism, and a mesh maintained part-time by application teams is a liability during exactly the incidents it was meant to help with. If I had asked "would we staff this permanently?" at the start, I would have had my answer in week one.

What did the cancellation cost you personally?

Credibility, measurably. The next platform proposal I made was scrutinised much harder, and that was appropriate — I had spent a chunk of trust and had to earn it back with a smaller, well-evidenced win before anyone would fund something ambitious again. It also cost me some confidence in my own judgment for a couple of months, which I think is healthy in moderation and corrosive if it lasts. What I would not accept is the framing that it cost the two engineers anything; I put real effort into ensuring it did not, and one of them led the OpenTelemetry rollout that replaced it, which was a better project and visibly successful.

What is your kill criterion practice now, concretely?

Before any initiative larger than a quarter, I write down three things and get them agreed: the specific belief that justifies the work, the observation that would falsify it, and a date by which we will have made that observation. For the mesh it would have been "we believe the mesh removes the need for in-process instrumentation; we will migrate one service and compare its traces against an OpenTelemetry-instrumented one by week four." That experiment costs two weeks and would have ended the project in month one. The discipline is not the writing, it is the agreeing — a criterion I hold privately is one I will rationalise around, and one the group holds is one that fires.

How do you decide what to keep from a cancelled project?

By asking what stands alone without the rest of it. mTLS on the two migrated services was self-contained and valuable, so it stayed; the deployment tooling we had written was mesh-specific and got deleted the same week, because half-abandoned infrastructure is worse than none — it looks supported and is not. The rule I apply is that anything kept must have a named owner and a reason that does not reference the cancelled project. I also insist on deleting promptly rather than leaving things "in case we revisit", because the option value is almost always lower than the confusion cost, and the two engineers deserved a clean end rather than a lingering one.

16Cutting the compute bill with the GC

tier: staff archetype: cost optimisation

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Finance flagged that our Kubernetes compute line had grown 60% year on year against 20% traffic growth. I took two weeks to look, expecting to find inefficient code. What I found was that we were paying for memory we had provisioned defensively and never used: 46 services, almost all requesting 2 GB, with actual working sets between 180 MB and 400 MB. The requests had been copied from a template, and the template had been sized in 2021 by someone who had seen an OOM once.

The technically interesting part is why the memory looked justified. On older Server GC, the collector sizes its budgets based on throughput and available memory: it will happily let the heap grow when there is memory to spare, because collecting less often is faster. On a node with lots of free memory, a service that genuinely needs 200 MB will show a working set of 1.5 GB and nobody can tell the difference between "needs it" and "was given it". That is exactly the confusion DATAS was built to remove — dynamic adaptation to application sizes keeps the heap roughly proportional to live data and shrinks it when the workload lightens, at a target throughput cost of around 2%. It shipped opt-in in .NET 8, became the default for Server GC in .NET 9, and got further tuning in .NET 10.

So the work had three parts. First, get everything onto a runtime where DATAS was the default and measure honestly — which meant finishing the .NET 8 to .NET 10 upgrades that had been sitting in the backlog, and that turned out to be the largest chunk of the effort. Second, right-size requests from observed p99 working set plus 40% headroom, rather than from a template. Third, and this is where I made my mistake, set container memory limits properly.

// The knobs that actually mattered. Defaults are usually right; measure before touching these.
{
  "configProperties": {
    "System.GC.Server": true,
    "System.GC.DynamicAdaptationMode": 1,   // DATAS: 1 = on (default with Server GC since .NET 9)
    "System.GC.HeapHardLimitPercent": 75    // leave headroom for stacks, native, and the runtime itself
  }
}

My mistake was setting the GC heap hard limit to 100% of the container limit on the first three services, reasoning that the GC should be allowed to use everything we paid for. Two of them got OOM-killed within a day. The GC heap is not the process: thread stacks (1 MB each by default), native allocations from libraries, the loader heap, and pooled buffers all live outside it, and the container's limit counts all of that. .NET's default when running under a container limit is 75% precisely for this reason, and my override removed the safety margin. Fixing it was a one-line revert; the lesson — that the default encoded knowledge I did not have — is one I now apply generally to runtime tuning.

The result was a 41% reduction in that cost line, and a second-order win nobody had predicted: with smaller requests, the scheduler could pack more pods per node, so we ran a third fewer nodes. p99 latency moved by about 3 ms, which is the throughput cost of DATAS showing up honestly and which nobody could perceive.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the heap look so large when the application did not need it?

Because a garbage collector's job is to trade memory for time, and classic Server GC takes that trade aggressively when memory is available. Each heap gets an allocation budget, and collecting less frequently means less CPU spent on collection — so on a machine with free memory, letting the heap grow is the throughput-optimal choice. The consequence is that working set tells you what the GC was allowed, not what the application needs, and those can differ by an order of magnitude. DATAS changes the objective: it sizes the heap proportional to live data and gives memory back when the workload lightens, accepting roughly a 2% throughput cost. That is why the same service on a DATAS-default runtime finally showed a number I could provision against.

Why did setting the heap limit to 100% cause OOM kills?

Because the GC heap is a subset of the process's memory and the container limit counts the whole process. Outside the heap you have thread stacks — 1 MB reserved each by default, and a service with a hundred pool threads is not trivial — plus native allocations from crypto, compression and interop libraries, the loader heap and JIT-generated code, the pooled buffers behind ArrayPool and Kestrel's memory pool, and the runtime's own bookkeeping. If the GC believes it may fill the container limit, it will let the heap grow until the cgroup killer intervenes, and the kill is abrupt and unlogged. .NET's default heap hard limit under a container is 75% of the limit for exactly this reason. My override deleted a margin that existed because someone had already made my mistake.

At what scale would DATAS be the wrong choice?

When throughput is the product and you own the machine. A latency-sensitive service with a fixed, dedicated allocation — a matching engine, a high-rate ingest tier — does not benefit from giving memory back, and the roughly 2% throughput cost plus the extra collections during heap adjustment are a real loss with nothing bought. The same applies where startup performance is critical, since DATAS starts conservatively and grows into the workload. The knob is System.GC.DynamicAdaptationMode, set to 0 to opt out, and I would only do it with a benchmark on the actual workload — my experience is that intuitions about GC behaviour are wrong roughly half the time, mine included.

What did you trade away for 41%?

Headroom, deliberately. Before, a service with a memory spike had 1.5 GB of accidental slack; now it has 40%, and that means a genuinely unusual workload — a customer uploading a file ten times larger than any before — can OOM where previously it would have absorbed. I accepted that with two mitigations: alerting on working set crossing 70% of the limit, which gives hours of warning rather than none, and keeping the higher allocation on three services with known bursty profiles rather than applying a uniform rule. The version I would push back on is a policy that right-sizes everything identically; the savings come from measurement, not from a number.

How did you get 46 services' owners to accept a change to their resources?

I did not ask them to do the work. I produced a per-service page showing observed p99 working set, the current request, the proposed one and the projected saving, then offered to raise the pull request myself and revert instantly on request. Almost everyone agreed; three pushed back with reasons, two of which were good — one had a quarterly batch job that only ran at month-end and was not in my window. That taught me to check for periodicity longer than the observation period, which I now do explicitly. The general lesson is that cost work fails on process rather than analysis: the analysis was a week, the socialisation was three, and the ratio would be the same again.

What would you look at next?

Idle capacity, which is the bigger prize and the harder sell. About a dozen of those services served almost no traffic outside business hours and still ran three replicas around the clock; scale-to-zero or aggressive off-hours scaling would beat the memory win, at the cost of cold start on the first request. That is the point where the Native AOT conversation from the disagreement story becomes relevant again — a worker that starts in 30 ms can scale to zero in a way that one taking 900 ms cannot. I would want per-service traffic profiles and a latency budget for the first request after idle before proposing anything, because the failure mode is a user-visible pause in exchange for money the user does not care about.

Thirty-one .NET services, five teams, four different logging conventions and three tracing approaches — two of them vendor SDKs bolted in at different times, one of them a bespoke correlation-id header that only worked if every hop remembered to forward it. Nobody could answer "why was this request slow" without asking three teams. I proposed standardising on OpenTelemetry and had eighteen months of prior evidence that platform mandates from my part of the organisation went nowhere.

So I did not mandate it. The approach that worked had three moves, and they generalise to almost any tool adoption. First, I made adoption a single line: a shared NuGet package that registered ASP.NET Core, HttpClient, EF Core and runtime instrumentation with our resource attributes and exporter already configured, so a team's cost of joining was adding a package and one call. Second, I picked the two loudest pain owners as the pilot rather than the two most enthusiastic teams — the enthusiasts would have adopted anything and proved nothing; the team that had spent a week on a cross-service latency mystery became the case study. Third, I let the results do the selling: after the pilot, I ran a thirty-minute session where the pilot team debugged a live slow request in front of everyone, in four minutes.

// The shared package's whole surface. Joining costs one line in a service's Program.cs.
public static IHostApplicationBuilder AddPlatformTelemetry(this IHostApplicationBuilder builder)
{
    builder.Logging.AddOpenTelemetry(o => { o.IncludeScopes = true; o.IncludeFormattedMessage = true; });

    builder.Services.AddOpenTelemetry()
        .ConfigureResource(r => r
            .AddService(builder.Environment.ApplicationName)
            .AddAttributes([new("deployment.environment", builder.Environment.EnvironmentName)]))
        .WithTracing(t => t
            .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation(o => o.RecordException = true)
            .AddHttpClientInstrumentation()
            .AddEntityFrameworkCoreInstrumentation()
            .AddOtlpExporter())
        .WithMetrics(m => m
            .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation()      // request duration, active requests, Kestrel connections
            .AddHttpClientInstrumentation()
            .AddRuntimeInstrumentation()          // GC pauses, heap size, thread pool queue, exceptions
            .AddOtlpExporter());

    return builder;
}

The technical case was strong enough to carry itself once people saw it, and it is worth being able to state precisely: OpenTelemetry is not a competing SDK in .NET, it is the native model. ActivitySource and Activity are the OpenTelemetry tracing API expressed in System.Diagnostics, and System.Diagnostics.Metrics.Meter is the metrics API — so ASP.NET Core, Kestrel, HttpClient, EF Core and the runtime emit standard telemetry with no adapter and no vendor coupling. Adopting it is not adding a dependency, it is turning on something the framework is already doing. The vendor lock-in argument was the one that changed the mind of the team most resistant to it: the exporter is a swap, so the choice of backend stops being a five-year commitment.

The hardest part was not adoption, it was semantic conventions. Two teams named the same concept tenant.id and customerId, so cross-service queries silently returned nothing. I wrote a one-page convention — use the OpenTelemetry semantic conventions where they exist, and for our own attributes use dotted lower-case with a fixed prefix — and, more usefully, added a test in the shared package that fails a build if a span sets an attribute outside the agreed vocabulary. A convention with a linter is a standard; a convention in a document is a suggestion.

Twenty-eight of thirty-one services in five months, without a mandate. The three holdouts were a service being decommissioned, one owned by a team in a reorganisation, and one whose tech lead genuinely preferred their existing setup — I left that one alone, because chasing the last 10% would have cost more goodwill than the coverage was worth, and being seen to accept a no is what keeps the yeses voluntary.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why OpenTelemetry rather than the vendor SDK you already had?

Because in .NET it is not an alternative library, it is the native instrumentation model. ActivitySource and Meter in System.Diagnostics are the OpenTelemetry APIs, and the framework libraries — ASP.NET Core, Kestrel, HttpClient, EF Core, the runtime — already emit through them. A vendor SDK means shimming that native signal into a proprietary model, and it means the choice of backend is embedded in every service. With OTel the backend is an exporter configuration, so switching vendors is a deployment change rather than a migration. That argument mattered organisationally as well as technically: it converted "which observability vendor do we marry" from a decision that needed executive alignment into one that could be revisited, which is why it got approved at all.

What is the actual cost of tracing everything? Did you sample?

Yes, and getting it wrong is expensive in both directions. Span creation is cheap when nobody is listening — ActivitySource checks for listeners before allocating, so uninstrumented paths cost close to nothing — but export volume and backend storage are not, and at our request rates head-based sampling at 100% would have been a serious bill. We ran head-based sampling at 10% with two carve-outs: always sample anything that errors, and always sample requests carrying a debug header, so on-call can get a full trace on demand. The refinement I would make now is tail-based sampling for slow requests, which is what you actually want — you cannot know at the head that a request will be interesting — at the cost of a collector that buffers spans, which is real operational surface.

At what scale does the shared-package approach break down?

It breaks when the package needs to change and thirty-one services need to adopt the change. We hit this at a version bump that altered an attribute name: rolling it out took six weeks, and for those six weeks queries spanning old and new services were subtly wrong, which is worse than being broken. Past a few dozen consumers you need either strong backwards compatibility discipline in the package — never rename, only add, deprecate slowly — or out-of-process configuration through an agent or collector so the change lands centrally. I would also say the package must stay thin: ours was tempting to grow into a general platform library, and the moment it carries unrelated concerns, its upgrade cadence is hostage to all of them.

What did you trade away by not mandating it?

Speed and completeness. A mandate might have got thirty-one services in two months instead of twenty-eight in five, and there were weeks where the partial coverage was genuinely worse than uniform bad telemetry, because a trace that stops at an un-instrumented service is confusing in a way that no trace is not. I accepted that because the previous two mandates from my group had produced compliance without engagement — services that adopted the letter and instrumented nothing meaningful — and telemetry that nobody uses is a pure cost. The judgment is that for tools whose value depends on people actually using them, voluntary adoption is slower and stickier, and I would make the same call again.

How did you handle the tech lead who said no?

I asked why, listened, and accepted it. His reasons were partly good — his team had a working setup and a quarter of committed roadmap — and partly a preference I did not share, and I decided not to spend capital distinguishing the two. What I did do was keep the door open concretely: I sent him the pilot team's four-minute debugging session, offered to do the integration myself whenever he wanted, and did not raise it again. They adopted it about eight months later, during a rewrite, without my involvement. I think chasing him would have cost more than the coverage was worth and would have made every other team's yes feel less voluntary, which is the thing that was actually doing the work.

What is different about doing this on .NET 10?

The built-in surface is substantially richer, which shrinks the amount of custom instrumentation a team has to write — and custom instrumentation is where adoption efforts die. ASP.NET Core 10 added first-class authentication and authorization metrics, ASP.NET Core Identity metrics, and a Microsoft.AspNetCore.MemoryPool meter, and the Blazor side gained component lifecycle and circuit metrics. The practical effect is that a team gets a genuinely useful dashboard from the shared package alone, which is exactly the demonstration that sells adoption. If I were starting today I would also seriously evaluate .NET Aspire, whose dashboard consumes OTLP directly and gives local development the same telemetry as production — that removes the "I cannot see it on my machine" objection, which was the second most common one I heard.

18On-call: the 3am hosted-service deadlock

tier: senior → staff archetype: on-call firefighting

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

3:10am, paged for a service that was up, healthy, responding to its liveness probe, and processing exactly zero messages from its queue. Queue depth 84,000 and climbing at about 900 a minute. No errors in the logs — the last log line was two hours old, which is the detail that told me most: a service that has stopped logging has not crashed, it has stopped doing.

The on-call decision came first and it was the right one. I restarted the pods, which cleared the backlog in twenty minutes, and I did that before understanding anything — but not before capturing evidence. A process dump takes about fifteen seconds and is the difference between a diagnosable incident and a recurring mystery, and I have been on the wrong side of that trade enough times to have made it a reflex.

# The fifteen seconds that made the next morning possible.
kubectl exec -it worker-7f9c -- dotnet-dump collect -p 1 -o /tmp/worker.dmp
kubectl cp worker-7f9c:/tmp/worker.dmp ./worker.dmp
# then, and only then:
kubectl rollout restart deploy/worker

The dump told the story in about ten minutes the next morning. dotnet-dump analyze, then dumpasync, showed 200 async state machines all parked on the same SemaphoreSlim.WaitAsync, and syncblk showed a thread holding a lock while inside an async continuation. The bug was a classic and the mechanism is what makes it a good story: a SemaphoreSlim with a count of one was being used to serialise access to a cache, and one code path awaited an HttpClient call while holding it. That call had no timeout. The upstream had gone silent — not returned errors, gone silent, so the TCP connection stayed open and the request never completed. One permit, never released, and every subsequent worker parked behind it forever.

// The bug: a network call inside the critical section, with no timeout, holding the only permit.
await _gate.WaitAsync(ct);
try
{
    if (!_cache.TryGetValue(key, out var v))
        v = await _http.GetFromJsonAsync<Rates>("/rates", ct);   // no timeout: parks forever
    ...
}
finally { _gate.Release(); }

// The fix has three parts, and all three matter.
using var cts = CancellationTokenSource.CreateLinkedTokenSource(ct);
cts.CancelAfter(TimeSpan.FromSeconds(5));                       // 1. every network call is bounded
var v = await _rates.GetAsync(cts.Token);                       // 2. fetch OUTSIDE the lock
await _gate.WaitAsync(cts.Token);
try { _cache.Set(key, v); } finally { _gate.Release(); }         // 3. lock covers memory only

The health check was the second failure, and arguably the worse one. Our liveness probe returned 200 if the process was running, so a completely stuck service looked perfectly healthy for two hours. A liveness probe should assert that the thing the service exists to do is still happening; ours asserted that the CLR had not exited. Rewriting it to check "have we processed a message, or found the queue empty, in the last two minutes" turned a two-hour silent stall into a self-healing 90-second restart. That is the higher-leverage fix by a distance, because it works for the next unknown hang as well as this one.

builder.Services.AddHealthChecks()
    .AddCheck<QueueProgressCheck>("queue-progress", tags: ["live"]);   // liveness: are we making progress?

app.MapHealthChecks("/health/live",  new() { Predicate = c => c.Tags.Contains("live") });
app.MapHealthChecks("/health/ready", new() { Predicate = c => c.Tags.Contains("ready") });

What I would do differently: I did not wake anyone. I spent ninety minutes alone on something that turned out to need a restart, because I did not want to disturb people at 3am, and that is a bad instinct dressed as consideration — a second pair of eyes would have suggested the dump-then-restart sequence twenty minutes earlier. Now my rule is that if I have not understood the shape of an incident within twenty minutes, I page a second person, regardless of the hour. Nobody has ever been annoyed about it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why did the service hang rather than fail?

Because every layer was waiting patiently for something that would never arrive. The upstream had gone silent rather than resetting the connection, so the socket stayed open and the HTTP request had no timeout to bound it. HttpClient's default timeout is 100 seconds and this code had disabled it; even so, the deeper problem is that the await sat inside a critical section guarded by a semaphore with a single permit, so the first stuck request became a global stop. Everything downstream then behaved correctly according to its own rules — the workers waited on the semaphore, the process stayed alive, the CLR had no reason to complain. Hangs are harder than crashes precisely because every component is doing what it was told.

Why is holding a lock across an await so dangerous?

Because it converts a bounded critical section into an unbounded one. A lock around in-memory work is held for microseconds; a lock held across a network call is held for as long as the network takes, which has no upper bound unless you impose one. It also breaks the usual reasoning about lock ordering, since the continuation may resume on a different thread, and it is why you cannot use lock across an await at all — the monitor is thread-affine, which is exactly why people reach for SemaphoreSlim and then forget that it removed the compiler's protection rather than the underlying hazard. My rule is that a critical section may not contain I/O. If the cached value needs fetching, fetch it outside and take the lock only to publish it, accepting that two callers may fetch concurrently — a duplicate fetch is much cheaper than a deadlock.

At what scale does the "fetch outside the lock" fix cause its own problem?

At high concurrency on a cold cache, it becomes a stampede: a thousand concurrent requests all miss, all fetch, and you have turned one upstream call into a thousand at the worst possible moment. The middle ground is to keep a per-key coordination primitive — a Lazy<Task<T>> or a ConcurrentDictionary of in-flight tasks — so concurrent callers for the same key await one shared fetch, while different keys proceed independently. That preserves the single-flight property without a global gate. It still needs a timeout on the shared task, because a stuck fetch now stalls everyone waiting on that key, and it needs the entry evicted on failure so a transient error is not cached as a permanently faulted task.

What did you trade away by restarting before diagnosing?

Live state — I could no longer observe the system in its stuck condition, attach a profiler, or test a hypothesis against it. The dump bought back most of that: async stacks, heap contents, lock ownership. What it did not preserve was anything dynamic, so if the cause had been a timing or a rate rather than a stuck object graph, I would have had far less. I still make the same trade every time, because eighty thousand messages backing up is a customer-facing outage and my curiosity is not, but the fifteen seconds for the dump is non-negotiable — that is the compromise that makes the trade acceptable rather than a coin flip.

How do you write a liveness check that does not do more harm than good?

The key distinction is liveness versus readiness. Liveness answers "would restarting me help?" and must therefore depend only on the service's own progress — a stalled processing loop, a wedged internal state. Readiness answers "should I receive traffic?" and may depend on dependencies. Conflating them is how you get a cascade: if a liveness probe checks the database, a database blip restarts every pod simultaneously, which is strictly worse than doing nothing. ASP.NET Core's health check tags make the split natural, and the other detail people miss is that the check itself must be bounded — a health check that hangs is indistinguishable to the platform from a failure, which is sometimes what you want and should always be deliberate.

How would you make this class of hang visible without a dump?

Progress metrics rather than error metrics, which is the general lesson. A counter of messages processed with an alert on "no increase in five minutes while queue depth is non-zero" catches every hang regardless of cause, and it is trivial to add. Beyond that, the runtime meters give you supporting evidence — a stall on locks shows in System.Runtime contention counters, and a thread pool queue that grows while CPU is idle points at blocking. .NET 10's diagnostics make some of this easier to collect continuously rather than on demand, but the honest answer is that the highest-value change was a two-line metric, not a tool: alert on the absence of expected work, not just on the presence of errors.

19Making the integration suite fast and honest

tier: staff archetype: CI / test reliability

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

The suite took 38 minutes and failed spuriously about one run in six. The team's adaptation was rational and corrosive: everyone had learned to hit re-run, so a genuine failure was indistinguishable from noise and got re-run too. We shipped a real bug to production that CI had correctly caught, twice, and both times someone re-ran it green. That is the incident I used to get the work funded, because "the tests are flaky" had been on the backlog for a year and "CI caught this and we ignored it" got two engineers for a sprint.

I started by measuring rather than fixing, which is the step people skip. A week of instrumented runs showed the failures were not evenly spread: 70% came from eleven tests out of 800, and those eleven shared one property — they asserted on wall-clock timing or on ordering that was never guaranteed. Another 20% came from shared database state, where tests passed alone and failed when a parallel test mutated a row they had assumed was theirs. The remaining 10% were real bugs, which is the number that should have frightened everyone and did not.

The fixes were unglamorous and specific. Time-dependent tests got a fake TimeProvider injected instead of using DateTime.UtcNow, which is the modern answer — TimeProvider arrived in .NET 8 and replaces the homegrown IClock abstraction you will still find in most codebases. State-dependent tests moved to a database container per test class with Testcontainers, so isolation was structural rather than a cleanup convention people forgot. And WebApplicationFactory replaced a hand-rolled host bootstrap, which mattered more than it sounds: it runs the real pipeline — real middleware order, real DI graph, real model binding — so the tests exercise the same composition that production does.

public sealed class ApiFactory : WebApplicationFactory<Program>, IAsyncLifetime
{
    private readonly PostgreSqlContainer _db = new PostgreSqlBuilder().Build();
    public FakeTimeProvider Time { get; } = new(new DateTimeOffset(2026, 9, 8, 12, 0, 0, TimeSpan.Zero));

    protected override void ConfigureWebHost(IWebHostBuilder builder) => builder
        .UseSetting("ConnectionStrings:Main", _db.GetConnectionString())
        .ConfigureTestServices(s =>
        {
            s.AddSingleton<TimeProvider>(Time);            // deterministic clock, no Thread.Sleep anywhere
            s.RemoveAll<IPaymentGateway>();                // the ONE external boundary we fake
            s.AddSingleton<IPaymentGateway, StubGateway>();
        });

    public Task InitializeAsync() => _db.StartAsync();
    async Task IAsyncLifetime.DisposeAsync() => await _db.DisposeAsync();
}

We kept a real PostgreSQL container and got the speed elsewhere: parallelising by test class (38 minutes to 6), reusing a container per class instead of per test, and applying migrations once into a template database that each class snapshots from.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not use the in-memory provider? It would have been much faster.

Because it answers a different question. The EF Core in-memory provider is a LINQ-to-objects store: it does not enforce unique or foreign-key constraints, does not translate queries to SQL so an untranslatable expression silently succeeds, has no real transaction semantics, and differs on case sensitivity, collation and type mapping. So it passes tests for code that throws in production, which is worse than no test because it carries false confidence. Microsoft's own guidance is to use it only where the database is incidental to what you are testing. Our fast suite came from parallelism and container reuse rather than from lowering fidelity — and the honest framing is that I would rather have a six-minute suite I believe than a two-minute one I do not.

Why does WebApplicationFactory matter versus constructing the services yourself?

Because it boots the actual application — the same Program, the same middleware pipeline in the same order, the same DI graph, the same configuration binding and model binding. A hand-rolled host tests a composition that exists nowhere else, which is exactly how you get a suite that is green while production fails: our middleware ordering bugs were invisible to the old tests because the old tests never ran the pipeline. ConfigureTestServices then lets you replace the few genuine external boundaries after the app's own registrations, which is the right seam. The cost is startup time per factory, which is why the container and factory live at class scope rather than per test.

At what scale does container-per-class stop working?

When the container count exceeds what the CI agent can run concurrently, which for us was around twelve on a standard agent before memory and disk I/O became the constraint and the suite got slower, not faster. Past that you shard across agents, or move to a shared container with schema-per-test-class isolation — cheaper to start but reintroduces a class of cross-test interference that you must then be disciplined about. The other scaling limit is start-up cost: at several hundred test classes, even a two-second container start dominates. Our mitigation was migrating once into a template database and having each class create from it, which turned a multi-second migration into a sub-second copy.

What did you trade away for six minutes?

Some determinism in failure attribution. Parallel tests mean a failure can be caused by resource contention on the agent rather than by the code, and we saw a handful of timeouts that were genuinely infrastructure rather than product. We handled it by making timeouts generous and by tagging a small set of tests as serial where they genuinely needed exclusive resources. We also took on operational surface — Testcontainers needs a working Docker daemon on every agent and on every developer machine, which was a real support cost for two people on unusual setups. I would still take the trade, but "it just works everywhere" was not true on day one and I should have said so up front.

How did you handle the tests you wanted to delete?

Carefully, because deleting tests reads as reducing safety and you only get to do it if people trust the reasoning. I proposed a rule rather than a list: delete any test that asserts framework behaviour we do not implement, or that cannot fail without another test also failing. Then I applied it to forty candidates, put them in one pull request with the rule at the top, and asked two people to challenge any of them. Three were rescued, correctly — one was covering a custom model binder I had misread. The general lesson is that for reductions, agree the criterion first and let people audit the application of it; leading with the list turns it into forty arguments.

What would you set up differently on a greenfield .NET 10 service?

Testcontainers and WebApplicationFactory from commit one, because retrofitting isolation is far more expensive than starting with it. TimeProvider injected everywhere rather than DateTime.UtcNow, with FakeTimeProvider in tests — that is now the framework-sanctioned abstraction and it removes a whole flake category permanently. I would put the flake rate on a dashboard from the start, because a number that is visible stays low and one that is not decays quietly. And I would seriously look at .NET Aspire's testing support, which composes the application's dependencies for integration tests the same way it does for local development, so the test environment and the developer environment stop drifting apart.

20Saying no to the EOL migration date

tier: principal archetype: pushing back on a stakeholder

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A VP announced to an all-hands that all services would be on the current LTS runtime "by the end of the quarter" — six weeks — because an auditor had flagged the upcoming end of support. Twenty-two services, four of them with real work involved, one of them with a native dependency that had no build for the target. The date had been chosen without asking anyone who would do the work, and it had been said in public, which is a different problem from a wrong date.

What I did not do was say it was impossible in the meeting. What I did was ask for two days, then come back with three things: an inventory of every service with its current runtime, its support end date and an honest estimate; a proposal with a different shape; and — the part that made it work — agreement on what the auditor actually needed, which turned out to be a documented plan with dates and owners, not completed work. Nobody had checked. The gap between "we must be migrated" and "we must be able to show a credible plan" was five weeks of panic.

The substance mattered too, and it is worth being precise because the support dates are real and current. .NET 8, which four of our services were on, is LTS and reaches end of support in November 2026; .NET 9 is STS and reaches end of support in the same month. .NET 10 is the current LTS, generally available since 11 November 2025 and supported until 10 November 2028. That means a service on .NET 9 gains nothing by moving to a runtime that expires alongside its own, and the only sensible target is .NET 10 — which is also an argument against rushing, because a hurried migration to the wrong target buys twelve months and costs the same effort again.

RuntimeTrackReleasedEnd of supportImplication for the plan

The counter-proposal was: eighteen of twenty-two services migrated in six weeks, because they were genuinely trivial — a target framework bump, a package restore and a test run; three more in ten weeks; and the one with the native dependency treated as its own project with a decision point at week eight between vendoring a build, replacing the library, or accepting a documented exception with a compensating control. I put dates and owners against all of it, which is what the auditor wanted anyway.

How I delivered it mattered as much as the content. I went to the VP privately before saying anything in a group, because he had committed to a date publicly and a public correction would have made the conversation about his credibility rather than about the plan. I opened with what we could do rather than what we could not. And I owned our part honestly: the reason four services were two versions behind was that my organisation had deprioritised upgrades for three quarters, and saying that first made the rest of the conversation collaborative.

What I would do differently: none of this should have been a surprise. The support dates for every .NET release are published years ahead, and there is no excuse for an auditor knowing our exposure before we did. I now keep a runtime inventory with support end dates as a standing dashboard item, reviewed quarterly, so the upgrade is scheduled work rather than a fire — and so that when someone asks, the answer takes ten seconds.

What the interviewer is really probing

Whether you can disagree upward without being either compliant or difficult. The signals are: you brought an alternative rather than an objection, you protected the stakeholder's position in public, and you owned your organisation's contribution to the problem. At principal level they also probe whether you fixed the systemic gap — a leader who wins the argument and lets it recur has not led.

Mistakes candidates make: telling it as a story about being right; making the stakeholder sound foolish; pushing back with no alternative; and no follow-up on what changed so it does not happen again.

(b) STAR/CARL scaffold

  • SituationThe ask, who made it, and why it was unachievable — with the number that makes it obvious.
  • TaskWhat you were protecting: the team, the outcome, or the stakeholder from their own commitment. Often all three.
  • ActionWhat you gathered before speaking, the alternative you brought, how and where you delivered it, and what you owned.
  • ResultThe revised plan, whether it held, and the relationship afterwards.
  • DifferentlyThe visibility gap that let it become a surprise at all.

(c) Recall prompts

When have you told someone senior that their plan would not work?

What did you bring with you? "I said it was unrealistic" is weak; "I brought an inventory and three options" is the answer they want.

Have you ever been overruled and had to commit anyway?

Disagree-and-commit is a real skill. Did you flag the leading indicator to watch? Did you avoid saying "I told you so" when it went wrong?

What deadline have you been surprised by that you should have seen coming?

Support end dates, certificate expiries, a contract renewal, a compliance deadline, a dependency's own EOL. All are knowable years in advance and all catch teams anyway.

Where has your organisation's own neglect created a crisis you had to defend?

Owning this is the highest-credibility move available in this archetype, and almost nobody does it.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is jumping to .NET 9 not a legitimate intermediate step?

Because it expires at the same time as what we are leaving. .NET 9 is a Standard Term Support release — 24 months from its November 2024 launch, so end of support in November 2026, the same month as .NET 8's LTS window closes. Migrating from 8 to 9 would consume the effort and produce a service that needs migrating again inside a year, with no reduction in audit exposure. The general rule I hold is that the target of a compliance-driven upgrade should be the newest LTS, because you are buying support runway rather than features; STS releases are the right choice for teams that upgrade continuously and treat every November as routine, which is a different posture and a legitimate one.

How did you know eighteen services were a week's work?

I did not take it on faith — I did one. I picked a mid-complexity service, ran the target framework bump and the package updates, and had it through CI in about three hours, which gave me a real basis rather than an estimate. Modern .NET major upgrades are usually mechanical because the breaking changes are documented and narrow, and the ones that matter tend to be behavioural rather than compile-time: for the .NET 10 move, cookie authentication returning 401 instead of redirecting on API endpoints, exceptions handled by IExceptionHandler no longer being logged by default, and Blazor's HttpClient response streaming being on by default. So my estimate was "three hours plus a careful read of the breaking-changes list", which held for seventeen of the eighteen.

At what point would you have escalated past the VP?

If he had insisted on the six weeks after seeing the inventory, and specifically if that insistence meant shipping the native-dependency service on an unsupported or hacked-together stack. My threshold for escalation is not disagreement — I am comfortable committing to a decision I argued against — it is when the decision creates a risk the organisation has not knowingly accepted. In that case the escalation would not have been "he is wrong"; it would have been "here is a risk that needs an owner at a level that can accept it", in writing, with him copied and preferably having seen it first. Escalation as information-routing rather than as appeal is the version that does not damage relationships.

What did you trade away by taking two days before responding?

Two days during which the team believed the six-week date was real, and I know at least one person spent a weekend worrying about it. That is a genuine cost and I would reduce it now by sending a short "I am putting together a plan, do not reorganise your work yet" note within the hour. What the two days bought was a conversation grounded in an inventory rather than in impressions, and the difference between "that seems tight" and "here are twenty-two services with dates" is the difference between an opinion and a plan. I would keep the two days; I would not keep the silence.

Did the revised plan actually hold?

Mostly. Eighteen landed in five weeks, slightly ahead. The three medium ones took twelve weeks rather than ten, because one had an EF Core provider that lagged the runtime — a dependency-readiness risk I had not explicitly listed and now always do. The native-dependency service went to the week-eight decision point and we vendored a build, which was the least attractive option and the right one. I reported the two-week slip proactively at the halfway mark rather than at the end, which mattered more than the slip itself: a plan that reports honestly while running is worth more than one that hits its date, because it is the one people will believe next time.

What does your runtime inventory actually track now?

Per service: the target framework, the runtime the container actually ships, that version's support end date, the owning team, and the date of the last successful upgrade. The last field is the interesting one — a service that has not been upgraded in eighteen months is a risk regardless of its current version, because the upgrade muscle has atrophied. It is reviewed quarterly, and anything within nine months of end of support becomes scheduled work automatically rather than needing a case made for it. That last rule is the one that actually changed behaviour: the argument was never about whether to upgrade, it was about whether it could beat feature work in a prioritisation meeting, and taking it out of that meeting was the fix.

These are the prompts that are not tied to one incident. For each, the answer names which prepared story to deploy and what the interviewer is listening for — the point is not a script, it is knowing instantly which of your twenty stories fits, so you spend your thinking budget on the content rather than the search.

Failure & ownership

Tell me about a time you were wrong.

Deploy: 15 (the cancelled migration) at principal level, or 16 (the heap-limit mistake that OOM-killed two services) if you want a tighter, more technical version. Listening for: whether the wrongness is real and costly, and whether the lesson changed a practice rather than producing a resolution. The heap-limit story works well because the mistake was specific, the fix was one line, and the lesson — that a default encoded knowledge you did not have — is genuinely transferable. Avoid failures that are secretly strengths ("I cared too much about quality"); interviewers hear those constantly and discount everything after them.

Tell me about the biggest mistake you have made in production.

Deploy: 12 (four months of silently lost work) or 16. Listening for: blast radius honestly stated, containment before fixing, customer communication, and detection added afterwards. The trap is telling a story where you were the hero of a mess someone else made. Lead with the impact number, own the part that was yours, and spend most of the answer on what you changed structurally — that is where seniority shows.

Describe a project that did not go well.

Deploy: 15. Listening for: your role in the decision to start, not just the decision to stop; the gap between knowing and acting; and how you treated the people involved. The strongest version includes the six weeks of sunk-cost delay, because a leader who claims they killed it the moment they knew is not credible.

When did you take a risk that did not pay off?

Deploy: 15, or the load-shedding call in 03 reframed — you traded a third of a promotion's revenue for stability, and it was the right call with real cost. Listening for: whether you can distinguish a bad outcome from a bad decision, and whether you would take the same risk again given the same information.

Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news.

Deploy: 20 (the EOL date) or 15 (the cancellation note). Listening for: timing — early and imperfect beats late and complete — plus whether you brought options and whether you protected the other person's standing. The detail that lands is going to the VP privately before saying anything in a group.

Conflict & influence

Tell me about a technical disagreement with a peer.

Deploy: 05 (Native AOT vs. JIT). Listening for: whether you converted opinion into measurement, what you conceded, and whether their idea shipped somewhere. Pick a disagreement with a peer or someone senior — a disagreement with someone junior is a coaching story and answers a different question.

How do you handle it when you are overruled?

Deploy: a real one if you have it; otherwise the escalation reasoning from 20's drill chain. Listening for: genuine commitment after the decision, and whether you flagged a leading indicator to watch rather than waiting to be proven right. Say explicitly that you do not relitigate — and mean it, because the follow-up is usually "and what happened when it went wrong?"

Tell me about influencing a team you had no authority over.

Deploy: 17 (OpenTelemetry) or 14 (contract tests in the producer's pipeline). Listening for: that you found the version of your proposal that solved their problem, and that you made saying yes cheap. In 14, framing contract tests as a deprecation capability rather than a safety net is the whole move.

Describe a time you had to give difficult feedback.

Deploy: 08 (the JWT review) for peer-level feedback with a safety dimension, or 10 (async) for developmental feedback. Deploy: 02 (modular monolith). Deploy: 10. Deploy: 02 or 05. Listening for: that you can drive a decision process — criteria, options, an experiment — without needing to be the deepest technically. This is a specific staff-plus competency and candidates rarely address it directly.

Deploy: 10, but be honest about the distinction it raises: a gap in model is cheap to fix, a gap in engagement is not, and you allocate your time differently. Listening for: specific intervention, calibrated investment, and no rescuing that quietly prevents growth.

Deploy: 13 (the compliance feature). Listening for: separating stable from volatile, recording interpretations, and one thing that stayed expensive regardless — the missing data field. That last part stops the answer sounding like architecture solved everything.

Deploy: 11 (nine weeks of scope cut after one call) and 04 (169 report pages deleted rather than migrated). Deploy: 03 (shedding 30% of promotional traffic at 8pm) or 18 (restart before diagnosis). Listening for: what you did to preserve optionality — capturing the dump, communicating the decision within ten minutes — rather than a claim of good instincts.

Deploy: 09 (funded by an incident that had already happened) and 20 (upgrades made automatic rather than prioritised each quarter). Listening for: a mechanism, not a philosophy. The strongest answer takes the recurring argument out of the prioritisation meeting entirely.

Values & motivation

What are you proudest of?

Deploy: 10 or 17 — something whose value came from other people. Listening for: whether your sense of contribution scales beyond your own keyboard. A story about someone else's growth or a capability the organisation now has answers this better than any system you built alone.

Deploy: your own view, grounded. 22Night-before cheat sheet

delivery read this last

Ten things to say to yourself before you walk in. They are about delivery, not content — by now the content is either there or it is not.

  1. Lead with the situation in one sentence, and make it a number. "A checkout API at three hundred requests a second whose p99 went from a hundred and eighty milliseconds to eleven seconds every weekday afternoon." One sentence buys you the interviewer's attention for the next four.
  2. Say the outcome before the journey. Chronological narration is the single most common way strong engineers give weak answers. Cause, then result, then how you got there — they will interrupt you with the follow-up they actually care about, which is what you want.
  3. Quantify the result twice: technically and in consequence. "p99 back to a hundred and eighty milliseconds" is half an answer. "…and the spurious pod restarts stopped, which had been paging someone twice a week" is the other half.
  4. Separate "I" from "we" without being coy. Say "the team shipped it; I owned the diagnosis and the call to mitigate first". Interviewers are not scoring solo heroics — they are scoring whether they can tell what you did, and vagueness reads as inflation.
  5. Own the "what I would do differently" before you are asked. Volunteering it makes everything preceding it more believable. Make it a change in practice, not a feeling: "I now start any leak investigation past an hour with a heap diff."
  6. Name the trade-off in every technical decision. "We chose X" is a statement; "we chose X, which cost us Y, and here is when I would have chosen otherwise" is an engineer. For this topic the recurring ones are throughput against memory, availability against correctness, and flexibility against directness.
  7. Say the tool and the counter. dotnet-counters and threadpool-queue-length. dotnet-dump and gcroot. dumpasync. Naming the exact instrument is the cheapest credibility available and most candidates gesture instead.
  8. Be precise about versions and dates. .NET 10 is the current LTS, GA 11 November 2025, supported to November 2028; .NET 8 and .NET 9 both leave support in November 2026; .NET 11 is in preview for a November 2026 release. Knowing that DATAS became the Server GC default in .NET 9, or that ASP.NET Core 10 changed cookie auth to return 401 on API endpoints instead of redirecting, signals current more than any adjective.
  9. When you do not know, say so and then reason. "I have not measured that — my expectation would be X, because Y, and I would check it with Z." That answer scores better than a confident guess, and at lead level it is often what is actually being tested.
  10. Let the drill-downs land where you are deep. You cannot control the question, but you can end an answer on the sentence you want probed. Finish on the mechanism you understand best — the thread pool's injection rate, why an outbox is atomic, what a mesh cannot see — and the follow-up will take you somewhere you are strong.
One last check

Every worked example on this page is fiction, written to jog your memory. Before the interview, make sure each story you plan to tell is yours — with your numbers, your team, your mistake. A borrowed story survives one question and collapses on the second, and the second question is always coming.
