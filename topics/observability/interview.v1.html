Observability & prod debugging

interview · OpenTelemetry specification 1.60.0 · released 2026-08-07

An observability interview at lead level is almost never a tool quiz: it tests whether you can take a vague symptom — "checkout is slow for some users" — and drive it to a mechanism, with evidence, while the clock runs and a customer is on the phone. Expect a loop to move from "what is the difference between a counter and a gauge" through "here is a dashboard and a trace, tell me what is wrong" to "design the telemetry pipeline for 400 services on a fixed budget" and "your burn-rate alert has been firing for three weeks, what do you do". Work the map below in frequency order: units 01-08 appear in nearly every loop and carry two thirds of this bank, 09-12 are where a senior back-end or SRE loop goes next, and 13-15 are specialist and ceiling material. Every answer is written to be spoken in 30-60 seconds and to survive one follow-up, and every block ends with the plausible wrong answer that gets candidates rejected — read those even when you skip the answers. Assumptions, stated once: this bank is vendor-neutral and anchored on OpenTelemetry (spec 1.60.0, 2026-08-07) with Prometheus/PromQL as the metrics reference and a generic trace/log backend, because that is what interviewers standardise on; vendor product trivia (Datadog monitor syntax, Splunk SPL) is out of scope and named only where it changes a decision.

01Foundations and the signal model

Every observability loop opens here, because it is the cheapest way to separate people who have bought a tool from people who have debugged with one. At screener level it is definitions; at senior level it is "which signal answers which question, and what does each one cost"; at lead level it is whether you can say what makes a system observable before the incident, and how you would know if your own telemetry is good enough. Expect the interviewer to push on unknown-unknowns and on cardinality within two questions of the definitions.

screenerWhat is the difference between monitoring and observability?

Answer

Monitoring is asking questions you decided on in advance: you pick the failure modes you expect, emit a signal for each, and alert when it crosses a threshold. Observability is the property that lets you ask questions you did not anticipate, after the fact, without shipping new code — you can explain any state the system got into from the telemetry it already emits. The practical test is the unknown-unknown: if answering "why are 0.3% of Android users on the EU cluster getting 502s after the last deploy?" requires a code change and a redeploy, you have monitoring, not observability. Monitoring is a subset, not a rival: you still want a small set of high-signal alerts on symptoms, backed by high-dimensional data you can slice arbitrarily.

Example

# monitoring: a question you decided on in advance
sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_count{status=~"5.."}[5m])) > 10

# observability: the same event stream, sliced by a dimension nobody pre-aggregated
#   -> traces/logs filtered on service, deploy.id, client.version, cloud.region, tenant
#      answered in seconds, with no code change

Follow-ups

  • Is observability just "three tools"? — No. It is a property of the data: high-dimensional, high-cardinality, correlatable. You can own all three tools and still be unable to answer a new question.
  • So do dashboards still matter? — Yes, as an entry point and a shared mental model during an incident, but a dashboard is a saved question; the debugging happens in the ad-hoc query after it.
  • Where does the term come from? — Control theory: a system is observable if its internal state can be inferred from its outputs. The engineering version is the same claim about production.

Common wrong answer

"Monitoring is metrics; observability is metrics plus logs plus traces." It sounds informed and it is the marketing definition, so interviewers hear it constantly — and it collapses the moment they ask what you would do with a symptom nobody instrumented. Frame it as pre-decided questions versus arbitrary questions, then mention the signals as the means.

screenerMetrics, logs, traces — and now profiles. What is each good at, and bad at?

Answer

Metrics are pre-aggregated numeric time series: cheap, constant cost per series regardless of traffic, ideal for alerting, SLOs and "is it happening and how much" — but they lose the individual event, so they cannot tell you which request or which customer. Logs are discrete events with arbitrary payload: maximum detail, best for "what exactly happened at 14:32:07", but cost scales with traffic and they are unstructured unless you make them structured. Traces are causally linked spans across services: the only signal that answers "where did the time go and who called whom", but they are sampled, so they are a sample of behaviour, not a census. Profiles — CPU and memory samples attributed to code — answer "which function burned the time", closing the last gap between "service X is slow" and a line number; the OpenTelemetry Profiles signal entered public alpha in March 2026, so treat it as promising, not settled.

Example

QuestionSignalWhy not the others
Are we breaking the SLO right now?MetricsTraces are sampled; logs are too slow and too expensive to aggregate
Which downstream call added 400 ms?TracesMetrics have no causality; logs have no parent/child
What was the exact payload that blew up?LogsMetrics threw the detail away; span attributes are usually truncated
Why is CPU at 90% with flat traffic?ProfilesEverything else tells you that it is, not where

Follow-ups

  • Which one would you keep if you could only have one? — Metrics, for alerting and SLOs; you cannot run a pager rotation on sampled traces. But say you would immediately fight to get traces back.
  • Are the "three pillars" still the right model? — The critique is fair: three siloed stores with three query languages is a cost and a correlation problem. The modern framing is one event model with different projections, linked by trace ID and resource attributes.
  • Is profiling really a fourth signal? — In OpenTelemetry, yes: a distinct signal with its own data model, collector support from v0.148.0, and an eBPF profiler shipped as an official collector distribution. Public alpha as of March 2026.

Common wrong answer

"They're three views of the same data, so it doesn't really matter which you use." It sounds sophisticated but it hides the fact that their cost models are completely different: metrics cost by cardinality, logs by volume, traces by sample rate. Interviewers ask this precisely to see whether you know which one you can afford to turn up mid-incident.

midWhat actually makes a system observable? Concrete properties, not adjectives.

Answer

Four properties. First, high dimensionality: events carry many attributes — user, tenant, region, deploy ID, client version, feature-flag state, instance — so you can slice along a dimension you did not think of. Second, high cardinality: those attributes are allowed millions of distinct values (user ID, request ID), because the interesting outages are always a thin slice. Third, correlation: every signal carries the same identifiers — trace ID, span ID, a shared resource identity — so you can jump from a metric spike to the exact traces to the logs of those traces. Fourth, arbitrary query at interactive latency, because an investigation is twenty questions in fifteen minutes, and a store that takes two minutes per query is unusable during an incident.

Example

{
  "timestamp": "2026-09-08T14:32:07.412Z",
  "trace_id": "4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736",
  "span_id":  "00f067aa0ba902b7",
  "service.name": "checkout", "service.version": "2026.9.1",
  "deployment.environment.name": "prod", "cloud.region": "eu-west-1",
  "http.route": "/api/checkout", "http.response.status_code": 502,
  "enduser.pseudo.id": "u_88213", "tenant.id": "acme",
  "feature.new_pricing": true, "db.pool.wait_ms": 1840,
  "duration_ms": 2317
}

One event like this answers a hundred questions. A counter named checkout_errors_total answers one.

Follow-ups

  • Doesn't high cardinality break your metrics store? — In a metrics store, yes: cardinality is the cost driver. Which is exactly why high-cardinality attributes belong on spans and events, not on labels.
  • How do you keep those attributes consistent across 200 services? — Semantic conventions plus a shared instrumentation library or a collector processor, so tenant.id means the same thing everywhere. Diverging attribute names are the number-one reason correlation fails.
  • Cheapest step towards this from a legacy stack? — Put the trace ID in every log line and adopt structured logging. That one change makes the existing logs correlatable.

Common wrong answer

"You add more dashboards and more alerts." That is the reflex from a monitoring background, and it makes things worse: more pre-decided questions, more alert fatigue, no new answers. Observability is about the shape of the data, not the number of saved views over it.

midWhat is a "wide event", and why do people argue it should replace metrics and logs?

Answer

A wide event is one structured record per unit of work — per request, per job, per span — carrying every attribute known at that point, typically dozens to low hundreds of fields, emitted once instead of as five log lines and three counters. The argument for it is that aggregation should be a query-time decision: if you keep the raw dimensions you can compute any metric later, but if you pre-aggregate into counters you have destroyed the dimensions forever. The argument against is cost and query engine — you are storing one fat row per request and you need a column store that aggregates billions of them interactively. Most teams land in the middle: wide events (spans) as the source of truth for investigation, plus a small set of derived metrics for alerting and SLOs, because you cannot page on a query that scans a petabyte.

Example

// legacy: five log lines and two counters, dimensions scattered and lost
logger.LogInformation("start checkout {CartId}", cartId);
metrics.Counter("checkout.started").Add(1);
// ... later, elsewhere ...
metrics.Counter("checkout.failed").Add(1, new("reason", "payment"));

// modern: one span == one wide event, every dimension on the unit of work
using var span = tracer.StartActiveSpan("checkout");
span.SetAttribute("cart.id", cartId);
span.SetAttribute("cart.item_count", cart.Items.Count);
span.SetAttribute("tenant.id", tenant);
span.SetAttribute("payment.provider", provider);
span.SetAttribute("pricing.experiment", variant);
span.SetStatus(Status.Error.WithDescription("payment_declined"));

Follow-ups

  • If wide events are so good, why keep metrics at all? — Alert evaluation and long retention. A counter costs the same at 1 rps and 100k rps; wide events do not, and you need thirteen months of SLO history you cannot afford as raw events.
  • How wide is too wide? — When you cannot say what a field means or who reads it. Practically, SDKs impose limits (OTel defaults to 128 attributes per span); beyond that you are logging a heap dump.
  • How does this relate to spans? — A span is a wide event with a start, a duration and a parent. Adopting tracing properly gets you most of the wide-event model for free.

Common wrong answer

"Wide events replace metrics entirely — query-time aggregation is strictly better." Strictly better on flexibility, strictly worse on cost and alert-evaluation latency. Candidates who cannot name the cost side sound like they have read the blog posts but never paid the bill.

seniorHow do you correlate a metric spike with the specific requests that caused it?

Answer

Three mechanisms, in increasing order of usefulness. Exemplars: a histogram bucket carries a handful of sample trace IDs alongside the count, so clicking the p99 bucket jumps straight to a trace that landed in it — the highest-value correlation feature there is, and badly under-used. Shared resource identity: every signal carries the same resource attributes (service.name, service.instance.id, k8s.pod.name, deployment.environment.name), so you can pivot from a metric on one pod to the logs and traces of that same pod without guessing. And trace context in logs — trace_id and span_id on every record — which turns log search into "show me everything inside this trace". Without exemplars the workflow degrades to "find the time window, then hope tail sampling kept a slow trace from it".

Example

# Prometheus 3.x: exemplar storage on
storage:
  exemplars:
    max_exemplars: 100000

# the OpenMetrics exposition line, carrying a trace ID next to the bucket count:
# http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{le="2.5"} 1027 # {trace_id="4bf92f35..."} 2.31 1757340727.412

Follow-ups

  • Why do exemplars so often come back empty? — The scrape does not negotiate OpenMetrics/exemplars, the SDK only attaches them for sampled spans, or tail sampling later dropped the trace the exemplar points at. Exemplar retention and trace retention must agree.
  • What if you have no exemplars? — Pivot on resource attributes plus time window, and make sure the slow path is always sampled (a local rule that force-samples spans over a latency threshold).
  • What breaks correlation most often in real estates? — Attribute drift: one team emits service, another service.name, a third app. Enforce it in the collector, not in code review.

Common wrong answer

"I'd look at the logs from the same timestamp." That is correlation by coincidence: at 40k rps the same second contains 40,000 unrelated requests. Interviewers want identifiers — trace ID, exemplar, resource — not timestamps.

seniorWhat is a Resource in OpenTelemetry, and what changed with Entities?

Answer

A Resource is the immutable set of attributes describing who is emitting the telemetry — service.name, service.version, service.instance.id, host.name, k8s.pod.name, cloud.region — attached once to every span, metric and log from that SDK instance rather than repeated per event. It is the join key that makes cross-signal correlation possible, and service.name is the one attribute the spec requires. Its long-standing gap is that a resource is a flat bag: it cannot say this pod belongs to that node in that cluster, and it cannot express lifecycle. Entities are the fix — the entity model gives telemetry-producing things a type and identifying attributes, entity events shipped in spec 1.58.0 (2026-06-22) as OTLP log records, and spec 1.60.0 (2026-08-07) added Entity support to the Resource SDK itself. Mention entities as the direction of travel, not as something you have already migrated to.

Example

# declarative config — stable since March 2026 (opentelemetry-configuration 1.0.0),
# pointed at by OTEL_CONFIG_FILE
file_format: "1.0"
resource:
  attributes:
    - name: service.name
      value: checkout
    - name: service.version
      value: 2026.9.1
    - name: deployment.environment.name
      value: prod
  detectors:
    - host:
    - container:

Follow-ups

  • Where should resource attributes come from? — Detectors and the deployment environment (env vars, the Kubernetes downward API, the collector's resourcedetection processor), never hard-coded in source, or every redeploy becomes a code change.
  • What happens if two services share a service.name? — Their telemetry merges and your dashboards silently lie. The most common and most damaging naming mistake in a large estate.
  • Legacy pattern to name? — Stuffing host and environment into every metric label by hand; it multiplies cardinality and drifts across teams.

Common wrong answer

"Resource attributes are just default labels, so I put the customer ID there too." Resource attributes are per-SDK-instance and immutable for the process lifetime; a per-request value there is either wrong or forces a new resource per request and explodes the backend's series count.

seniorBlack-box versus white-box monitoring — when do you need both?

Answer

Black-box monitoring probes the system the way a user does — a synthetic transaction against the public endpoint, from outside your network — and tells you whether the thing works, with no knowledge of internals. White-box monitoring reads the system's own instrumentation and tells you why. You need both because they fail in opposite directions: white-box misses everything outside the instrumented perimeter (DNS, CDN, an expired TLS certificate, a broken load balancer, a BGP problem, your own telemetry pipeline being down), while black-box cannot localise a fault and has terrible resolution — one probe per region per minute is a handful of data points, not a signal. The senior framing is that black-box probes are your independent check on the whole stack including the observability stack, which is why they should run from outside your cloud account and page loudly, while white-box drives everything else.

Example

# black-box: does the user-visible path work, seen from outside?
probe_success{job="blackbox", target="https://api.example.com/health"} == 0

# white-box: the same outage, localised
sum by (pool) (db_client_connections_pending) > 50

Follow-ups

  • Where should synthetics run from? — Outside the environment they test, ideally a different provider or region, or a control-plane outage takes down the monitoring along with the service.
  • Do synthetics replace real user monitoring? — No: synthetics have perfect measurement availability and zero coverage of real client diversity. RUM has the opposite profile.
  • What is the classic gap black-box catches? — Certificate expiry and DNS. Neither is visible from inside the application, and both are total outages.

Common wrong answer

"We have full OpenTelemetry coverage, so we don't need synthetics." This gets people rejected by SRE panels: it assumes the failure is always inside the instrumented code, and forgets that the telemetry pipeline is itself a system that can fail silently and take your visibility with it.

principalHow would you measure whether your organisation's observability is actually good?

Answer

Measure outcomes and behaviour, never coverage. Outcomes: time to detect (track how many incidents were customer-reported rather than alerted), time to first useful hypothesis, and the share of postmortems whose action items are "add instrumentation" — a high share means your telemetry fails exactly when it matters. Behaviour: how many distinct people query telemetry per week (if three people can drive it, you have a bus factor, not a practice), how often a novel question is answerable without a code change, and alert quality — actionable rate, pages per on-call shift, and how many alerts are permanently silenced. Cost per useful answer is the fourth axis and the one leads are judged on: spend divided by incidents resolved, attributed per team so they can see their own bill. Do not report "percentage of services instrumented"; estates at 100% coverage still have incidents where nobody can see anything, because coverage says nothing about whether the right attributes are on the right events.

Detection<10% of incidents customer-reportedUsers tell you first

Alerts>70% of pages actionedStanding silences, muted channels

Follow-ups

  • How do you get a team to care? — Make the bill and the pager theirs. Teams that pay for their telemetry and carry their own pager converge on good instrumentation without a mandate.
  • One metric for a leadership slide? — Share of incidents detected by our own alerting before a customer reported it. Honest, unambiguous, and it cannot be gamed by adding dashboards.
  • What about a team that refuses to instrument? — Auto-instrumentation and a collector-level baseline give them coverage for free; then the conversation is about the last 20%, not about all of it.

Common wrong answer

"We track instrumentation coverage and dashboard count." Both are activity metrics that rise while capability stays flat, and experienced interviewers read them as a tell that the candidate has run a programme rather than an incident. Anchor on detection, postmortem gaps and cost per answer.

02Metrics and time series

Metrics are where interviewers check that you understand a data model rather than a UI, because almost every bad dashboard in the world comes from a misunderstanding in this unit. Screener level is instrument types and rate(); mid level is percentiles and cardinality; senior level is temporality, histogram formats and query correctness under counter resets; principal level is standards and cost across hundreds of services. If you only prepare one PromQL fact, make it "always rate() before you aggregate a counter".

screenerCounter, gauge, histogram, summary — what is the difference and when do you use each?

Answer

A counter only goes up (and resets to zero on process restart); you never read its value, you read its rate(). Use it for requests, errors, bytes, retries — anything you count. A gauge goes up and down and you read the current value: queue depth, in-flight requests, temperature, memory in use. A histogram records a distribution by counting observations into buckets, so you can compute quantiles across instances at query time — that is the key property. A summary computes quantiles inside the client process, which makes them cheap to read but impossible to aggregate: the p99 of three instances is not the average of their p99s, so summaries are effectively deprecated for anything multi-instance. In OpenTelemetry the equivalents are Counter/UpDownCounter, Gauge (usually observable), and Histogram — plus ExponentialHistogram, which is the modern default for latency.

Example

var meter = new Meter("checkout", "1.0.0");

// counter: only ever increases
var requests = meter.CreateCounter<long>("http.server.requests");

// up/down counter: a level, reported as a delta
var inFlight = meter.CreateUpDownCounter<long>("http.server.active_requests");

// observable gauge: sampled at collection time
meter.CreateObservableGauge("queue.depth", () => queue.Count);

// histogram: distribution, aggregatable across instances
var duration = meter.CreateHistogram<double>(
    "http.server.request.duration", unit: "s");

Follow-ups

  • Why is a summary bad? — Client-side quantiles cannot be re-aggregated. Ten pods each reporting p99=100 ms tell you nothing about the fleet p99.
  • When is a gauge the wrong choice? — When the quantity is a rate of events. A gauge sampled every 15 s misses everything between samples; a counter loses nothing.
  • What is an UpDownCounter for, if a gauge exists? — It reports a delta rather than an absolute reading, so it survives being summed across instances and is cheap to increment in hot paths.

Common wrong answer

"A histogram and a summary are basically the same, pick either." They differ on the one axis that matters in a distributed system — where the quantile is computed. Saying they are interchangeable tells the interviewer you have never had to explain why the fleet p99 on the dashboard was wrong.

screenerWhy do you write sum(rate(x[5m])) and never rate(sum(x)[5m])?

Answer

Because counters reset. rate() is defined over a single time series and knows how to detect and correct a reset (a sample lower than the previous one means the process restarted, so the drop is not negative traffic). If you sum first, one pod restarting makes the summed series drop by that pod's whole accumulated total, and the subsequent rate() either goes hugely negative or gets clamped — either way the number is wrong exactly when a pod is crashlooping, which is exactly when you are looking at it. So: rate() per series first, then aggregate. The same rule applies to increase() and irate(), and it is the single most common PromQL bug in production dashboards.

Example

# correct: per-series rate, then aggregate
sum by (service) (rate(http_server_requests_total[5m]))

# error rate as a ratio — same rule on both sides
sum(rate(http_server_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]))
  /
sum(rate(http_server_requests_total[5m]))

# WRONG: aggregation before rate; a pod restart poisons the whole panel
# rate(sum(http_server_requests_total)[5m:])

Follow-ups

  • rate vs irate vs increase? — rate is an extrapolated per-second average over the window (use it); irate uses only the last two samples (spiky, for graphs of fast-moving signals); increase is rate × window, for "how many in the last hour".
  • How do you choose the window? — At least 4× the scrape interval so a missed scrape does not empty the window; 5m over a 15s scrape is the safe default. Alert windows want to be longer than one scrape gap.
  • What is the extrapolation gotcha? — rate/increase extrapolate to the window edges, so increase can return non-integer counts like 3.7. That is expected, not a bug.

Common wrong answer

"It doesn't matter, both give roughly the same number." They agree only while nothing restarts. Interviewers ask this because the wrong form silently produces correct-looking graphs for weeks and then lies during the deploy that caused the incident.

midHow is a p99 computed from a histogram, and how wrong can it be?

Answer

A classic Prometheus histogram is a set of cumulative bucket counters (_bucket{le="0.1"}, le="0.25", …, le="+Inf"). histogram_quantile() finds the bucket containing the 99th percentile of observations and linearly interpolates inside it, which means the answer is only as precise as your bucket boundaries. If your highest finite bucket is 1 s and the real p99 is 4 s, the function cannot tell you 4 s — it will report something at or near 1 s, or bail to the +Inf bucket. That is why latency buckets must be chosen to bracket your SLO and your tail: a bucket boundary exactly at the SLO threshold makes the SLO query exact rather than interpolated. Native (exponential) histograms remove most of this problem by using exponentially spaced buckets with a configurable relative error, which is why they are the modern choice.

Example

# classic histogram: quantile by interpolation, per route
histogram_quantile(0.99,
  sum by (le, http_route) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])))

# what you actually want for an SLO — an exact ratio, no interpolation,
# because 0.5 is a real bucket boundary
sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{le="0.5"}[5m]))
  /
sum(rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_count[5m]))

Follow-ups

  • Why must le stay in the sum by? — histogram_quantile needs the whole bucket ladder. Aggregating le away leaves it with a single number and it returns nonsense.
  • What does a p99 of exactly the top bucket mean? — Your tail is off the end of the ladder; the value is a floor, not a measurement. Add buckets or move to native histograms.
  • Can you average percentiles across services? — No. Averaging quantiles is meaningless; re-aggregate the buckets and recompute.

Common wrong answer

"The p99 is whatever the dashboard says; it's exact." Every classic-histogram quantile is an interpolation over buckets somebody chose two years ago. Candidates who cannot explain that the accuracy is a configuration decision get caught the moment the interviewer asks why the SLO panel and the trace data disagree.

midWhat is cardinality, why does it kill metrics systems, and how do you control it?

Answer

Cardinality is the number of distinct time series, and it is the product of the distinct values of every label on a metric — add a label with 10,000 values to a metric that already has 50 series and you now have 500,000. It kills metrics systems because each series has an index entry, an in-memory head block and its own chunk on disk; cost, memory and query latency all scale with series count, not with sample volume. The controls, in order of effectiveness: never put unbounded values (user ID, request ID, full URL path, error message, timestamp) in labels; normalise before labelling (http.route — the templated route — not http.target, the raw path); enforce limits in the collector or the SDK with attribute allow-lists and a cardinality limiter; and set per-tenant series limits in the backend so one team cannot take down everyone else's. High-cardinality dimensions still belong in your telemetry — just on spans and logs, where the cost model is per-event rather than per-series.

Example

# collector: drop the two attributes that explode series count,
# and cap what survives
processors:
  attributes/metrics:
    actions:
      - key: user.id        # unbounded — belongs on the span, not the metric
        action: delete
      - key: http.target    # raw path with IDs in it
        action: delete
  transform/route:
    metric_statements:
      - set(attributes["http.route"], "/orders/{id}")
        where attributes["http.route"] == nil

Follow-ups

  • How do you find the offender fast? — Prometheus TSDB status page (top series by metric name and by label), or topk(20, count by (__name__)({__name__=~".+"})). In a hosted backend, the per-metric series count report.
  • What is a safe rule of thumb per metric? — Keep any single metric under a few thousand series per service; treat anything over ~100k series for one metric name as an incident waiting to happen.
  • Does OpenTelemetry help? — Yes: SDK views can drop or rename attributes before aggregation, and the collector can enforce it centrally for teams that will not change code.

Common wrong answer

"We just add more memory to Prometheus." It buys a month and hides the real defect — an unbounded label somebody added deliberately. Interviewers want to hear that the fix is at the emission point plus a guard rail, not at the storage layer.

midPull versus push for metrics — which do you pick and why?

Answer

Pull (Prometheus scraping a /metrics endpoint) gives you service discovery as the source of truth, a free liveness signal (up), natural back-pressure — a slow backend just scrapes less — and easy local debugging, because you can curl the endpoint. Push (OTLP to a collector or gateway) is the only option for short-lived things — batch jobs, serverless functions, browsers, mobile clients — that will be gone before any scraper arrives, and it works across network boundaries where inbound connections are impossible. The modern answer is that this is no longer a religious war: applications emit OTLP to a local collector, and the collector decides — it can expose a Prometheus endpoint to be scraped, or remote-write onwards. Prometheus 3.x also accepts OTLP directly on /api/v1/otlp/v1/metrics, and with UTF-8 metric names it can store http.server.request.duration without mangling dots into underscores.

Example

# collector as the adapter: push in, pull out
receivers:
  otlp:
    protocols: { grpc: {}, http: {} }
exporters:
  prometheus:                 # scraped by Prometheus
    endpoint: "0.0.0.0:8889"
  prometheusremotewrite:      # or pushed onward
    endpoint: "https://mimir.internal/api/v1/push"
service:
  pipelines:
    metrics:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [batch]
      exporters: [prometheus]

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle a batch job with pull? — Pushgateway, historically — but it is a known foot-gun (stale series persist forever). The current answer is to push OTLP to a collector instead.
  • What is staleness and why does it matter? — Prometheus marks a series stale when a scrape target disappears, so queries stop returning a frozen last value. Push pipelines must emulate this or your dashboards show dead pods forever.
  • What does the up metric give you for free? — A per-target health signal, which is why pull systems detect a dead instance without the instance cooperating.

Common wrong answer

"Push is modern, pull is legacy." Interviewers hear this from candidates who only know one vendor agent. Pull's service-discovery-driven liveness detection has no clean push equivalent, and the real production answer is a collector that does both.

seniorClassic buckets versus native (exponential) histograms — when do you migrate?

Answer

A classic histogram is N separate counter series, one per bucket boundary, chosen by hand at instrumentation time: cheap to query, expensive in cardinality (a 12-bucket latency histogram with 20 routes is 260 series), and permanently wrong if you guessed the boundaries badly. A native histogram is a single series whose buckets are exponentially spaced with a configurable schema (relative error), so it covers nanoseconds to minutes automatically at roughly the cost of one series, and quantiles get better rather than worse in the tail. Migrate when your latency histograms dominate your series count or when your buckets do not bracket the real tail — those are the two symptoms. The caveats are real: consumers must support the format (some exporters, recording rules and dashboards still assume _bucket series), so the transition path matters. Prometheus added Native Histograms with Custom Buckets (NHCB) so classic-bucket data can be stored in the native composite structure, and the project's 2026 work on composite samples is aimed squarely at making that migration non-breaking — expect a follow-up on how you would run both in parallel.

Example

# classic: N series per histogram, le must survive aggregation
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])))

# native: one series; the quantile function reads the whole distribution
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds[5m])))

Follow-ups

  • How do you migrate without breaking dashboards? — Dual-emit for one retention period, port panels and recording rules, verify the quantiles agree within tolerance, then drop the classic buckets.
  • What is the accuracy knob? — The schema/scale: higher scale means finer relative error and more buckets. You are trading storage against tail resolution, and unlike classic buckets you can change it later.
  • Any reason to keep classic buckets? — Yes: an SLO threshold that must be exact wants a real bucket boundary at that threshold, and some downstream systems still only speak classic.

Common wrong answer

"Native histograms are strictly better, we'd switch everything on Monday." The data model is better; the ecosystem is not uniformly ready, and a big-bang switch silently breaks every recording rule and alert that references _bucket. The senior answer always includes the dual-emit window.

seniorDelta versus cumulative temporality — why does it matter and where does it bite?

Answer

Cumulative means each export carries the running total since the start of the process; delta means each export carries only what happened since the last export. Cumulative is what Prometheus assumes: it is resilient to a lost export (the next one still carries the total) and it lets the server detect resets, which is why rate() works. Delta is what many vendor agents and short-lived workloads prefer: a lost export loses that window forever, but it is stateless in the client and it is the only sane model for a function that exits after 200 ms. Where it bites is the boundary: OpenTelemetry SDKs can be configured per-instrument with a temporality preference, and if you push delta metrics into a cumulative store you need conversion with per-series state — which is exactly what Prometheus's OTLP receiver does when you enable delta-to-cumulative conversion. As of 2026 native delta ingestion in Prometheus is still blocked on the start-timestamp work; the February 2026 composite-samples proposal names it explicitly, so the honest interview answer is "convert at the collector, and know that you are paying for that state".

Example

# OTel SDK: delta for counters/histograms (what most vendor backends want)
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_METRICS_TEMPORALITY_PREFERENCE=delta

# ... or cumulative (what Prometheus wants) — the default
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_METRICS_TEMPORALITY_PREFERENCE=cumulative

Follow-ups

  • What is the cost of delta-to-cumulative in the collector? — Per-series state in memory, and correctness depends on all points for a series reaching the same collector instance — so it breaks under naive load balancing.
  • Which is safer under an unreliable network? — Cumulative: a dropped export is self-healing. Delta loses that interval permanently.
  • Why do serverless platforms prefer delta? — There is no long-lived process to hold a running total, and the instance disappears between invocations.

Common wrong answer

"Temporality is an implementation detail, the backend sorts it out." It is a data-model decision that changes what a lost packet costs you and whether you can load-balance your collectors statelessly. Candidates who wave it away usually also cannot explain why their counters went flat after a collector scale-out.

seniorShow me the PromQL mistakes you look for in a code review.

Answer

Five, in the order I find them. Aggregating before rate(), which breaks on counter resets. Averaging an average — avg(avg_over_time(...)) or averaging per-pod means — which weights a pod serving 3 rps the same as one serving 3,000; you want sum(rate(sum_seconds)) / sum(rate(count)). Missing by/without discipline, so a panel silently merges prod and staging, or drops le from a histogram. Range windows shorter than 4× the scrape interval, which return empty during a single missed scrape and make an alert flap. And comparing a ratio to a threshold without guarding for a zero denominator — at 03:00 with no traffic, errors/total is NaN and your "error rate > 1%" alert either never fires or fires forever, depending on the operator.

Example

# WRONG: unweighted mean of per-pod means
avg(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_sum[5m])
    / rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count[5m]))

# RIGHT: traffic-weighted fleet mean
sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_sum[5m]))
  /
sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count[5m]))

# guard the no-traffic case so the alert does not evaluate to NaN
(
  sum(rate(requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]))
  /
  sum(rate(requests_total[5m]))
) > 0.01
and
sum(rate(requests_total[5m])) > 1

Follow-ups

  • How do you test a PromQL rule? — promtool test rules with a unit-test YAML: it takes a synthetic series, a time, and the expected result. Almost nobody does this, and mentioning it lands well.
  • What does absent() buy you? — Alerting on a metric that stopped existing — the failure mode where a service dies and its series vanishes, so every threshold alert quietly stops evaluating.
  • How do you keep dashboards fast? — Recording rules for the expensive aggregations, evaluated once per interval instead of once per dashboard view.

Common wrong answer

"If it renders a graph, it's fine." The dangerous PromQL bugs all render beautiful graphs — they are only wrong during restarts, during no-traffic windows, or when a pod is unhealthy. That is precisely when someone is looking at the graph.

seniorHow do you keep thirteen months of metrics without paying for thirteen months of raw resolution?

Answer

Tier it. Keep raw 15-second data for a short local window — two to six weeks is typical, enough for incident forensics — and push everything to long-term object storage (Thanos, Mimir, Cortex, or a hosted equivalent) where it is compacted and downsampled to 5-minute and 1-hour resolutions. Precompute what you will actually need at thirteen months with recording rules: SLI numerator and denominator series, per-service request rates, error ratios. That way the expensive high-cardinality raw data expires while the handful of series that feed compliance reports, capacity planning and SLO history survive at a few hundred bytes per day. The failure mode to name is downsampling the wrong aggregate: a downsampled max is not a downsampled p99, and if you only kept the average you can never reconstruct the tail.

Example

groups:
  - name: sli
    interval: 30s
    rules:
      # SLI numerator/denominator, kept forever, tiny
      - record: sli:http_requests:rate5m
        expr: sum by (service) (rate(http_server_requests_total[5m]))
      - record: sli:http_requests_good:rate5m
        expr: sum by (service) (rate(http_server_requests_total{status!~"5.."}[5m]))

Follow-ups

  • What resolution do you need for a 30-day SLO? — Minutes, not seconds. Burn-rate windows are 5m/1h/6h, so 5-minute downsampled data is sufficient after the first few weeks.
  • What breaks when you downsample histograms? — Nothing if you downsample the bucket counters (they are counters). It breaks if someone downsampled the computed quantile instead — quantiles do not aggregate.
  • Is object storage cheap enough to keep raw? — Storage is, queries are not: the index and the scan cost dominate. Downsampling is as much about query latency as about bytes.

Common wrong answer

"Set retention to 13 months on Prometheus." Local TSDB retention at raw resolution across a real estate means terabytes of head blocks and an unqueryable index; also a single Prometheus is a single point of failure for your history. Interviewers expect the raw/long-term split and recording rules.

principalYou own metrics standards for 400 services. What do you mandate, and how do you enforce it?

Answer

Mandate four things and nothing else. One: names and units come from OpenTelemetry semantic conventions where they exist (http.server.request.duration in seconds, not api_latency_ms) — the current cut is semconv 1.43.0 (2026-07-03), with HTTP stable since 1.23.0 and database since 1.33.0, so most of your surface is already specified. Two: a fixed set of required resource attributes on every emitter — service.name, service.version, deployment.environment.name — because those are the join keys. Three: an attribute allow-list per metric, so nobody adds user_id as a label. Four: every service exposes the RED signals (rate, errors, duration) on its public entry points, because that is what makes cross-service dashboards possible without bespoke work. Enforce it in the pipeline, not in review: the collector is the choke point where you rename, drop, cap cardinality and reject unknown metric names, and a shared instrumentation library or auto-instrumentation makes the compliant path the default one. Publish the per-team bill alongside the standard, because a cardinality rule with a price tag attached gets adopted and one without does not.

Example

# the choke point: one collector config, enforced for everyone
processors:
  transform/semconv:
    metric_statements:
      - set(name, "http.server.request.duration") where name == "api_latency_seconds"
  filter/allowlist:
    metrics:
      metric:
        - 'not (name =~ "^(http|db|rpc|messaging|system|process)\\..*")'
  resourcedetection:
    detectors: [env, system, k8s]
  batch:
    send_batch_size: 8192

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle the team that has 200 legacy metric names? — Rename in the collector, keep both for one retention period, then delete. Never ask 400 teams to do a synchronised code change.
  • What do you not mandate? — Business metrics and internal instrumentation. Standardise the shared surface; leave teams free inside their own service or the standard becomes a tax.
  • How do you keep the standard alive? — Make violations visible as a per-team report with cost attached, and make the compliant SDK/collector the default in the service template.

Common wrong answer

"We write a naming convention document and review PRs." A wiki page plus code review does not survive contact with 400 services and it makes you the bottleneck. What interviewers are listening for is enforcement at a choke point with a migration path that does not require simultaneous change.

03Distributed tracing and context

Tracing is the sub-area where interviewers most often find that a candidate has looked at traces but never made them work. The screener questions are the data model and W3C propagation; the real interview is sampling strategy, broken context across async boundaries, and what you do when the one trace you need was sampled away. At lead level expect "roll this out to 300 services" and "cut the trace bill in half without losing debuggability".

screenerWhat is a trace, a span, and a span context?

Answer

A span is a single timed operation — a name, a start time, a duration, a status, a set of attributes, and optionally events and links. A trace is the tree (strictly, the DAG) of spans that belong to one logical request, tied together by a shared 128-bit trace ID, with each span naming its parent's 64-bit span ID. The span context is the small immutable bundle that travels between processes: trace ID, span ID, trace flags (including the sampled bit) and trace state; it is what turns N independent service traces into one picture. The root span has no parent and usually owns the user-visible latency, which is why "look at the root span duration, then find the child that accounts for it" is the first move in any trace investigation.

Example

trace 4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736
└─ POST /api/checkout            SERVER   2317 ms   checkout
   ├─ GET  /inventory/reserve    CLIENT    120 ms   checkout -> inventory
   │  └─ SELECT items            CLIENT     95 ms   inventory -> postgres
   ├─ POST /payments/authorize   CLIENT   1840 ms   checkout -> payments   <-- the time
   │  └─ acquire db connection   INTERNAL 1790 ms   payments               <-- the cause
   └─ publish order.created      PRODUCER    8 ms   checkout -> kafka

Follow-ups

  • Why is a trace a DAG and not a tree? — Span links. A batch consumer span can link to many producer spans, so a node can have several causal parents.
  • How long can a trace be? — Technically unbounded, practically the backend caps it. Very long-lived traces (a multi-hour workflow) are usually better modelled as separate traces joined by links.
  • What are the ID sizes? — 16 bytes for the trace ID, 8 for the span ID, both random; all-zero is the invalid value.

Common wrong answer

"A span is a log line with a duration." Close enough to sound right, wrong enough to fail the next question: spans carry causal structure (parent, kind, links) which is the entire reason tracing exists. If a span were just a timed log line, you could not tell a serial chain from a parallel fan-out.

screenerHow does trace context propagate between services?

Answer

Over the wire, as headers, using the W3C Trace Context standard: traceparent carries version, trace ID, parent span ID and flags, and tracestate carries vendor-specific key-value data that must survive intermediaries. Inside a process, the SDK keeps the active span in a context object that rides along the call — an AsyncLocal/Activity in .NET, a ThreadLocal+Scope in Java, context.Context passed explicitly in Go, AsyncLocalStorage in Node. The instrumentation for an outbound call reads the active context and injects the headers; the inbound instrumentation extracts them and starts a child span. Everything that goes wrong with tracing in practice is one of these two halves failing: a boundary where nobody injects, or an async hop where the in-process context does not follow.

Example

traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01
             ^   ^                                ^                ^
          version trace-id (16B)              parent-id (8B)   flags: sampled

tracestate: acme=t61rcWkgMzE,congo=t61rcWkgMzE

Follow-ups

  • What was the legacy format? — B3 (Zipkin): X-B3-TraceId, X-B3-SpanId, X-B3-Sampled, single-header or multi-header. You will still meet it; run a composite propagator (tracecontext,b3multi) during migration.
  • Why does tracestate exist? — So a vendor can carry its own sampling decision or account key without breaking the standard fields. It is size-limited and must be preserved by anything in the path.
  • What strips headers in real life? — API gateways, service meshes with header allow-lists, CDNs, and any hand-rolled HTTP client that builds requests from scratch.

Common wrong answer

"The tracing agent handles it automatically, so I don't need to know." True until the trace stops at your message queue or your gateway, which is the exact scenario the interviewer is about to describe. Know the header names — being able to say "I'd curl with a traceparent and see which hop drops it" is the answer that lands.

midHead sampling versus tail sampling — how do you choose?

Answer

Head sampling decides at the root, before the request is processed, and propagates that decision in the sampled flag; it is cheap, stateless and consistent (every service in the trace agrees), but it is blind — it throws away 99% of traces without knowing which ones were interesting, so the error you care about is probably gone. Tail sampling decides after the trace is complete, in a collector that has buffered all its spans, so you can keep 100% of errors, 100% of traces over 2 seconds, and 1% of the boring ones; the price is memory, a buffering window (typically 10-30 s), and the hard requirement that all spans of a trace reach the same collector instance. That last constraint is the whole architecture question: you need a load-balancing exporter that routes by trace ID into a second collector tier. The usual production answer is both — a head sampler at a generous rate to protect the app, then tail sampling for the intelligent selection.

Example

# tier 1: route by trace ID so a trace lands on one tail-sampling collector
exporters:
  loadbalancing:
    routing_key: traceID
    protocol: { otlp: { tls: { insecure: true } } }
    resolver: { dns: { hostname: otel-tailsampler.observability.svc } }

# tier 2: the actual policy
processors:
  tail_sampling:
    decision_wait: 20s
    num_traces: 200000
    policies:
      - name: errors        # keep every failure
        type: status_code
        status_code: { status_codes: [ERROR] }
      - name: slow          # keep every slow trace
        type: latency
        latency: { threshold_ms: 2000 }
      - name: baseline      # and a sample of the rest
        type: probabilistic
        probabilistic: { sampling_percentage: 1 }

Follow-ups

  • What does decision_wait cost you? — Memory proportional to span rate × window, and it must exceed your slowest trace or long traces get judged on partial data.
  • What is consistent probability sampling? — Deriving the decision from the trace ID (a threshold on its low bits) so every independent service reaches the same verdict without coordination, and so counts can be correctly scaled back up.
  • How do you avoid biased statistics from sampling? — Never compute rates from sampled traces. Derive RED metrics from span metrics (unsampled) and use traces for investigation only.

Common wrong answer

"We tail-sample, so we just point every collector at the same policy." Without trace-ID-aware routing, each collector sees a fragment of each trace and makes a decision on partial evidence — you get half-traces, and the errors you meant to keep get dropped because the erroring span went to a different pod. Naming the load-balancing tier is what separates a real answer from a config-file answer.

midSpan kind, attributes, events, links — what goes where?

Answer

Span kind tells the backend the role of the span in the call graph: SERVER and CLIENT pair up across a synchronous call, PRODUCER and CONSUMER pair up across an asynchronous one, and INTERNAL is everything inside a process. Getting kind right is not cosmetic — service maps, RED metrics and latency attribution are all computed from it. Attributes are the dimensions of the operation and should follow semantic conventions (http.request.method, db.system.name, server.address) so cross-service queries work. Events are timestamped things that happened inside the span — a retry, a cache miss, an exception (recorded with exception.type/message/stacktrace) — which is much cheaper than a child span when there is no duration to measure. Links express causality that is not parent-child: a batch consumer span links to the producer span of each message it processed.

Example

using var span = tracer.StartActiveSpan("orders.publish", SpanKind.Producer);
span.SetAttribute("messaging.system", "kafka");
span.SetAttribute("messaging.destination.name", "orders");
span.AddEvent("partition.assigned", new() { ["partition"] = 7 });

// consumer side: one span for the batch, one link per message
var links = messages.Select(m => new Link(ExtractContext(m.Headers))).ToArray();
using var batch = tracer.StartActiveSpan(
    "orders.process", SpanKind.Consumer, links: links);

Follow-ups

  • When is an event better than a child span? — When the thing has no meaningful duration or would multiply span count: retries, cache lookups, validation failures. Spans are not free — each one is a row.
  • Why record exceptions as events rather than attributes? — Multiple exceptions can occur in one span, and events keep their timestamps. Setting the span status to ERROR is separate and is what drives error-based sampling.
  • What is the attribute limit? — SDK defaults cap attribute count (128) and value length; exceeding it silently truncates, which is a classic "why is my payload missing" bug.

Common wrong answer

"I'd make a child span for every function so we can see everything." That is how you get 4,000-span traces that no UI can render and a tracing bill nobody will pay. Span granularity is a design decision — one span per network hop, per meaningful unit of work, and per thing you would want to see a duration for.

midA trace stops halfway through. Walk me through diagnosing broken context propagation.

Answer

Establish where the break is first: find the last span that exists and the first service that has none. Then it is one of four causes. The hop does not inject — a hand-built HTTP client, a raw socket, or a library with no instrumentation. The hop does not extract — the receiving framework is not instrumented, or it is a queue consumer where nobody wrote the extraction. Something strips the header — a gateway, mesh or CDN with an allow-list that does not include traceparent. Or the in-process context is lost across an async boundary: a fire-and-forget task, a manual thread, a custom thread pool, a Node callback outside AsyncLocalStorage, a Go goroutine that did not receive the context.Context. The diagnostic is cheap: send a request with a known traceparent and log the incoming headers at each hop, or read the raw headers at the receiving service. That test tells you in one minute whether it is a wire problem or an in-process problem.

Example

# is the context reaching the next service at all?
curl -s -D- -o/dev/null https://api.internal/checkout \
  -H 'traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01'

# in Go, the classic in-process break: ctx not passed to the goroutine
go func() { doWork(context.Background()) }()   // trace ends here
go func(ctx context.Context) { doWork(ctx) }(ctx)  // trace continues

Follow-ups

  • What is special about queues? — There is no ambient context on the consumer side; the producer must serialise traceparent into message headers and the consumer must deserialise it. Most brokers do not do this for you.
  • How do you catch this in CI? — A contract test that fires a request with a known trace ID through the stack and asserts the span count and depth in the backend.
  • Two traces where you expected one — what happened? — Extraction succeeded but the sampler or the propagator was misconfigured, or something re-rooted the trace (a gateway that starts a fresh trace instead of continuing).

Common wrong answer

"The SDK must be broken; I'd upgrade it." Almost never the cause. In production the answer is nearly always a missing injection at one boundary or a lost async context — and the interviewer wants to hear you isolate wire versus in-process before touching anything.

seniorHow do you get RED metrics that are correct when traces are sampled at 1%?

Answer

You do not compute them from stored traces — you generate metrics from the unsampled span stream before sampling drops anything. In OpenTelemetry that is the collector's span-metrics connector, which turns every span into request-count and duration-histogram series keyed by service, span name, kind and status; it sits before the tail sampler in the pipeline, so it sees 100% of spans while only 1% of traces are stored. The alternative — and the belt-and-braces answer — is that services also emit their own HTTP/RPC metrics via the standard instrumentation, which is unsampled by construction. Either way the rule is: metrics from the full stream, traces as a sampled exhibit. If you must estimate counts from sampled traces (a vendor that only stores traces), you need consistent probability sampling with the sampling probability recorded on the span so you can scale each kept trace back up by its inverse probability — and you must say out loud that the tail of the distribution is then estimated, not measured.

Example

connectors:
  spanmetrics:
    histogram: { explicit: { buckets: [10ms, 50ms, 100ms, 500ms, 1s, 2s, 5s] } }
    dimensions: [{ name: http.route }, { name: deployment.environment.name }]

service:
  pipelines:
    traces:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [batch]
      exporters: [spanmetrics, loadbalancing]   # metrics first: 100% of spans
    metrics/spanmetrics:
      receivers: [spanmetrics]
      exporters: [prometheusremotewrite]

Follow-ups

  • What is the cardinality risk here? — Every dimension you add multiplies series. http.route is fine; http.url or a customer ID is a cardinality bomb generated automatically at full traffic.
  • Where must the connector sit? — Before any sampling processor. Put it after the tail sampler and your request counts drop by 99%, which is a genuinely common misconfiguration.
  • Does this replace application metrics? — Mostly for RED at boundaries; it cannot see business metrics or anything not represented as a span.

Common wrong answer

"We multiply the trace counts by 100 because we sample at 1%." That works only with consistent probability sampling and a recorded sample rate, and it is wildly wrong under tail sampling — where errors are kept at 100% and normal traces at 1%, so naive scaling reports a 50% error rate. This exact mistake has produced real incident reports about phantom outages.

seniorAuto-instrumentation or manual? How much of each?

Answer

Start with auto-instrumentation for 100% of services, because it gets you the call graph, the HTTP/gRPC/DB spans and the resource attributes with no code change — a Java agent, the .NET auto-instrumentation, the Node loader hooks, or the Kubernetes operator injecting them all as a sidecar/init container. Auto gets you the boundaries; it cannot know your domain. Manual instrumentation is then targeted at three things: business-meaningful units of work (a span called order.fulfil, not HTTP POST), attributes that make the slice possible (tenant.id, payment.provider, experiment.variant), and the internal boundaries where you actually lose time (a lock, a queue wait, a batch). The rule I give teams is "auto for structure, manual for meaning, and one attribute per question you have had to answer in an incident". eBPF-based zero-code instrumentation is the newer third option: it gets you network-level spans with no language support at all, at the cost of no application semantics.

Example

# Kubernetes: the operator injects the agent, no code change
apiVersion: opentelemetry.io/v1alpha1
kind: Instrumentation
metadata: { name: default }
spec:
  exporter: { endpoint: http://otel-collector.observability:4317 }
  propagators: [tracecontext, baggage]
  sampler: { type: parentbased_traceidratio, argument: "0.1" }
---
# pod annotation that opts a workload in
# instrumentation.opentelemetry.io/inject-dotnet: "true"

Follow-ups

  • What is the overhead of auto-instrumentation? — Typically low single-digit percent CPU and some startup cost; the real risk is span volume, not CPU. Measure it on a canary rather than quoting a number.
  • What does auto-instrumentation always miss? — Domain semantics and anything behind a custom transport. It also over-instruments: noisy internal spans you will want to filter.
  • How do you stop manual instrumentation rotting? — Treat span names and attributes as an API: reviewed, named from semantic conventions, and covered by the same contract test that checks propagation.

Common wrong answer

"We instrument everything manually so it's exactly what we want." At any real scale that is thousands of engineer-hours and inconsistent naming across teams, and coverage will never reach 100%. The credible answer is auto as the floor, manual as the differentiator.

seniorHow do you trace through a message queue without producing one giant trace?

Answer

Inject the trace context into message headers at publish time and extract it at consume time — that part is mandatory or the trace simply ends at the broker. The design question is whether the consumer span is a child of the producer span or a new trace linked to it. Child-of keeps one trace and is right when the work is genuinely part of the same user request and completes in seconds. A linked new trace is right when the consumer is a batch (one span consuming 500 messages cannot have 500 parents), when processing is delayed by minutes or hours (the parent trace is long closed and the backend has garbage-collected it), or when a retry loop would otherwise attach thousands of spans to one ancient root. The failure mode to name is the unbounded trace: a fan-out to a queue where every downstream job is a child produces traces with tens of thousands of spans that break both the UI and the tail sampler's memory.

Example

// producer: serialise context into the message
var props = new Dictionary<string, string>();
Propagators.DefaultTextMapPropagator.Inject(
    new PropagationContext(Activity.Current.Context, Baggage.Current),
    props, (d, k, v) => d[k] = v);

// consumer: batch span links back, does not become a child
var parent = Propagators.DefaultTextMapPropagator.Extract(
    default, msg.Headers, (h, k) => new[] { h[k] });
using var span = tracer.StartActiveSpan(
    "orders.process", SpanKind.Consumer,
    links: new[] { new Link(parent.ActivityContext) });

Follow-ups

  • How do you measure queue latency? — Record the enqueue timestamp on the message and emit the wait time as an attribute or a metric on the consumer span; the span duration alone hides it.
  • What about fan-out to 10,000 consumers? — Links, always. And consider sampling the fan-out separately from the root request.
  • Does the sampled flag propagate through the queue? — Yes, in traceparent flags — which means an unsampled request produces unsampled downstream work, so a background job that only runs for unsampled requests will look invisible.

Common wrong answer

"Everything downstream is a child span so we can see the whole flow." It reads as thorough and it is the trap: for batch consumers and delayed processing it produces unbounded traces, breaks tail sampling windows, and attributes hours of latency to a request that returned in 40 ms. Say "child for synchronous continuation, link for batch or delayed".

seniorThe trace you need was sampled away. What now, and how do you stop it happening again?

Answer

In the moment: fall back to what is unsampled — span-derived RED metrics for the shape, logs for the specific request (logs are usually kept at 100%), and exemplars, which may still point at a kept trace in the same bucket. Then reproduce with forced sampling: most SDKs and gateways honour a caller-supplied sampled flag or a debug header, so you can replay the failing request with sampling forced on and get a full trace immediately. Structurally, the fix is policy, not rate: keep 100% of errors and 100% of slow traces via tail sampling, keep a per-tenant or per-route floor so low-traffic-but-important paths are never entirely dark, and add a local "always sample" rule for spans that exceed a latency threshold. Finally, ensure the sampling decision itself is observable — record the policy that kept or dropped a trace, because "why is this missing" is otherwise unanswerable.

Example

# force the sampled flag on for a single reproduction
curl https://api.internal/checkout \
  -H 'traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01'
#                                                                        ^^ sampled=1

Follow-ups

  • Isn't a debug header a DoS risk? — Yes: gate it behind authentication or an internal-only path, and rate-limit it, or a caller can force 100% sampling of your whole fleet.
  • What is a per-tenant sampling floor? — A policy that guarantees a minimum number of traces per tenant or route per minute, so your biggest customer does not drown out the one with the outage.
  • How do you know your sampling is losing something important? — Compare span-metric error counts against traces kept for errors; a systematic gap means a policy is dropping failures.

Common wrong answer

"Turn sampling up to 100% during incidents." Attractive and usually unavailable: the change takes a deploy or a config rollout, the incident is over before it lands, and at full rate you may take down the ingestion pipeline while already in an outage. Interviewers want policy-based retention of interesting traces, decided in advance.

principalYou are asked to roll out tracing across 300 services. What is your plan?

Answer

Sequence it so value arrives before the cost does. Phase one: pick one revenue-critical request path that crosses five to eight services, instrument it end-to-end, and use it to settle propagation, the collector topology and the sampling policy — one working path is worth more politically than 300 half-instrumented ones. Phase two: auto-instrumentation everywhere via the platform (operator injection, base images, service templates) with a conservative head sample rate, so coverage becomes the default rather than a project. Phase three: enforcement and semantics — required resource attributes at the collector, span-metrics for RED, tail sampling with error/latency policies, and a contract test in CI that proves context survives each boundary type your estate uses. Throughout, publish one number: median time from "alert fires" to "we know which service is responsible". That is the metric that funds phase three. The parts that will actually hurt are the ones to name unprompted: message queues and legacy transports where nothing propagates, teams with no capacity, and the cost curve — which is why span-metrics plus tail sampling must land before broad enablement, not after.

"Mandate OpenTelemetry SDK adoption in every service by end of quarter." 04Logs and structured logging

Logs are the signal every candidate has used and almost nobody has designed. Interviewers use this unit to test cost sense and discipline: anyone can say "use structured logging", far fewer can say what a log line costs at 50k rps, which fields must never appear, and when a log should have been a span attribute instead. At lead level the questions turn into retention policy, audit-versus-debug separation, and how you cut the log bill by 60% without losing the ability to debug.

screenerWhat is structured logging and why does it matter?

Answer

Structured logging emits each record as a set of typed key-value fields — usually JSON or a binary equivalent — instead of an interpolated sentence. It matters because it makes logs queryable without regex: you can filter status_code=502 AND tenant="acme", aggregate by a field, and get correct results even when a value contains a space or a quote. It also makes the message template a stable identity: "checkout failed for {CartId}" is one event type with a varying parameter, so you can count occurrences of that event rather than of a string that changes every time. And it is what makes ingestion-side processing possible — redaction, sampling, routing and enrichment all need fields, not prose.

Example

// legacy: a sentence. Parseable only by regex, and the regex breaks on the first comma in a name.
logger.LogError("Checkout failed for cart " + cartId + " user " + userName);

// modern: message template + fields. The template is the event identity.
logger.LogError("Checkout failed for {CartId}", cartId);
// with a scope carrying the dimensions
using (logger.BeginScope(new Dictionary<string, object>
{
    ["tenant.id"] = tenant,
    ["payment.provider"] = provider,
})) { /* ... */ }

Follow-ups

  • JSON or something denser? — JSON for interoperability; at very high volume the wire format usually becomes OTLP or a columnar format, because JSON parsing dominates ingest cost.
  • What is the one field people forget? — The trace ID. Without it, structured logs are still islands.
  • Does structured logging cost more? — More bytes per record, far less CPU and human time at query. The net is strongly positive above trivial volume.

Common wrong answer

"We already log in a consistent format, so grep works fine." Grep works fine on one host. It does not aggregate, it does not survive multi-line stack traces, and the moment a field contains a delimiter your parser produces confidently wrong answers. Interviewers ask this to hear whether you have ever had to answer a question across a fleet.

screenerHow do you decide the log level for a given event?

Answer

Level is a statement about who needs to act, not about how interesting the developer found it. ERROR means this request failed and a human may need to do something — it should be rare enough that a spike is meaningful. WARN means something degraded but was handled: a retry succeeded, a fallback was used; if nobody would ever act on it, it is INFO. INFO is the record of normal significant transitions — service started, config loaded, a job completed — and should be low, constant volume, not per-request chatter. DEBUG is developer detail, off in production but switchable, and TRACE is firehose. The two rules that matter in review: a caught-and-handled exception is not an ERROR, and if you log an error and rethrow it, the same failure gets logged three times up the stack and your error counts are meaningless.

Example

try { await payments.AuthorizeAsync(order); }
catch (TransientException ex)
{
    // handled, will retry -> WARN, not ERROR
    logger.LogWarning(ex, "Payment authorize transient failure, retrying {Attempt}", attempt);
    throw;   // and DO NOT log it again in every catch block on the way up
}

Follow-ups

  • Should you alert on ERROR log volume? — As a coarse safety net, maybe; as a primary alert, no. Alert on user-visible symptoms (SLO burn), or you page on a library that decided something is an error.
  • How do you change level without a deploy? — Dynamic log level via config reload or a control endpoint, ideally scoped per logger and time-boxed so it reverts automatically.
  • What about per-request debug? — Level overrides driven by a header or trace flag, so you can turn on DEBUG for one tenant or one trace instead of the whole fleet.

Common wrong answer

"Log everything at INFO in production so we have it if we need it." That is how a log bill triples and how ERROR becomes noise. The signal is the level discipline; if everything is INFO, the level field carries no information and every query has to fall back to text search.

midHow do you correlate logs with traces, and what is the OpenTelemetry logs model?

Answer

Correlation is one rule: every log record emitted inside an active span carries that span's trace_id and span_id. Then a log query becomes "everything that happened inside this trace", and a trace UI can show the logs for a span inline. OpenTelemetry's logs design is deliberately different from traces and metrics: rather than asking everyone to rewrite logging calls, it defines a log data model and a bridge (appender) that plugs into the logging library you already use — Serilog/ILogger, Logback, log4j, Winston — and attaches trace context and resource attributes automatically. A log record has a timestamp, severity number and text, body, attributes, resource, and the trace context fields. The pragmatic legacy path, which you will still meet everywhere, is to inject the trace ID into the logging library's context (MDC in Java, ILogger scopes in .NET) and ship via a file agent — that works and is often the right first step.

Example

{
  "timestamp": "2026-09-08T14:32:07.412Z",
  "severity_text": "ERROR", "severity_number": 17,
  "body": "Checkout failed for {CartId}",
  "attributes": { "CartId": "c_7781", "tenant.id": "acme" },
  "trace_id": "4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736",
  "span_id": "00f067aa0ba902b7",
  "resource": { "service.name": "checkout", "service.version": "2026.9.1" }
}

Follow-ups

  • Why did OTel bridge instead of replacing logging APIs? — Because logging is the most entrenched API in every codebase; a bridge gets adoption without a rewrite. Traces and metrics had no incumbent of the same size.
  • What is the severity number for? — A normalised 1-24 scale so WARN, WARNING and W from three ecosystems compare correctly.
  • Do you still need a file agent? — Often yes: writing to stdout and letting a collector filelog receiver tail it survives an application crash better than in-process export.

Common wrong answer

"We put the request ID in the logs, which is the same thing." A per-service request ID does not cross service boundaries and does not link to the trace, so you still cannot follow one user's request through the system. The trace ID is the identifier the rest of your telemetry already uses — use it.

midWhat must never appear in a log line, and how do you enforce it?

Answer

Never: credentials and secrets (passwords, tokens, API keys, session cookies, connection strings), payment data (PAN, CVV), and raw personal data — full names, email addresses, national identifiers, precise location, health data — beyond what you have a lawful basis to keep. Also never log entire request or response bodies "for debugging", because that is how every one of the above ends up in a log store with a two-year retention and no access control. Enforcement has to be layered, because a code-review rule will not hold: a redacting formatter in the logging library (destructuring policies, field allow-lists), a redaction processor in the collector as a second net, and a scanner that samples the log store for patterns (card numbers, JWTs, email addresses) and raises a ticket. The design principle is to log identifiers, not values: enduser.pseudo.id rather than an email, an order ID rather than an address.

Example

# collector-side net: mask anything that looks like a token or a card number
processors:
  transform/redact:
    log_statements:
      - replace_pattern(body, "eyJ[A-Za-z0-9_-]{10,}\\.[A-Za-z0-9_-]+\\.[A-Za-z0-9_-]+", "[JWT]")
      - replace_pattern(body, "\\b(?:\\d[ -]*?){13,16}\\b", "[PAN]")
  attributes/strip:
    actions:
      - key: http.request.header.authorization
        action: delete

Follow-ups

  • What if a secret is already in the log store? — Treat it as a credential-exposure incident: rotate the secret first (deletion is slow and often incomplete), then purge, then fix the emitter.
  • How do you handle the "we need the payload to debug" argument? — Log a hash or a redacted shape, keep full payloads only in a short-retention, access-controlled store, and make the exception explicit and time-boxed.
  • What about pseudonymised IDs? — Better, but still personal data under GDPR if you can re-identify. Retention and access rules still apply.

Common wrong answer

"We tell developers not to log PII and review it in PRs." Every large log store that has ever been audited contained secrets despite exactly that rule. The answer interviewers want is defence in depth — library redaction, pipeline redaction, and detection — because the failure is statistical, not individual.

midCompare index-everything log stores with label-based ones. What changes for you?

Answer

An inverted-index store (Elasticsearch/OpenSearch, most hosted log products) indexes every field at ingest: queries on arbitrary text are fast, but ingest is CPU-heavy and storage is often larger than the raw logs, so cost scales with what you write. A label-based store (Loki and its descendants) indexes only a small set of labels and keeps the log body as compressed chunks; ingest is cheap and storage is small, but a query that is not selective on labels has to brute-force scan chunks, so cost moves to what you read. What changes for you is query discipline: on a label store you must always narrow by service, namespace and time first, and you must keep label cardinality low — putting a request ID in a Loki label is the same mistake as putting it in a Prometheus label. Choose index-everything when arbitrary forensic search across long retention is the core use case; choose label-based when volume is huge and queries are almost always scoped to a known service and time window.

Example

# label store: narrow by labels FIRST, then filter the body
{service_name="checkout", deployment_environment="prod"} |= "payment_declined" | json | tenant_id="acme"

# index store: the same query is a single search, at higher ingest cost
service.name:checkout AND message:"payment_declined" AND tenant.id:acme

Follow-ups

  • What is the cost driver in each? — Index store: ingest volume and index size. Label store: bytes scanned per query and label cardinality.
  • Which is better for long retention? — Label-based, usually: object storage plus tiny index is cheap to keep for a year; a full text index for a year is rarely affordable.
  • How do you keep a label store usable? — Few labels (service, namespace, level, environment), everything else parsed at query time from structured bodies.

Common wrong answer

"Loki is just cheaper Elasticsearch." They make opposite trade-offs, and treating them as substitutes leads to the classic migration failure: teams add high-cardinality labels to recreate their old query patterns, blow up the index, and conclude the tool is broken.

seniorWhen should a log line have been a span attribute or a metric instead?

Answer

Three tests. If you only ever count the log line — "how many payment declines per minute" — it should be a metric or a span attribute you aggregate; counting log lines is the most expensive way to maintain a counter, and it breaks the moment someone rewords the message. If the line describes something about the current unit of work — which provider, which variant, how many items — it belongs as an attribute on the span, where it is already correlated and costs nothing extra to carry. If it is a timestamped moment inside an operation with no duration of its own, it is a span event. What genuinely belongs in a log is the detail you need once, for one failing request: the exception with its stack trace, the specific validation error, the exact state that made the decision. In practice, moving per-request INFO chatter onto spans is the single largest log-cost reduction available to most teams, and it improves debugging because the data arrives already joined to the trace.

  • Why not put the stack trace on the span? — You can (as an exception event), but attribute-length limits truncate it and trace sampling may drop it. Logs are kept at 100%, so the durable copy belongs there.
  • Does this mean fewer logs is always better? — Fewer per-request logs. Keep the ones that carry irreplaceable detail; delete the ones that narrate control flow.
  • How do you find the chatter? — Top-N by log-template volume. The top ten templates are usually 80% of the bill and almost always removable.

Common wrong answer

"Logs are cheap, keep them all — storage is basically free." Storage is cheap; ingest, indexing and query are not, and at 50k rps a single extra INFO line per request is billions of records a month. This answer signals someone who has never seen the observability invoice.

seniorYour log bill doubled in a month. How do you cut it 60% without losing debuggability?

Answer

Measure before cutting: get volume by service, by log template and by level, because the distribution is always extremely skewed and usually two or three templates dominate. Then apply, in order of cost-to-value: delete the top offending templates that narrate control flow (they are almost never read); move per-request dimensions onto spans; drop DEBUG in production and replace it with per-trace or per-tenant dynamic level; sample repetitive successful-path logs — keep 1% of "request completed" lines but 100% of errors, ideally with a tail-based rule so you keep all logs for traces that failed; and tier retention — 7 days hot and searchable, 90 days in object storage, with only audit logs kept longer. Enforce the result at the collector so it does not regress, and give each team a visible bill. What you must not do is a flat percentage sample across all logs, because that keeps 40% of the noise and loses 60% of the errors.

Example

processors:
  # keep every error; keep 1 in 100 of the routine completion lines
  filter/drop_debug:
    logs:
      log_record:
        - 'severity_number < SEVERITY_NUMBER_INFO'
  probabilistic_sampler/routine:
    sampling_percentage: 1
service:
  pipelines:
    logs/errors:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [batch]
      exporters: [loki]
    logs/routine:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [filter/drop_debug, probabilistic_sampler/routine, batch]
      exporters: [loki]

Follow-ups

  • How do you sample logs without breaking a trace's log set? — Sample by trace ID, not per record, so a kept trace keeps all of its logs and a dropped one drops all of them.
  • What do you protect absolutely? — Errors, audit records, security events, and anything with a legal retention requirement. Put them in a separate pipeline so a cost change cannot touch them.
  • How do you keep it from regressing? — A per-service volume budget with an alert, and a report of the top templates by cost sent to the owning team monthly.

Common wrong answer

"Turn on 10% sampling across the board." It is the fastest way to hit the number and the fastest way to be unable to debug: you now have one in ten log lines of every failed request, so no failure has a complete story. Sampling must be structured — by severity, by template, by trace.

principalHow do you separate audit logging from operational logging, and why does it matter?

Answer

They are different systems with different guarantees that happen to share the word "log". Audit records answer "who did what to which resource, when, and was it allowed" — they need integrity (append-only, tamper-evident), completeness (a dropped audit record is a compliance failure, so no sampling and no lossy buffering), defined long retention measured in years, and tight access control, because the audit trail itself is sensitive. Operational logs are best-effort diagnostics: sampled, short-retention, widely readable, and it is fine to drop them under load. Mixing them is a double failure — you either apply audit-grade retention and access to terabytes of debug chatter, which is unaffordable and makes the debug logs unusable, or you apply operational cost controls to audit records and silently break your compliance posture the first time the pipeline sheds load. So: separate emission path, separate store, separate retention, separate access, and an explicit owner. In an interview, name the shedding scenario — it is the concrete failure that makes the point.

  • Where do security events sit? — Closer to audit: they feed detection, need long retention and integrity, and usually go to a SIEM rather than the observability store.
  • Can OTLP carry audit records? — Technically yes, but the delivery semantics of a telemetry pipeline (batching, drop-on-overload) are wrong for them. Prefer a transactional path.
  • What is the give-away that a team has mixed them? — A retention rule of "keep everything for seven years" applied to application logs, and a bill to match.

"It's all logs, we just tag audit records and keep everything for seven years." That is the expensive half of the mistake and it still fails an audit, because a telemetry pipeline that drops under load cannot promise completeness. Separate the paths, then argue about tooling.

05SLIs, SLOs and alerting

This is the unit that decides senior and lead loops, because it is where observability stops being tooling and becomes a decision framework. Screener level is the SLI/SLO/SLA vocabulary; mid level is error budgets and burn-rate alerts; senior level is picking targets and windows that survive a real traffic profile, and writing SLOs for systems that are not request/response. At principal level the question is organisational: how do you get 80 teams to own SLOs without producing 80 vanity dashboards?

screenerSLI, SLO, SLA — define them and give an example of each.

Answer

An SLI is a measurement of service behaviour from the user's point of view, almost always expressed as a ratio of good events to valid events — for example, the proportion of checkout requests that return a non-5xx response in under 500 ms. An SLO is a target for that indicator over a window: 99.9% of those requests, measured over a rolling 30 days. An SLA is a contract with a customer that includes consequences — service credits, penalties — and it should always be looser than the internal SLO, so that you breach your own target and react well before you breach the contract. The chain matters in that order: you cannot set a target for something you do not measure, and you should never sign a contract term you cannot measure and beat with margin.

Example

SLI  proportion of valid checkout requests served <500 ms with a non-5xx status
SLO  99.9% over a rolling 30 days   -> error budget = 43m 12s of bad events
SLA  99.5% per calendar month, 10% service credit below that (looser, on purpose)

Follow-ups

  • What does "valid events" exclude? — Requests that are not your responsibility: client 4xx from malformed input, synthetic probes, health checks. Defining the denominator is where most SLO arguments actually happen.
  • How much is 99.9%? — About 43 minutes a month, 8.7 hours a year. 99.99% is 4.3 minutes a month, which is less than most deploy rollbacks take.
  • Why should the SLO be tighter than the SLA? — So the error budget runs out while you still have room to act. If they are equal, the first breach is a contractual one.

Common wrong answer

"The SLO is our uptime target — 99.99% because we want to be reliable." Two errors in one sentence: uptime measured by a ping is not a user-centric SLI, and a target picked for ambition rather than from user needs and cost commits the team to a budget of four minutes a month that no deploy process can honour. Targets come from what users need and what you can afford, not from how many nines sound impressive.

screenerWhat makes a good SLI? Where do RED, USE and the four golden signals fit?

Answer

A good SLI is user-visible, measurable at the point closest to the user, expressed as good-over-valid, and it moves when users suffer and stays flat when they do not. CPU utilisation fails every part of that; "proportion of search requests returning results in under 300 ms" passes. The frameworks are shortcuts for picking them: the four golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) are the general checklist; RED (rate, errors, duration) is the request-driven service version and maps directly onto SLIs; USE (utilisation, saturation, errors) is for resources — CPU, disk, connection pools — and is a debugging lens rather than an SLI source. The practical rule is RED for the SLO, USE for the investigation: you alert on the symptom the user feels and you diagnose with the resource metrics.

Follow-ups

  • Where should the SLI be measured? — As close to the user as you can trust: load balancer or gateway beats application-internal, because it captures failures the application never saw. RUM is closer still but noisier.
  • How many SLIs per service? — Two to four. Availability and latency on the main journey, plus freshness or correctness where relevant. More than that and nobody can say whether the service is healthy.
  • Is saturation ever an SLI? — Rarely; it is a leading indicator. Use it for capacity alerts, not for the user-facing objective.

Common wrong answer

"We use the four golden signals as our SLOs, so we have an SLO on saturation." Saturation is a cause, not an experience: a service can run at 95% CPU and serve every request perfectly. Turning it into an objective produces pages nobody can act on and trains the team to ignore the SLO channel.

midWhat is an error budget and what is it actually for?

Answer

The error budget is the complement of the SLO: at 99.9% over 30 days you are permitted 0.1% bad events, which is roughly 43 minutes of full outage or a much longer period of partial degradation. Its purpose is not accounting, it is decision-making: it converts reliability from an argument between engineering and product into a number both sides can spend. Budget remaining means you can ship faster, take risks, run experiments; budget exhausted means the next unit of work is reliability, not features. The second use is triage — a burn rate tells you how urgent an issue is, because a problem consuming a month of budget in an hour is a page while one that would take three weeks to exhaust it is a ticket. The requirement that makes it real is a pre-agreed policy about what happens at zero, agreed before the budget runs out rather than negotiated during the outage.

Example

# budget consumed over the 30d window (0 = fresh, 1 = exhausted)
(
  1 - (
    sum(rate(sli_good_total[30d])) / sum(rate(sli_valid_total[30d]))
  )
) / (1 - 0.999)

Follow-ups

  • Rolling or calendar window? — Rolling 28-30 days for engineering decisions (no artificial reset on the 1st); calendar months only where a contract demands it.
  • What about planned maintenance? — It either counts against the budget or it is excluded by an explicit, documented rule. Silently excluding it is how SLOs lose credibility.
  • Who owns the budget? — The team that owns the service, with the policy agreed with their product counterpart. A budget owned by a central SRE team is a report, not a lever.

Common wrong answer

"The error budget is how much downtime we're allowed before we get in trouble." Framed as a punishment, it becomes something to hide — teams start arguing that failures were "not real users" or excluding traffic until the number looks fine. Its value is as a shared currency for deciding what to work on next.

midExplain multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting.

Answer

Burn rate is how fast you are consuming the error budget relative to the rate that would exactly exhaust it over the window: burn rate 1 means you finish the 30-day budget in exactly 30 days; burn rate 14.4 means you finish it in about 2 days. You alert on burn rate rather than on raw error rate because it automatically scales with the SLO and tells you urgency. Multi-window means each alert pairs a long window with a short one: the long window (say 1 hour at 14.4×) gives precision — a genuine sustained problem — and the short window (5 minutes at the same rate) acts as a fast reset so the alert clears quickly when the problem stops, instead of hanging around for an hour. Multi-burn-rate means you run two or three of these in parallel: a fast, high-rate one that pages (2% of budget in an hour), and a slow, low-rate one that opens a ticket (10% of budget in three days). The result is few pages, all of them worth waking someone for.

Example

groups:
- name: slo-checkout
  rules:
  - alert: CheckoutFastBurn        # 2% of a 30d budget in 1h  -> page
    expr: |
      (slo:error_ratio:rate1h  > (14.4 * 0.001))
      and
      (slo:error_ratio:rate5m  > (14.4 * 0.001))
    labels: { severity: page }
  - alert: CheckoutSlowBurn        # 10% of budget in 3d       -> ticket
    expr: |
      (slo:error_ratio:rate6h  > (1 * 0.001))
      and
      (slo:error_ratio:rate30m > (1 * 0.001))
    labels: { severity: ticket }

Follow-ups

  • Why not just alert when the error rate exceeds 1%? — Because 1% for thirty seconds is noise and 0.2% for three days is an outage. A static threshold cannot tell those apart; burn rate can.
  • What are the usual burn-rate pairs? — 14.4× over 1h/5m for the page and 6× over 6h/30m or 1× over 3d/6h for the ticket; they come from the Google SRE workbook and are a fine default.
  • What breaks at low traffic? — Small denominators: three requests, one failure, 33% error rate. Add a minimum-traffic guard or fall back to a raw-count condition.

Common wrong answer

"We alert when the SLO is breached." By then the budget is gone and the incident is over — an SLO breach is a retrospective fact, not a signal. The whole point of burn-rate alerting is to fire while there is still budget left to protect.

midSymptom-based versus cause-based alerting — and how do you kill alert fatigue?

Answer

Symptom-based alerts fire on what the user experiences: elevated error ratio, latency past the SLO threshold, orders not being processed. Cause-based alerts fire on internal conditions that might cause that: high CPU, disk 80% full, a pod restarting, a queue growing. You page on symptoms, because they are few, always meaningful, and independent of which cause produced them; you keep cause-based signals as dashboards, tickets and enrichment on the symptom page. Alert fatigue is then treated as a defect with a measurable rate: track pages per shift and the proportion actioned, review every non-actioned page in a weekly triage, and delete or downgrade it — deleting alerts is the work, and it needs explicit permission from leadership or nobody will do it. Add three mechanics: every page carries a runbook link and the current burn rate, alerts are grouped and inhibited (a cluster-down alert suppresses the fifty service alerts under it), and anything that fires more than twice without action is auto-demoted.

Example

# Alertmanager: one page for a cluster outage, not fifty
inhibit_rules:
  - source_matchers: [ severity="page", alertname="ClusterUnreachable" ]
    target_matchers: [ severity="page" ]
    equal: [ cluster ]
route:
  group_by: [ cluster, service, alertname ]
  group_wait: 30s
  group_interval: 5m
  repeat_interval: 4h

Follow-ups

  • Are cause-based alerts ever pages? — Yes, when the cause is certain to become a symptom and the lead time is short: a certificate expiring in 24 hours, a disk that will fill in two hours, a leaked credential.
  • What is a healthy page volume? — Commonly cited: no more than about two pages per 12-hour shift, and every one actionable. Above that, on-call quality and retention both degrade.
  • How do you handle flapping? — for: durations, the short-window reset in burn-rate alerts, and hysteresis. Never solve it by raising the threshold until it stops firing.

Common wrong answer

"We alert on everything and let on-call filter it." That is the definition of alert fatigue: humans triage the first fifty pages carefully and the fifty-first — the real one — reflexively. Interviewers want to hear that deleting alerts is a deliberate, funded activity.

seniorHow do you actually choose an SLO target and window?

Answer

Work backwards from users and cost, and start from measured reality. First, look at the past 90 days of the SLI: if you have been running at 99.95%, an SLO of 99.99% commits you to work nobody has funded, and one of 99.5% gives away reliability you already deliver. Second, ask what users actually notice — for many internal or batch systems the honest answer is that 99.5% is invisible, while for a payment path a single minute matters. Third, price the next nine: each one typically costs an order of magnitude more in redundancy, review and release friction, and much of it is spent on dependencies you do not control, so your SLO can never meaningfully exceed the composition of your critical dependencies' SLOs. For the window: 28-30 days rolling is the default because it is long enough to smooth a single bad deploy and short enough to react to; shorter windows make the budget jumpy, longer ones let a bad month hide. And write down the exclusions before the first incident, not after.

Example

measured last 90d : 99.94%   (checkout, good/valid at the gateway)
user tolerance    : failures noticed above ~0.1% (support ticket correlation)
dependency floor  : payments provider 99.9% -> our ceiling is ~99.9% for that path
=> SLO 99.9% / 30d rolling, budget 43m, exclusions: 4xx from malformed input,
   synthetic probes, and traffic from the load-test tenant

Follow-ups

  • What if the dependency has no SLO? — Measure it yourself and treat the observed availability as the ceiling; state the assumption in the SLO document.
  • Different SLOs per tier of customer? — Reasonable and common (per-tenant SLIs), but it multiplies the alerting surface; do it only where the contract or the business genuinely differs.
  • When would you lower an SLO? — When it has been breached for months and nobody acted: an unachievable target is worse than an honest one, because it teaches the team that the number is fiction.

Common wrong answer

"Four nines, because that's what a serious service does." It is the answer that gets probed hardest and collapses fastest: 99.99% is 4.3 minutes a month, which is shorter than most incident detection times, and it is arithmetically impossible if any dependency in the path offers 99.9%. Composition of dependencies is the fact to name unprompted.

seniorHow do you write SLOs for a system that is not request/response?

Answer

Pick the property the consumer actually depends on and express it as good-over-valid anyway. For a streaming or batch pipeline the usual three are freshness (proportion of time the output is no more than N minutes behind the source), completeness (proportion of input records that appear in the output) and correctness (proportion of outputs that pass validation). For a queue-driven worker it is often end-to-end latency from enqueue to acknowledgement, plus a dead-letter ratio. For storage it is durability (a separate, much stricter class) plus read and write latency and success ratios. For an ML or ranking system you add a quality SLI — proportion of responses served with a model version no older than X, or with a fallback rather than the real model. The common mistakes to avoid are alerting on job exit codes (a job can succeed while emitting nothing) and on average lag (a single stuck partition disappears into an average — use the maximum lag across partitions).

Example

# freshness SLI: fraction of minutes where the worst partition is under 5 minutes behind
avg_over_time(
  (max(kafka_consumergroup_lag_seconds) by (group) < bool 300)[30d:1m]
)

Follow-ups

  • Why maximum lag, not average? — One stuck partition is a real outage for the customers whose keys hash to it; the average across 200 partitions hides it completely.
  • How do you measure completeness cheaply? — Counts at the boundary: records in versus records out per window, with a tolerance. Full reconciliation is a separate, slower job.
  • What is the SLI for a nightly report? — Delivered before the deadline. Runtime is diagnostic; the user cares only about the deadline.

Common wrong answer

"The job either runs or it doesn't, so the SLI is job success rate." Pipelines fail silently far more often than they crash: they emit zero rows, they process stale input, they skip a partition. Exit code zero and a completely wrong output is the normal failure mode, which is why freshness and completeness exist.

seniorThe error budget is exhausted with two weeks left in the window. What happens?

Answer

Whatever the policy you agreed in advance says — and the interview answer is that the policy exists before the moment, is written down, and is owned jointly by engineering and product. A typical policy: a feature freeze on that service, with only reliability work, security fixes and rollbacks shipping; a required review of what consumed the budget; and an explicit exception process with a named approver so genuinely urgent business work is not blocked by dogma. Before enforcing anything, sanity-check the measurement: a surprising number of exhausted budgets are instrumentation faults, a bad deploy of the SLI recording rule, or one synthetic client hammering an endpoint. If the budget is genuinely gone and the causes are structural, the right escalation is not more freeze — it is a conversation about whether the target is right, since a target you cannot hit for three consecutive windows is a target nobody has funded. What you must not do is silently reset the budget or quietly exclude the traffic that broke it.

Example

Budget statePolicy
>50% remainingShip freely; use the budget for risky experiments
10-50%Normal; slow burn alert opens a ticket
<10%Reliability work prioritised; risky deploys need approval
ExhaustedFeature freeze on this service; named-approver exceptions only
Exhausted 3 windows runningRe-negotiate the target or fund the work; the number is not the problem

Follow-ups

  • Who can override the freeze? — A named accountable person, with the exception recorded. An override with no record turns the policy into theatre.
  • Does a single huge incident justify an exclusion? — Only under a documented rule agreed beforehand (for example, a third-party regional outage). Retroactive exclusions destroy trust in the SLO.
  • What if the team disagrees the incidents were their fault? — Often correct, and it is the right trigger to move the SLI measurement point or split the SLO by dependency.

Common wrong answer

"We'd stop all feature work until reliability improves." Said without a pre-agreed policy or an exception path, this is either not credible or actively harmful, and interviewers push on it hard. The senior answer is that the policy was agreed with product before the budget ran out, and includes both a freeze and a way to override it deliberately.

principalYou are asked to introduce SLOs across 80 teams. How do you avoid 80 vanity SLOs?

Answer

Do not start with 80 teams. Start with the three to five user journeys the business would name if you woke the CEO — sign-in, search, checkout, the API partners depend on — and define SLOs for those journeys end to end, crossing whatever services they cross. Journey SLOs resist gaming in a way per-service ones do not: a service can be 99.99% available while the journey through it fails, and nobody can hide behind "my service was green". Then give teams a platform, not a mandate: a generated SLO from a declarative spec, standard recording rules, burn-rate alerts and a dashboard produced automatically from a few lines of config, so the cost of having an SLO is minutes. Make the artefacts social: SLO status reviewed in the same forum as delivery, budget policy agreed with product, and postmortems referencing budget spend. Finally, audit for the vanity failure modes — SLIs measured behind the failure point, denominators that exclude the interesting traffic, targets set below current performance, and SLOs with no alert attached — and treat a green SLO during a real customer-visible incident as a defect in the SLO, not a success.

Example

# the whole team-facing surface: a declarative SLO that generates
# recording rules, burn-rate alerts and a dashboard
apiVersion: slo/v1
kind: ServiceLevelObjective
metadata: { name: checkout-availability, service: checkout }
spec:
  objective: 99.9
  window: 30d
  indicator:
    good:  sum(rate(http_server_requests_total{route="/checkout",status!~"5.."}[5m]))
    valid: sum(rate(http_server_requests_total{route="/checkout"}[5m]))
  alerting:
    page:   { burn_rate: 14.4, long: 1h, short: 5m }
    ticket: { burn_rate: 1,    long: 3d, short: 6h }

Follow-ups

  • How do you detect a vanity SLO? — It has never been below target, it has no alert, or it stayed green through an incident. All three are auditable automatically.
  • Who owns a journey SLO that crosses six teams? — The product owner of the journey, with each service team owning its contribution. Unowned journey SLOs decay within a quarter.
  • What is the first thing you would cut from the rollout? — Breadth. Five real SLOs that drive decisions beat 200 that decorate dashboards.

Common wrong answer

"Every service defines an SLO by end of quarter, tracked on a compliance dashboard." You will get 80 SLOs, all green, none connected to a decision, and the exercise will discredit SLOs in that organisation for years. Journeys first, platform second, breadth last.

06The production debugging playbook

This unit is the one interviewers most often deliver as a scenario rather than a question — "you get paged at 3am, the checkout error rate is 8%, go" — and they are scoring your method, not your guess. What they listen for: do you mitigate before you diagnose, do you form a hypothesis and name the observation that would falsify it, do you narrow by dimension instead of by intuition, and do you say out loud what you are ruling out. At lead level they also watch whether you would run this with five other engineers in the room without turning it into six parallel investigations.

screenerYou are paged: checkout error rate is 8%. What are your first five minutes?

Answer

Confirm, scope, mitigate, then diagnose — in that order. Confirm it is real and user-visible: check the SLI at the point closest to the user, not one internal metric, and check that the alert is not an instrumentation fault. Scope it: which routes, which regions, which tenants, which client versions, and since when — the "since when" almost always points at the change. Mitigate before you understand: if a deploy went out in the window, roll back; if one dependency is failing, shed or fall back; if one tenant is causing it, throttle them. Diagnosis can wait, customer impact cannot, and a rollback is reversible while a fix under pressure often is not. Only then diagnose, and communicate at every step — declare the incident, say what you know and what you are doing, and give a next-update time.

Example

t+0   ack page; open the SLI dashboard   -> 8% 5xx on /checkout, EU only, started 02:41
t+1   what changed at 02:41?             -> deploy checkout 2026.9.1 at 02:39
t+2   declare incident, post: impact, scope, action, next update 15 min
t+3   roll back to 2026.9.0               (mitigate first, understand later)
t+6   error rate falling; confirm at the gateway, not just in the app
t+10  now diagnose: diff the release, pull traces from the bad window

Follow-ups

  • When would you not roll back first? — When the release contains a schema migration that is not backwards compatible, or when the symptom clearly predates the deploy. Say the caveat; it is what separates reflex from judgment.
  • What if you cannot tell whether it is real? — Treat it as real and verify in parallel. The cost of a five-minute false alarm is much lower than the cost of a twenty-minute delay.
  • Who do you tell? — Declare the incident and post in the channel immediately, before you have answers. Silence during an outage is the thing stakeholders remember.

Common wrong answer

"I'd look at the logs and find the root cause." Root-causing first is the most common and most costly mistake: it optimises for your curiosity while customers keep failing. Interviewers explicitly listen for "mitigate first" — and for the fact that you rolled back before you knew why.

midGive me your systematic method for narrowing down a production problem.

Answer

Differential diagnosis by dimension. Start with the symptom expressed as a number, then split it by every dimension you have — service, route, region, availability zone, instance, tenant, client version, deploy ID, feature flag — and look for the cut where the bad population separates cleanly from the good one. "All errors are on three of forty pods" is a host problem; "all errors are one tenant" is a data or quota problem; "all errors are client version 4.2" is a contract problem; "uniform across everything" is a shared dependency or a global change. Each split is a hypothesis with a prediction, so you are falsifying rather than browsing. When the dimensions run out, move down the stack: the request path (trace), then the process (profile, GC, thread state), then the machine (CPU, memory, disk, network), then the dependency. Write the hypotheses and the rulings-out somewhere visible, because in a real incident three people will otherwise re-check the same thing.

Example

# the split that usually ends the investigation in under two minutes
topk(10,
  sum by (pod, http_route, tenant_id, service_version) (
    rate(http_server_requests_total{status=~"5..", service="checkout"}[5m])
  )
)

Follow-ups

  • What if no dimension separates? — That itself is evidence: a uniform failure points at something shared — a dependency, DNS, a config rollout, a certificate, a clock.
  • How do you avoid confirmation bias? — State the prediction before you look, and note what would disprove it. "If it's the deploy, the error should start within a minute of the rollout, and it should be absent in the canary."
  • How long before you change approach? — Time-box each hypothesis (ten minutes is a common rule) and hand it off if it is unresolved; incidents are lost to sunk-cost investigations.

Common wrong answer

"I'd check the usual suspects — database, network, memory." Pattern-matching from past incidents works right up until this incident is not one of them, and it produces the classic 40-minute detour where three engineers investigate a database that was fine all along. Splitting the population by dimension is evidence; a suspect list is a guess.

midA product manager says "the app feels slow". How do you turn that into a measurement?

Answer

Convert it into who, what, where, when, and how much — because "slow" without those is unfalsifiable. Who: which users, which tenants, which client version, which platform. What: which interaction — page load, search, checkout — because "the app" is a dozen different latency distributions. Where: which region, and is it client-side or server-side, which RUM answers immediately and server metrics never do. When: since when, and is it continuous or time-of-day shaped. How much: p50 versus p95 versus p99, since "feels slow" usually means the tail got worse while the median did not move — a change from a 200 ms p50 with a 900 ms p99 to a 210 ms p50 with a 6 s p99 feels catastrophic and is invisible on an average. Then compare against a baseline from before the reported change; without a baseline you cannot tell a regression from normal.

Example

# compare the distribution, not the average, and compare against last week
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{route="/search"}[5m])))
  /
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket{route="/search"}[5m] offset 7d)))

Follow-ups

  • Server metrics look flat but users complain — what next? — Look client-side: RUM, TTFB versus render, CDN, DNS, and the mobile network. Server-side latency excludes most of what a user experiences.
  • Which percentile should you care about? — The one that matches the experience: p50 for "everything feels sluggish", p99 for "sometimes it hangs". Also check per-user worst-case, since one user's session may hit the tail repeatedly.
  • What if there is no baseline? — Establish one now and compare against a control group — another region, another cohort, another route — rather than against a feeling.

Common wrong answer

"Our average response time is 180 ms, so it isn't slow." Averages hide exactly the failure being reported, and answering a user complaint with a mean is the fastest way to lose the room. The interviewer is testing whether you reach for the distribution and for the client side.

midHow do you establish whether a deploy caused the problem?

Answer

Correlate on time and on population. Time: overlay deploy markers (from the CI/CD system, or as annotations on the dashboard) on the symptom, and check that the symptom starts after the rollout begins and tracks the rollout's progress — a symptom that grows as pods are replaced is very strong evidence. Population: because rollouts are gradual, you almost always have a natural control group — compare the error rate of pods on the new service.version against the old ones in the same window. That single query settles most arguments, and it is the reason service.version and a deploy or build ID belong in your resource attributes. If the versions look identical, the deploy is probably innocent and you should look at what else changed: feature flags, configuration, a dependency's own deploy, a scheduled job, a traffic shift, or a certificate rotation. Flag changes are the most under-instrumented change source in most estates — treat a flag flip as a deploy and emit an event for it.

Example

# natural A/B: old version versus new, same window, same traffic
sum by (service_version) (rate(http_server_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]))
  /
sum by (service_version) (rate(http_server_requests_total[5m]))

Follow-ups

  • What if the deploy was hours before the symptom? — Look for something that accumulates: a leak, a cache filling, a queue draining, a nightly job hitting new code, or a lazily-loaded path first exercised at peak.
  • What is the fastest way to make this question easy? — Canary deploys with automatic comparison, plus deploy and flag events in the same store as your telemetry.
  • Correlation is not causation — how do you strengthen it? — Roll back and watch the symptom follow. That is an experiment, not a correlation.

Common wrong answer

"Nothing was deployed, so it can't be a change." Something almost always changed — a flag, a config map, a dependency's release, a data volume crossing a threshold, a scheduled job, an expiring certificate. Asserting "no change" without checking the non-code change sources is the mistake that sends teams chasing ghosts for hours.

seniorIt only happens in production and you cannot reproduce it. How do you proceed?

Answer

Accept that production is the environment and instrument it rather than trying to recreate it. First, enumerate what production has that your environments do not: real data volume and skew, real concurrency, real hardware and neighbours, real network, real client diversity, and time — leaks and cache growth need hours. Then get evidence without a reproduction: raise sampling or logging for the affected slice only (one tenant, one route, one trace ID) using dynamic configuration rather than a fleet-wide change; capture a snapshot of the process at the moment of failure — a heap dump, a thread dump, a CPU profile of the affected pod; and add targeted instrumentation as a small, fast deploy, because "we need three more attributes on this span" is usually a ten-minute change and answers the question permanently. If the failure is rare, capture on trigger: a rule that dumps state when a request exceeds a threshold. Finally, shrink the search space with production experiments you control — shadow traffic, a canary with the suspect code path, or a feature flag toggled for 1% of users while you watch.

Example

# capture the process at the moment of the symptom, on the affected pod only
kubectl exec -it checkout-7d9f -- dotnet-dump collect -p 1
kubectl exec -it checkout-7d9f -- dotnet-counters monitor -p 1 \
  --counters System.Runtime,Microsoft.AspNetCore.Hosting

# or a targeted, time-boxed log-level change for one tenant
curl -X POST localhost:8080/admin/loglevel \
  -d '{"logger":"Checkout.Payments","level":"Debug","ttl":"10m","tenant":"acme"}'

Follow-ups

  • Why not just turn on debug logging everywhere? — Cost and blast radius: at scale it can saturate the logging pipeline and cause a second incident. Scope it by tenant, route or trace, and time-box it.
  • Is adding instrumentation during an incident acceptable? — Yes, if it is small, additive and behind a fast deploy path. A ten-minute instrumentation deploy often beats an hour of speculation — and it is permanent value.
  • What about debuggers in production? — Breakpoints, no; non-breaking snapshot debuggers and dynamic logging tools, yes, with rate limits and audit.

Common wrong answer

"I'd try to reproduce it locally with the same inputs." For genuinely production-only failures — concurrency, data skew, resource pressure, a specific downstream version — you can spend days and never reproduce it. The senior move is to make production observable enough to answer the question in place.

senior0.1% of requests fail, seemingly at random. How do you catch it?

Answer

"Random" is a hypothesis to destroy, not a conclusion. Take the failing population and compare it against the succeeding one on every attribute you have — one in a thousand is plenty of events at scale, so a clean split will show up quickly. Usual culprits at that rate: one bad instance or node out of a thousand; one shard, partition or replica; a connection-pool or DNS entry that has gone stale on a subset of clients; a race that needs a specific interleaving; a retry storm hitting a rate limit; a single tenant with unusual data; a client version; or a certificate/keepalive expiring on a schedule. Make sure the failures are actually captured: at 0.1%, head sampling at 1% keeps almost nothing, so force sampling on error and keep 100% of failing traces. Then look at the shape in time — a periodic spike points at a cron, a cache TTL or a token refresh; a burst points at a deploy or a scale event; genuinely uniform-in-time points at a per-request race or a load-balanced bad instance.

Example

# is it one instance? (a 0.1% overall rate can be 100% of one pod out of 1000)
topk(5, sum by (instance) (rate(requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m])))
  /
sum by (instance) (rate(requests_total[5m]))

# is it periodic? look at the failure rate at high resolution over a day

Follow-ups

  • Why is "one bad instance" so common? — Because load balancers keep sending traffic to a process that is alive but broken: a wedged thread pool, a full disk, an exhausted connection pool, a corrupted local cache. Health checks that only test liveness will not remove it.
  • What if the failures never appear in traces? — Then they fail before your instrumentation: at the LB, in TLS, in DNS, or as a client-side timeout. Look at the gateway's own logs and at RUM.
  • How do you tell a race from a bad instance? — A race spreads across instances and correlates with concurrency; a bad instance is concentrated and correlates with routing.

Common wrong answer

"0.1% is within tolerance, it's just noise — probably transient network errors." Sometimes true, but it is the answer that ends the investigation before it starts, and at 100k rps 0.1% is 100 failing users per second. Interviewers ask this to see whether you treat a small number as a population to be split, not as an acceptable constant.

seniorThe service has almost no instrumentation. What can you still learn from a running process?

Answer

A great deal, and being able to list it is a strong senior signal. From outside the process: CPU, memory, page faults and run-queue length; open file descriptors and socket states (ss -s — a pile of CLOSE_WAIT means the app is not closing connections, TIME_WAIT exhaustion means a client-side port problem); disk latency; and packet capture or eBPF tracing for the traffic that is actually flowing. From inside, without changing code: a thread or stack dump answers "what are all my threads doing" — the single most valuable artefact for a hung service; a CPU profile answers "where is the time going"; a heap dump answers "what is holding memory". Language runtimes expose more: JFR and jcmd in Java, dotnet-dump/dotnet-counters/EventPipe in .NET, pprof endpoints in Go, py-spy in Python — none of which require the application to have been instrumented in advance. And the highest-value cheap step is always the same: turn on the framework's built-in metrics endpoint and add a request log with a duration field, because that alone gives you RED.

Example

ss -s                              # socket summary: CLOSE_WAIT / TIME_WAIT pile-ups
ss -tnp state close-wait | head    # who is leaking connections
jcmd <pid> Thread.print            # every thread's stack: the hung-service answer
dotnet-counters monitor -p <pid>   # GC, thread pool queue length, exceptions/s
go tool pprof http://localhost:6060/debug/pprof/profile?seconds=30

Follow-ups

  • What does a thread dump tell you that a profile does not? — Where threads are blocked. A profile shows CPU; a hung service usually has near-zero CPU and every thread waiting on the same lock or socket.
  • Is taking a heap dump in production safe? — It pauses the process and can be gigabytes; do it on one instance removed from the load balancer, not on all of them.
  • What can eBPF add here? — Syscall, network and function-level tracing without touching the application, which is exactly the gap when the process cannot be changed.

Common wrong answer

"Without instrumentation you can't really debug it — we'd have to add telemetry and wait for it to happen again." Sometimes that is the right long-term action, but as an immediate answer it concedes the incident. Interviewers use this question specifically to find out whether you have ever opened a thread dump.

seniorEvery dashboard is red across twelve services. How do you find the origin?

Answer

In a cascade, the useful question is not "what is broken" but "what broke first". Order the services by the timestamp at which each one's error rate or latency began to deviate — a second of resolution is usually enough — and the origin is at the head of that list; everything after it is a victim. Corroborate with the dependency graph, which your service map or span data already gives you: the origin is a leaf or a shared dependency, not a front-end. Then check the shared substrate that produces simultaneous failure everywhere: DNS, the identity provider, a config or secret rollout, a certificate, the network, the container runtime, and — embarrassingly often — the observability pipeline itself, which can make healthy services look dead. During this, resist the pull of the loudest dashboard: the front door has the most traffic so it screams first and loudest, but it is almost never the cause.

Example

first deviation (from the error-rate onset per service):
  02:41:03  postgres-primary   connection saturation     <-- origin
  02:41:11  orders             pool wait, timeouts
  02:41:14  checkout           upstream timeouts, retries
  02:41:16  api-gateway        5xx to users               <-- loudest, latest

Follow-ups

  • What if timestamps are ambiguous? — Look at the type of failure: timeouts and pool exhaustion are downstream symptoms; connection refused, disk full, or a crash loop are upstream causes.
  • How do retries confuse this? — They amplify: one slow dependency becomes 3× load, which makes the caller look like the problem. If retry counts are up everywhere, suspect amplification and check the origin's saturation.
  • What should you build to make this easy? — A service map with error and latency overlays, and clock discipline (NTP) so ordering across services is trustworthy.

Common wrong answer

"Start where the customer impact is — the API gateway — and work inward." Sounds customer-focused, and it is how teams spend forty minutes examining the one service that is behaving correctly under an impossible load. Order by first deviation; the loudest signal is usually the last domino.

principalSix engineers are on the call and everyone is investigating. How do you run it?

Answer

Impose structure immediately, because six unmanaged investigators produce six half-investigations and one very confused stakeholder channel. Take (or explicitly assign) incident command and stop doing hands-on work yourself — the commander coordinates, they do not debug. Split the room into two tracks: mitigation, which owns restoring service by any reversible means, and diagnosis, which owns understanding — and make it explicit that mitigation wins ties. Assign hypotheses to named people, one each, with a time box and an instruction to report back whether it is confirmed or ruled out; maintain the shared list of hypotheses, findings and rulings-out in the channel so nobody repeats work. Appoint a communications lead for stakeholders and a scribe for the timeline, because the postmortem is written from that timeline and reconstructing it afterwards is both painful and inaccurate. Enforce one change at a time with an announced owner: parallel unannounced fixes are how a bad incident becomes a much worse one, and they destroy the ability to attribute recovery to any action.

Example

IC: Adam (not debugging)          COMMS: Priya       SCRIBE: Tom
MITIGATION  Sam   - roll back checkout 2026.9.1        [in progress, ETA 4m]
DIAGNOSIS   Lea   - H1 connection pool exhaustion      [10m box]
            Nils  - H2 payments provider degraded      [10m box]
RULED OUT   - not DNS (resolution normal from 3 zones, 02:45)
            - not the flag rollout (unchanged since Tuesday)
NEXT UPDATE 03:15
CHANGE LOCK: only Sam changes prod until further notice

Follow-ups

  • What if a senior engineer ignores the structure and starts changing things? — Stop it immediately and in public; the change lock is the rule that prevents the second incident. Discuss it after, not during.
  • When do you hand over command? — On fatigue (2-4 hours), on a shift boundary, or when the incident's centre of gravity moves to a domain someone else owns. Hand over explicitly, with the state document.
  • How do you keep stakeholders out of the technical channel? — A separate stakeholder channel with scheduled updates and a named comms lead. Never let the technical channel become the status channel.

Common wrong answer

"As the most senior person I'd dig into the hardest part myself." It is the instinct that feels most responsible and it is the classic failure of a lead in an incident: the best debugger becomes unavailable for coordination, nobody is tracking mitigation, and the timeline is lost. If you are commanding, you are not debugging.

07Incident response and command

Once you are interviewing at lead or principal level, this unit carries as much weight as any technical one, and it is scored on specifics: do you have a severity scale you can recite, do you know what an incident commander does and does not do, can you write a postmortem that changes something. Junior candidates describe how incidents feel; strong candidates describe roles, decisions and artefacts. Every answer here should sound like it came from an incident you actually ran.

screenerWhen do you declare an incident, and how do you pick a severity?

Answer

Declare early and deliberately: the cost of declaring an incident that turns out to be minor is a few minutes of overhead, while the cost of not declaring is that nobody is coordinating, nobody is communicating, and there is no timeline. The trigger should be objective — an SLO burn-rate page, a customer-visible failure, or any situation where one person needs help from another team. Severity should be defined by impact and response required, not by how alarming the technology looks: SEV1 means major customer-visible outage or data loss, all hands, immediate comms; SEV2 significant degradation or a major feature broken, dedicated response during business hours or paged out of hours; SEV3 limited or internal impact, handled by the owning team in normal hours. Give each level a defined response — who is paged, who communicates, how often — otherwise the number is decoration. And make it explicit that severity can be raised or lowered as you learn; the initial call is a starting point.

Example

SevImpactResponseComms
SEV1Core journey down, or data loss/exposureIC + all needed teams, immediateStatus page + updates every 30 min
SEV2Major degradation, workaround existsIC + owning team, pagedInternal + affected customers
SEV3Limited or internal impactOwning team, business hoursInternal channel only

Follow-ups

  • Who can declare? — Anyone. If declaring is gated on seniority you get delayed declarations, which is the failure mode that actually costs money.
  • Does a security event follow the same scale? — Usually a parallel track with its own severities and a different escalation path, because the containment and legal obligations differ.
  • What if you declared and it was nothing? — Say so, close it, and never criticise the declaration. The moment declaring feels risky, people stop.

Common wrong answer

"We declare once we understand the impact." That inverts the order: you declare in order to get the people who can determine impact. Waiting for certainty is the most common reason a 20-minute outage becomes a 2-hour one.

midWhat are the roles in incident response and why separate them?

Answer

Four, borrowed from emergency incident command. The incident commander owns the incident: they decide, delegate, keep the state, and explicitly do not debug — their value is that someone has the whole picture. Operations (or the tech lead) owns making changes to the system; they are the only ones with hands on production, which is what makes the change log coherent. Communications owns stakeholders, the status page and customers, so that the technical channel is not interrupted every four minutes by "any update?". The scribe keeps the timeline: what was observed, what was decided, what was changed, at what time — and this is the single most under-valued role, because the postmortem, the customer statement and any regulatory report are all written from it. They are separated because in a real incident every one of these jobs is full-time; a single person doing all four does all four badly, and the first thing to be dropped is always communication.

Example

SEV1 payments outage
  IC        Priya   - decides, delegates, holds state; does NOT debug
  OPS       Sam     - the only person changing prod
  COMMS     Lea     - status page, exec channel, customer support brief
  SCRIBE    Tom     - timeline: 02:41 onset, 02:47 declared, 02:53 rollback started
  SME       Nils    - payments domain expert, on call for questions

Follow-ups

  • Small incident, two people — still four roles? — The roles collapse but the responsibilities do not: one person is IC and comms, the other is ops. Say who is who out loud even with two people.
  • Should the IC be the most technical person? — No, and preferably not: your best debugger is most valuable debugging. IC is a coordination skill and should be trained and rostered separately.
  • What does the IC actually say? — "What do we know, what are we doing, who is doing it, when do we next check in." Repeated every ten minutes.

Common wrong answer

"Whoever is on call handles it and pulls people in as needed." That works for SEV3 and collapses at SEV1: the on-call engineer ends up debugging, updating executives and coordinating three teams simultaneously, and communication stops within ten minutes. Naming roles explicitly at declaration time is the thing interviewers listen for.

midWhat makes a postmortem worth writing, and what does "blameless" actually mean?

Answer

A postmortem is worth writing when it changes something. That requires four parts done honestly: a precise timeline with detection, decisions and actions; an impact statement in user terms (how many users, for how long, what did they experience, what was the budget spend); a contributing-factors analysis that goes past the proximate trigger into why the system allowed it and why it was not caught sooner; and action items that are specific, owned, dated and tracked in the same backlog as feature work. Blameless means the analysis assumes people acted reasonably given the information and incentives they had, so the question is never "who deployed it" but "what made this deploy look safe" — the deploy that broke production is evidence about your safety net, not about the engineer. That is not the same as no accountability: teams still own their services and their action items; what you remove is the incentive to hide facts, because a postmortem written under fear of blame is a postmortem with the important detail missing.

Example

Proximate  : a config change set the DB pool to 10 (was 100)
Why it shipped     : config is not code-reviewed and has no schema validation
Why it wasn't caught : staging runs 1/50th of prod traffic; pool saturation is invisible there
Why detection was slow : the alert was on CPU, not on pool wait time or SLO burn
Actions    : (1) schema + review for config  [owner, date]
             (2) pool saturation SLI + burn alert  [owner, date]
             (3) load profile in staging for pool-sensitive paths  [owner, date]

Follow-ups

  • Which incidents get a postmortem? — All SEV1 and SEV2, plus any near miss the team found interesting. Near misses are the cheapest lessons available.
  • How many action items? — Few and real. Twenty unowned items is a document nobody reads; three funded ones change the system.
  • What is the tell of a bad postmortem? — A single root cause, an action item that says "be more careful", and no mention of why detection took as long as it did.

Common wrong answer

"We do a five-whys and identify the root cause." Complex systems fail from combinations, not from a single cause, and five-whys reliably terminates at "human error" — the least useful conclusion available. Say "contributing factors" and include the detection and response failures, not just the trigger.

midHow do you design an on-call rotation that people can sustain?

Answer

Four constraints. Load: a page rate that lets people sleep — commonly no more than about two pages per 12-hour shift, and if you cannot hit that, the fix is deleting alerts, not hardening humans. Size: at least six to eight people in a rotation, so a week on call comes round every couple of months rather than every fortnight; below that, attrition does the rostering for you. Support: every alert has a runbook with the first three things to check and the mitigation, the escalation path is written down and answered, and there is an explicit expectation that waking a colleague is fine. Fairness and recovery: compensation or time off in lieu, no on-call during the week you are also delivering a milestone, and a handover at the start of each shift covering what is ongoing. On top of that, ownership matters more than any of it: teams that carry the pager for their own code fix the causes; a separate ops team paging on someone else's code fixes symptoms forever.

Example

Runbook: CheckoutFastBurn
  What it means : >2% of the 30d error budget burned in the last hour
  First checks  : (1) recent deploys/flags, (2) payments provider status,
                  (3) DB pool wait, (4) error-rate split by pod and tenant
  Mitigations   : roll back last release | disable flag pricing_v2 | shed tenant traffic
  Escalate to   : payments team (@payments-oncall) after 15 min with no improvement
  Do not        : restart pods before capturing a thread dump

Follow-ups

  • What do you do about a rotation with four people? — Merge rotations, reduce scope, or fund hiring; do not pretend it is sustainable. Interviewers respect the answer that names the constraint.
  • How do you improve runbooks? — The on-call engineer updates the runbook as part of closing the page. If a runbook did not help, that is a defect worth ten minutes.
  • Follow-the-sun? — Excellent if you genuinely have teams in three regions who own the same services; a bureaucratic disaster if the handover crosses ownership boundaries.

Common wrong answer

"We rotate weekly and everyone helps out when it's busy." No page-rate target, no escalation policy, no runbooks, no recovery — this is the arrangement that quietly produces burnout and resignations. The number interviewers want to hear is pages per shift, treated as a metric you actively manage down.

seniorRollback, roll forward, or feature flag — how do you choose under pressure?

Answer

Prefer the fastest reversible action with the smallest blast radius, and prefer it before you understand the cause. A feature flag is best when it exists: seconds to apply, precisely scoped, no deploy pipeline involved — which is why "everything risky ships behind a flag" is worth the engineering cost. Rollback is next: minutes, well-tested path, reverses the whole change; its blocker is state — a database migration that is not backwards compatible, a message format the consumers have already adopted, a cache the new version populated in a new shape. Roll forward is the last resort during an incident, because you are writing new code under time pressure with no review time; it is right only when rollback is impossible (irreversible migration) or when the fix is trivially small and well understood. Whatever you pick, announce it, do one at a time, and watch the SLI — an action whose effect you cannot see is indistinguishable from no action.

Example

Feature flag offSecondsYesFlag not existing; flag system down
RollbackMinutesYesNon-backwards-compatible schema or data
Traffic shift / shedSeconds-minutesYesSingle region; stateful routing
Roll forwardTens of minutesNoReview, build, test under pressure

Follow-ups

  • How do you keep rollback always available? — Expand/contract migrations (add column, dual-write, backfill, switch reads, only then drop), versioned message contracts, and a tested rollback in CI — an untested rollback path is a hypothesis.
  • What if the rollback also fails? — Then you are in a bigger incident: shed load, serve degraded, and consider standing up a known-good version alongside. Say that you would communicate the change in posture.
  • Do flags create their own risk? — Yes: flag combinations are untested state space, and stale flags accumulate. Treat flag changes as deploys — reviewed, logged, emitted as events.

Common wrong answer

"We'd fix forward — rolling back loses the work and the fix is simple." Under incident pressure "simple" is an unreviewed guess, and roll-forward has no undo if it is wrong. Interviewers want reversibility first, with the migration caveat named unprompted.

seniorWhat do you tell customers during an outage, and when?

Answer

Publish early, in terms of impact rather than cause, and on a schedule you commit to. The first update should go out within minutes of declaring a customer-visible incident and should say what is affected, who is affected, what a customer should do (or that no action is needed), and when the next update will come — even if the answer to everything else is "we are investigating". Then keep the cadence you promised, because a missed update is read as chaos. Never speculate about cause, never blame a vendor by name before you are certain, and never say "resolved" until you have confirmed recovery from the customer's side, not just on your dashboard. Afterwards, publish a summary proportionate to the impact — for a major outage, a public postmortem with the timeline, the impact and what will change — which is the thing that actually rebuilds trust. Internally, keep stakeholders in a separate channel with the same cadence, so the technical channel stays technical.

Example

02:47  Investigating - some customers are seeing errors when completing checkout.
       Browsing and cart are unaffected. Next update by 03:15.
03:10  Identified - a change released at 02:39 is causing checkout failures in
       the EU region. We are rolling it back. Next update by 03:40.
03:32  Monitoring - the rollback is complete and error rates are back to normal.
       We are monitoring. Next update by 04:15.
04:10  Resolved - checkout has been operating normally since 03:28. Approximately
       31 minutes of degraded checkout for EU customers. A full report will follow.

Follow-ups

  • Who writes it? — The comms lead, from the scribe's timeline, with the IC approving. Not the person debugging.
  • What about contractual notification windows? — Know them in advance (many enterprise and regulatory agreements have hard clocks) and treat them as part of the incident checklist, not an afterthought.
  • How specific should the impact number be? — As specific as you can defend. "Approximately 4% of EU checkout attempts between 02:41 and 03:28" beats "some users may have experienced issues".

Common wrong answer

"We wait until we know what's wrong so we don't give out incorrect information." Silence is itself information, and customers fill it with worse assumptions — plus support gets flooded because nobody has told them anything. Impact-first, cause-later, on a promised cadence.

seniorThe same incident happened three times this quarter. What is going wrong?

Answer

Repeat incidents are almost never a mystery about the technology; they are a signal that the loop from incident to change is broken. Check the obvious first: were action items created, were they owned by a named person with a date, and did they enter the same backlog as feature work — because items parked in a separate "reliability" list have a completion rate close to zero. Then check whether the actions addressed the actual contributing factors or only the proximate trigger: fixing the specific bad config value while leaving config unvalidated and unreviewed guarantees a fourth occurrence with a different value. Then look for a systemic cause the team cannot fix alone — an architectural weakness, a dependency that is simply unreliable, or a capacity ceiling — which needs escalation and funding, not another action item. As a lead, the two mechanisms I would put in place are a standing review of overdue action items with the same seriousness as an outage, and a rule that a repeat incident automatically escalates one severity level and gets a different reviewer.

Example

Incident 1 (Jul)  pool exhausted -> action: raise pool size          [done]
Incident 2 (Aug)  pool exhausted -> action: raise pool size again    [done]
Incident 3 (Sep)  pool exhausted -> the actual problem was never addressed:
                  no saturation SLI, no back-pressure, no admission control,
                  and a retry policy that triples load exactly when saturated

Follow-ups

  • How do you get action items prioritised against features? — Error-budget policy, plus expressing the item as expected cost avoided. "This class of incident cost 9 hours of engineering and 40% of the budget this quarter" is a product conversation.
  • What if the fix is genuinely a rewrite? — Say so explicitly, propose an interim mitigation with a known cost, and put the decision in front of whoever owns the risk. Silent under-mitigation is the failure.
  • How do you spot the pattern earlier? — Tag incidents by contributing factor and review the distribution quarterly; the third occurrence should have been visible after the second.

Common wrong answer

"We need better monitoring so we catch it faster next time." Faster detection of an incident you should not be having is a consolation prize, and it is the reflex answer that gets flagged as symptom-treating. The interviewer is testing whether you look at the action-item pipeline and the systemic factor.

principalHow would you measure the health of an incident-response programme? What is wrong with MTTR?

Answer

MTTR is a mean over a wildly skewed, small-N distribution of heterogeneous events, so it is unstable, easy to game (declare fewer incidents, or split one into three) and it hides the thing you care about — the long tail. If you must use duration, use the distribution: median and 90th percentile time to detect, to mitigate, and to resolve, tracked separately, because they have different fixes — detection is an alerting problem, mitigation is a tooling and reversibility problem, resolution is an engineering problem. Then add the measures that are harder to game: proportion of incidents detected by alerting rather than by customers; proportion of SEV1/SEV2 with a postmortem within a week; action-item completion rate and age; repeat-incident rate by contributing factor; pages per on-call shift and their actioned rate; and error-budget consumption by service. And read them as a portfolio, quarterly, for trend — not as targets for individual teams, because the moment a team is measured on MTTR the reliable response is to stop declaring incidents.

08OpenTelemetry in practice

OpenTelemetry is now the default answer to "how do you instrument", so interviewers have moved past "have you heard of it" to "have you operated it". The high-yield areas are the API/SDK split, the collector as an architectural component, what is actually stable today, and the migration story from a vendor agent. Be precise about maturity — claiming a signal is stable when it is in alpha is an instant credibility loss, and the maturity table moves every quarter.

screenerWhat is OpenTelemetry and what does it actually give you?

Answer

It is a CNCF project — the second most active after Kubernetes — that standardises the production of telemetry so that the choice of backend stops being a code decision. Five parts matter. The API: the interface your code and libraries call, with a no-op default. The SDK: the implementation you configure with samplers, processors and exporters. OTLP: the wire protocol for traces, metrics, logs and profiles. The Collector: a standalone binary that receives, processes and exports telemetry, so you can transform, filter, sample and route without touching applications. And the semantic conventions: agreed names for attributes and metrics, which is what makes telemetry from 300 services queryable as one dataset. What it deliberately does not provide is storage, query or dashboards — that is where a backend still comes in.

Example

app ──OTLP──> collector (agent, per node)
                 │  receivers -> processors -> exporters
                 ▼
              collector (gateway, tail sampling / routing)
                 ├──> metrics backend (Prometheus-compatible)
                 ├──> trace backend
                 └──> log backend

Follow-ups

  • What is the current maturity per signal? — Traces, metrics and logs are stable across the major SDKs; profiles entered public alpha in March 2026 (collector support from v0.148.0). Say the alpha part out loud.
  • Is it a replacement for Prometheus? — No: it replaces the instrumentation and the shipping, not the TSDB. Prometheus 3.x can even ingest OTLP directly.
  • What did it merge from? — OpenTracing (API) and OpenCensus (SDK plus collector). Both are archived; you may still find them in old code.

Common wrong answer

"OpenTelemetry is an observability platform." It is deliberately not: no storage, no UI, no alerting. Candidates who describe it as a platform usually mean "my vendor's agent is OTel-based", and the follow-up about the collector exposes that quickly.

screenerWhy does OpenTelemetry separate the API from the SDK?

Answer

So that libraries can be instrumented without imposing a telemetry stack on their users. A library depends only on the API, which is a thin, stable, no-op-by-default interface: if the application never installs an SDK, the calls compile to almost nothing and cost effectively zero. If the application does install and configure an SDK, the same calls suddenly produce real spans and metrics. That is what makes native instrumentation in HTTP clients, database drivers and frameworks viable at all — the alternative would be every library shipping a hard dependency on a specific telemetry implementation, which nobody would accept. The practical consequence for you: application code configures the SDK exactly once at startup, library code and shared internal packages depend on the API only, and the version-compatibility guarantee is on the API, which is why it changes slowly.

Example

// library: API only. No SDK dependency, no-op unless the app configures one.
private static readonly ActivitySource Source = new("Acme.Payments", "1.4.0");
using var activity = Source.StartActivity("payments.authorize");
activity?.SetTag("payment.provider", provider);   // note the null-conditional

// application: configures the SDK once, at startup
builder.Services.AddOpenTelemetry()
    .ConfigureResource(r => r.AddService("checkout", serviceVersion: "2026.9.1"))
    .WithTracing(t => t.AddSource("Acme.Payments")
                       .AddAspNetCoreInstrumentation()
                       .AddOtlpExporter());

Follow-ups

  • What is the cost of an API call with no SDK? — Near zero: a null check and a return. That is the design constraint that makes library instrumentation acceptable.
  • Why is activity?. conditional in .NET? — Because with no listener the activity is null; the null-conditional is the no-op path, and forgetting it is a real NullReferenceException source.
  • Who should configure sampling? — The application, never the library. A library that sets a sampler is broken.

Common wrong answer

"It's just a layering nicety." It is the reason the ecosystem exists: without a no-op API, no third-party library would ever add instrumentation, and you would be back to monkey-patching. Interviewers ask it to see whether you understand why native instrumentation became possible.

midWhat is OTLP, and how do gRPC and HTTP transports differ in practice?

Answer

OTLP is the OpenTelemetry Protocol: a Protobuf-defined request/response format for traces, metrics, logs and profiles, with a defined retry and partial-success behaviour. It has two transports: gRPC (default port 4317) and HTTP with either Protobuf or JSON bodies (default port 4318). gRPC gives you multiplexed streams over one connection, better throughput and built-in flow control, which is why it is the default for service-to-service export; HTTP/protobuf is what you use when something in the path cannot do gRPC — a browser, a serverless platform, a proxy or load balancer that mishandles HTTP/2, or a corporate network that inspects HTTP only. Both support gzip compression and a partial-success response so the server can tell you it rejected some spans. The operational detail worth knowing is the response codes: a 429 or a gRPC RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED means back off (honour Retry-After), while a 400-class rejection means the data is malformed and retrying is pointless.

Example

export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT=http://otel-collector:4317   # gRPC
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_PROTOCOL=grpc
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_COMPRESSION=gzip

# or HTTP, when gRPC is not an option (browser, some serverless, some proxies)
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT=https://otel-collector:4318
export OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_PROTOCOL=http/protobuf

Follow-ups

  • Signal-specific endpoints? — Yes: OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_TRACES_ENDPOINT and friends override the general one, and the HTTP paths are /v1/traces, /v1/metrics, /v1/logs.
  • What about very high volume? — OTel Arrow is a columnar encoding designed for high-volume export with substantially better compression; it is the answer when egress cost dominates.
  • Does OTLP guarantee delivery? — No. It is at-most-once with retries; durability is the exporter's queue, and a crash loses whatever is in memory.

Common wrong answer

"Use gRPC, it's always better." Until it meets an L7 load balancer that terminates HTTP/2 badly, a browser, or a serverless runtime with no gRPC support — all common. Knowing when to fall back to http/protobuf is the practical half of the answer.

midDescribe a collector deployment topology and why you would not export straight to the backend.

Answer

The standard shape is two tiers. An agent collector runs next to the workload — a DaemonSet per node or a sidecar — and does the cheap local work: receiving OTLP over localhost, adding host and Kubernetes resource attributes, batching, and getting data off the application's critical path fast. A gateway tier runs as a horizontally scaled deployment and does the expensive stateful work: tail sampling (with trace-ID-aware load balancing in front of it), span-to-metrics, redaction, routing to multiple backends, and holding the retry queue. Exporting straight from the application to a vendor is possible and it is what everyone does on day one, but it puts backend endpoints, credentials, sampling policy and retry behaviour into every service, so changing any of them becomes a fleet-wide redeploy. The collector is where you buy the ability to change your mind — including changing vendor — without touching application code.

Example

receivers:
  otlp: { protocols: { grpc: { endpoint: 0.0.0.0:4317 } } }
processors:
  memory_limiter: { check_interval: 1s, limit_percentage: 80, spike_limit_percentage: 25 }
  k8sattributes: {}
  resourcedetection: { detectors: [env, system] }
  batch: { timeout: 5s, send_batch_size: 8192 }
exporters:
  otlp/gateway:
    endpoint: otel-gateway:4317
    sending_queue: { enabled: true, num_consumers: 10, queue_size: 5000 }
    retry_on_failure: { enabled: true, initial_interval: 5s, max_elapsed_time: 300s }
service:
  pipelines:
    traces:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [memory_limiter, k8sattributes, resourcedetection, batch]
      exporters: [otlp/gateway]

Follow-ups

  • Why must memory_limiter be first? — It has to reject data before the rest of the pipeline allocates for it. Put it anywhere else and it cannot protect you.
  • Why batch last? — So every processor that drops or modifies data has already run, and you batch only what will actually be sent.
  • Sidecar or DaemonSet? — DaemonSet for efficiency; sidecar when you need per-workload isolation or the pod's own lifecycle (short-lived jobs) makes a node agent unreliable.

Common wrong answer

"We export directly from the app to keep the architecture simple." Simple until you need tail sampling, a redaction rule, a second backend or a vendor change — each of which then costs a redeploy of every service. The collector is the seam that makes those config changes instead of code changes.

midHow do you configure an OpenTelemetry SDK, and what changed in 2026?

Answer

Historically: environment variables. OTEL_SERVICE_NAME, OTEL_RESOURCE_ATTRIBUTES, OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_*, OTEL_TRACES_SAMPLER and OTEL_TRACES_SAMPLER_ARG, plus in-code configuration for anything expressive. That works but it is flat and cannot express structure — you cannot describe two exporters, a view, and a per-instrument aggregation in a string. The change is declarative configuration, which went stable in March 2026: opentelemetry-configuration 1.0.0 defines a JSON schema and YAML file format for the whole SDK, pointed at by OTEL_CONFIG_FILE, and it covers the data model, the in-memory config model, the ConfigProperties representation, a plugin provider mechanism, and the Parse/Create SDK operations. As of the announcement it shipped in C++, Go, Java, JavaScript and PHP, with .NET and Python in progress — so in an interview say "declarative config where the SDK supports it, environment variables elsewhere, and expect env vars that do not map cleanly to be deprecated over time".

Example

# OTEL_CONFIG_FILE=/etc/otel/config.yaml
file_format: "1.0"
resource:
  attributes:
    - name: service.name
      value: checkout
tracer_provider:
  sampler:
    parent_based:
      root: { trace_id_ratio_based: { ratio: 0.1 } }
  processors:
    - batch:
        exporter:
          otlp_grpc: { endpoint: http://otel-collector:4317 }

Follow-ups

  • Which sampler should be the default? — parentbased_traceidratio: respect an upstream decision, and make an independent ratio decision only at the root. A plain ratio sampler at every hop produces broken partial traces.
  • What does OTEL_SDK_DISABLED do? — Turns the SDK into a no-op, which is the emergency switch when instrumentation itself is causing a problem. Know it exists.
  • Where do you set resource attributes? — OTEL_RESOURCE_ATTRIBUTES from the deployment, or the resource block in declarative config — not in source.

Common wrong answer

"Configuration is all in code in our startup file." It works and it is the easiest thing to write, but it means every sampling, endpoint or exporter change is a build and a deploy across the fleet — exactly the coupling OpenTelemetry's configuration story exists to remove.

seniorWhat is stable in OpenTelemetry today and what is not? Be specific.

Answer

Traces, metrics and logs are stable in the specification and across the major language SDKs, though log SDK maturity still varies by language — Go promoted its Logs API and SDK to release candidate during 2026, for instance. Declarative configuration is stable as of March 2026 (opentelemetry-configuration 1.0.0). Profiles are the notable exception: public alpha since March 2026, collector support from v0.148.0, an official eBPF profiler collector distribution — and an explicit warning from the project not to use it for critical production workloads. On semantic conventions, the surface stabilised in stages: HTTP since 1.23.0 (November 2023), database since 1.33.0 (2025), with the July 2026 cut at 1.43.0; GenAI conventions are still Development and were moved out into their own repository around 1.42.0, so anything gen_ai.* should be treated as unstable and subject to rename. The spec itself is at 1.60.0 (2026-08-07), which added Entity support to the Resource SDK, with entity events having shipped in 1.58.0 (2026-06-22).

seniorWhat happens to your telemetry when the backend is down for twenty minutes?

Answer

By default you lose it, and the interviewer wants to hear that you know that. The exporter has a sending queue and a retry policy with exponential backoff; when the backend is unavailable, the queue fills, and once full new data is dropped — which is correct behaviour, because the alternative is unbounded memory growth and taking your application down to protect your telemetry. To survive a twenty-minute outage you have to make three decisions: queue size (how much data you are willing to hold), persistence (a file-backed queue on the collector survives a restart, an in-memory one does not), and back-pressure policy — and here the important rule is that telemetry must never block the application. The memory_limiter processor is the safety valve that starts refusing data before the collector OOMs. Separately, the application-side batch processor has its own bounded queue; when the local collector is unreachable, applications should drop rather than buffer, because a service that stalls on telemetry export is a much worse outage than a gap in your traces.

Example

exporters:
  otlp/backend:
    endpoint: backend:4317
    sending_queue:
      enabled: true
      queue_size: 100000
      storage: file_storage/otc      # survives a collector restart
    retry_on_failure:
      enabled: true
      initial_interval: 5s
      max_interval: 30s
      max_elapsed_time: 1800s        # keep retrying for 30 minutes
extensions:
  file_storage/otc: { directory: /var/lib/otelcol }

Follow-ups

  • How do you know you dropped data? — The collector's own internal metrics: otelcol_exporter_send_failed_spans, queue size and capacity, refused counts. Monitor the collector like any other service — and with a different backend if you can.
  • Should you ever block the application? — Practically never. A telemetry outage becoming a service outage is the classic self-inflicted incident.
  • What about ordering and duplicates on retry? — Retries can duplicate; backends deduplicate on span ID, and metrics with cumulative temporality are idempotent, which is another argument for cumulative.

Common wrong answer

"The SDK buffers until the backend comes back." Only up to a bounded queue, and the default is small. Assuming infinite buffering is how teams discover — during a backend outage — that either they lost everything or their pods started OOMing.

seniorYou are migrating 200 services from a proprietary agent to OpenTelemetry. How?

Answer

Insert the collector first, while everything else stays the same: point the existing agents at a collector that forwards to the incumbent backend unchanged. Nothing changes for users, but you now own the seam. Then dual-emit: run OTel instrumentation alongside the existing agent on a small set of services, export both streams, and compare — span counts, latency distributions, error rates — until you can show they agree; this comparison is the entire risk-reduction of the migration and skipping it is why migrations get rolled back. Then move service by service, starting with ones that are low-risk and well understood, using auto-instrumentation so most services need no code change; the collector rewrites attribute names so dashboards and alerts built on the old naming keep working during the transition. Port dashboards and alerts explicitly as a tracked work item — that is usually the largest and least glamorous part — and only then remove the old agent. Expect the tail: a handful of services with custom instrumentation or unsupported runtimes will take as long as the other 190.

Example

# the transition period: one input, two backends, names normalised on the way
processors:
  transform/compat:
    metric_statements:
      - set(name, "api_latency_seconds") where name == "http.server.request.duration"
exporters:
  otlp/new: { endpoint: new-backend:4317 }
  datadog/old: { api: { key: "${env:DD_API_KEY}" } }
service:
  pipelines:
    traces:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [batch]
      exporters: [otlp/new, datadog/old]

Follow-ups

  • What does dual-emit cost? — Roughly double ingestion for the overlap window, which needs to be budgeted and time-boxed. Say the number out loud; unbounded overlap is how migrations become permanent.
  • Biggest hidden cost? — Dashboards, alerts and runbooks referencing old metric names. Inventory them at the start, not at the end.
  • What is the exit criterion per service? — Its SLO alerts fire correctly from the new data, its traces are complete end to end, and its dashboards are ported. Then remove the old agent.

Common wrong answer

"Swap the agent for the OTel SDK service by service and cut over." Without the collector seam and a dual-emit comparison you get a period where nobody trusts either dataset, alerts referencing missing metric names, and the first incident during the migration ends it permanently.

principalYour CTO asks whether to standardise on one vendor or stay vendor-neutral. What do you say?

Answer

Separate the two decisions: how telemetry is produced and shipped, and where it is stored and queried. Production and shipping should be vendor-neutral without hesitation — OTel instrumentation and your own collector — because that is what makes the storage decision reversible, and the marginal cost is close to zero now that vendors accept OTLP natively. Storage and query is where you should be willing to buy: running a highly available metrics, trace and log platform at scale is a real team's full-time job, and unless observability is your product, that team is better spent elsewhere. So the recommendation is usually: OTel plus collector everywhere, one primary commercial backend for the good UX and correlation, and a credible exit (the collector can route to an OSS stack, and you keep the ability to dual-export). Then name the real risks: cost growth that scales with traffic rather than value, per-seat pricing that discourages exactly the broad access you want, and proprietary query languages that lock in your dashboards and your team's skills more effectively than the data format ever does.

LayerRecommendationWhy
InstrumentationOpenTelemetry, alwaysReversible; no code change to switch backend
ShippingOwn the collectorRouting, redaction, sampling, cost control as config
Storage/UIBuy, usuallyRunning it well is a full-time team
Dashboards/queriesPortable where cheapThe real lock-in, more than the data

  • When would you self-host? — Extreme volume where the bill exceeds a team's cost, hard data-residency constraints, or observability being core to your product.
  • How do you keep the exit credible? — Rehearse it: route one signal to an alternative backend and keep a small set of ported dashboards working. An untested exit is not an exit.
  • What about multi-vendor by signal? — Common (metrics in one, traces in another) and workable via the collector, but it costs correlation, which is the main thing you are buying.

Common wrong answer

"Stay fully vendor-neutral and self-host everything to avoid lock-in." Self-hosting the storage layer at scale is a team of engineers and a permanent operational burden, and it trades vendor lock-in for a bespoke platform that is much harder to leave. Neutral at the instrumentation and shipping layer, pragmatic at the storage layer, is the answer that survives scrutiny.

09Latency and performance debugging

Latency questions separate people who read dashboards from people who understand queues. Interviewers probe percentile literacy first, then queueing intuition (why a service falls over at 80% utilisation, not 100%), then the specifically distributed effects — tail amplification across fan-out — and finally where latency actually comes from in a real stack: the database, the runtime, and the connection pool. This is a senior-heavy unit; at screener level it rarely goes past "why not use the average".

midWhy do you report percentiles instead of averages, and which percentile?

Answer

Because latency distributions are long-tailed and multi-modal, so the mean describes almost nobody: a service with a 50 ms cache hit path and a 3 s timeout path can average 200 ms while no single request ever takes 200 ms. Percentiles describe experience — p50 is the typical request, p95 and p99 are what your unhappy users get, and p99.9 is where the systemic problems (GC pauses, retries, a bad instance, lock convoys) hide. Which one you care about depends on the question: p50 for "does this feel fast", p99 for "how bad is the bad case", and for a page composed of twenty backend calls the relevant number is much further out than you expect — with twenty independent calls, a p99 per call means roughly one in five page loads hits a p99. And percentiles are always computed over a window and a population, so state them: "p99 over 5 minutes, per route" is a measurement; "our p99" is not.

Example

10,000 requests: 9,500 served from cache at 20 ms, 500 timing out at 3,000 ms
  mean   = 169 ms   <- describes no request that ever happened
  p50    =  20 ms
  p95    =  20 ms   <- still fine!
  p99    = 3000 ms  <- the actual story
  p99.9  = 3000 ms

Follow-ups

  • Can you average percentiles across services or windows? — No. Re-aggregate the underlying histogram buckets and recompute; averaging quantiles is a mathematically meaningless operation that appears on thousands of dashboards.
  • How much data do you need for a p99.9? — At least a few thousand events in the window, or the estimate is dominated by noise. At low traffic, widen the window or drop to p95.
  • What about per-user experience? — Look at the worst request per session; a user who hits the tail three times in one journey experiences a broken product even at a healthy fleet p99.

Common wrong answer

"Our average latency is 180 ms, which is well within target." Averages hide precisely the failure users report, and quoting one in response to a complaint is the single most common way to lose credibility in a performance discussion. Lead with the distribution.

midA service is at 80% CPU and latency has gone through the roof. Why?

Answer

Queueing. In any system with variable arrival and service times, waiting time grows non-linearly with utilisation — roughly proportional to 1/(1−ρ) — so going from 50% to 80% utilisation roughly triples the queue wait, and from 80% to 90% doubles it again. That is why the knee is real and why "we still have 20% headroom" is a misreading: the last 20% of capacity buys you a small amount of throughput at an enormous latency cost. The related tool is Little's Law, L = λW: concurrency equals arrival rate times latency, which lets you check any two numbers against the third — if you see 500 concurrent requests at 2,000 rps, average latency is 250 ms whether or not your dashboard says so. Practically: target 60-70% utilisation for latency-sensitive services, alert on saturation (queue depth, thread-pool wait, connection-pool wait) rather than on CPU, and remember that "CPU" here is a stand-in — the same maths governs thread pools, connection pools, disks and locks.

Example

relative queue wait, M/M/1 approximation  W ∝ 1/(1-ρ)
  ρ = 0.50  ->  2×
  ρ = 0.70  ->  3.3×
  ρ = 0.80  ->  5×
  ρ = 0.90  ->  10×
  ρ = 0.95  ->  20×      the "we have headroom" illusion

Little's Law: L = λ × W
  2,000 rps × 0.25 s = 500 concurrent requests in flight

Follow-ups

  • What should you alert on instead of CPU? — Saturation signals: run-queue length, thread-pool queue depth and wait time, connection-pool wait, and request queue time before handling starts.
  • Does more concurrency help? — Only if the bottleneck is not the shared resource. Raising thread counts against a saturated database moves the queue, deepens it, and makes latency worse.
  • Where does this bite hardest? — Connection pools, because they are small and invisible: a pool of 10 saturates long before CPU does, and the symptom is pure wait time with an idle-looking process.

Common wrong answer

"80% CPU is fine, we have headroom — it must be the network." It is the intuition from capacity planning applied to a latency problem, and it is why services are routinely run into the queueing knee. Naming 1/(1−ρ) and the saturation-versus-utilisation distinction is what makes this answer land.

midHere is a trace. How do you find where the latency actually went?

Answer

Work the critical path, not the biggest span. Start at the root duration, then find the child spans that are on the path — for parallel children only the longest matters, so a 400 ms call running concurrently with a 900 ms one contributes nothing. Then look for the two things traces reveal that nothing else does: unaccounted time — a gap between the parent's duration and the sum of its children's, which means time spent in the service itself, in queueing before handling, in serialisation, in GC, or waiting for a connection — and serialisation, where calls that could run in parallel are chained. After that check for repetition (twenty near-identical database spans is an N+1), for retries (the same operation appearing several times with errors), and for a child whose duration is suspiciously close to a timeout value, which means you are measuring a timeout, not work. If the span tree looks fine but the root is slow, the time is in the gaps and you need finer instrumentation or a profile.

Example

POST /checkout                       2,317 ms
├─ auth.verify                          12 ms
├─ inventory.reserve                   120 ms   ┐ these two are sequential
├─ pricing.calculate                   180 ms   ┘ but could be parallel  (-120 ms)
├─ payments.authorize                1,840 ms
│  └─ db.connection.acquire          1,790 ms   <-- pool exhaustion, not the provider
└─ (unaccounted)                       165 ms   <-- GC? serialisation? queue wait?

Follow-ups

  • What does a large unaccounted gap usually mean? — Time before the handler ran (queueing at the server), garbage collection, TLS handshakes, or work in an uninstrumented library. A profile at that moment settles it.
  • How do you tell a slow dependency from a slow network? — Compare the client span's duration with the server span's duration for the same call; the difference is network plus queueing on both sides.
  • What if the trace looks fine but users are slow? — You are measuring from the wrong end: check RUM and the gateway, since server-side traces exclude DNS, TLS, CDN and client render.

Common wrong answer

"The biggest span is the problem, so optimise that." Not if it is off the critical path, not if it is a parent whose time is really its child's, and not if it is a timeout rather than work. Reading a trace means reading the critical path and the gaps.

seniorEvery backend has a p99 of 100 ms, yet your page is slow. Explain.

Answer

Tail latency amplification. If a request fans out to N services and must wait for all of them, the probability that none hits the tail falls exponentially: with 100 parallel calls each having a 1% chance of exceeding 100 ms, roughly 63% of page loads contain at least one slow call, so the user-perceived p50 is close to the backend p99. This is why per-service percentiles are a trap in fan-out architectures and why the fix is rarely "make the backends faster" — you have to attack the tail specifically. The standard tools: hedged requests (send a second copy after the p95 deadline and take the first response, which costs a few percent extra load and can cut the tail dramatically), aggressive per-call deadlines with partial results rather than waiting for stragglers, reducing fan-out width, and micro-partitioning plus load-aware routing so a single slow replica does not appear in every request. Also check the trivial cause first: the calls may be sequential rather than parallel, in which case the latency is a sum, not a maximum.

Example

P(at least one slow call) = 1 - (1 - p)^N     with p = 0.01 (per-call p99)

  N =   1   ->   1.0%
  N =  10   ->   9.6%
  N =  50   ->  39.5%
  N = 100   ->  63.4%    <- the "all our services are fast" outage

Follow-ups

  • What does a hedged request cost? — Extra load equal to the hedge rate (send after p95 → about 5% more requests), which is usually a bargain; but never hedge non-idempotent operations, and cancel the loser.
  • What is the alternative to waiting for all N? — Deadline plus degraded response: render without the recommendations block rather than making the whole page wait for it.
  • How do you see this in telemetry? — Compare the root span duration distribution with the max-child distribution; if the root tracks the max of many children, you have amplification.

Common wrong answer

"Each service is within its SLO, so the problem must be the front end." Every component can meet its objective while the composition fails — that is the whole point of the arithmetic above. Interviewers ask this question specifically to see whether you reason about composition rather than per-service compliance.

seniorWhat is coordinated omission, and why might your load test be lying?

Answer

Coordinated omission is the measurement error you get when your load generator waits for a response before sending the next request. When the system stalls for two seconds, a closed-loop client simply does not send the requests it would have sent during that stall, so the stall is recorded as one slow sample instead of the thousands of delayed requests real users would have experienced. The result is a latency distribution that systematically under-reports the tail, often by an order of magnitude, and it is why load tests routinely show a healthy p99 for a system that visibly hangs in production. The fixes: use an open-loop generator that sends at a fixed rate regardless of responses, or correct the results by back-filling the requests that should have been issued during a stall (what HdrHistogram's "record with expected interval" does). The same bias exists in production monitoring wherever a client retries or a queue absorbs the delay — always ask whether your measurement starts when the user wanted the thing or when your server started working on it.

Example

Target 1,000 rps for 10 s. The server stalls for 2 s.

closed loop  : during the stall the client sends nothing.
               recorded: ~8,000 fast requests + a handful at ~2 s  -> p99 looks fine
open loop    : 2,000 requests pile up and are served late.
               recorded: 2,000 samples between 0 and 2 s  -> p99 ≈ 1.9 s  (the truth)

Follow-ups

  • Where does this show up in production? — Anywhere a queue hides the wait: measure from enqueue (or from the client's request start), not from when your handler began executing.
  • Which tools handle it? — Open-loop generators (wrk2, k6 with constant-arrival-rate, Gatling's open model) and HdrHistogram's expected-interval correction.
  • How does this relate to server-side metrics? — Server-side latency excludes queueing at the load balancer and in the accept backlog, which is coordinated omission by another name.

Common wrong answer

"We load tested at 2× peak and the p99 was fine, so we have headroom." With a closed-loop tool that statement is close to meaningless, and it is exactly the reasoning that precedes a capacity incident. Being able to name coordinated omission is a strong senior signal.

seniorLatency spikes every few minutes with flat traffic. What do you suspect?

Answer

Periodicity with flat load points at something on a schedule rather than at load. The usual suspects, in rough order of frequency: garbage collection — a full or compacting GC pause that scales with heap size, visible as a latency spike correlated with a drop in allocation rate and a jump in pause-time counters; a cache expiring in bulk, so many keys go cold simultaneously and stampede the origin (fix with jittered TTLs and request coalescing); a scheduled job — backup, index rebuild, log rotation, checkpoint, metrics scrape of an expensive endpoint — competing for the same disk or CPU; token or certificate refresh; connection-pool recycling or DNS TTL expiry causing a burst of reconnects and TLS handshakes; and in containers, CPU throttling from CFS quota, which produces very regular stalls with CPU usage that looks well under the limit. The diagnostic is to overlay the spike times on runtime counters (GC pauses, throttled periods), on the cron schedule, and on cache-miss rate — the period itself is usually a fingerprint: 5 minutes smells like a scrape or a TTL, 60 minutes like a job, 24 hours like a rotation.

Example

# container CPU throttling — the most under-diagnosed periodic stall
rate(container_cpu_cfs_throttled_seconds_total[5m]) > 0

# GC pause correlation (.NET)
rate(dotnet_gc_pause_time_seconds_total[1m])
# cache stampede: miss rate spiking on a fixed period
rate(cache_misses_total[1m]) / rate(cache_requests_total[1m])

Follow-ups

  • Why does CFS throttling look like idle CPU? — The average over the scrape interval is low, but within each 100 ms period the container exhausts its quota and is stopped. You must look at the throttled-periods counter, not utilisation.
  • How do you confirm GC? — Runtime counters plus a pause histogram; if spikes align with pauses and heap size, it is GC. Then it is an allocation problem, not a latency problem.
  • How do you fix a cache stampede? — Jitter the TTLs, coalesce concurrent misses into one origin request, and serve stale while revalidating.

Common wrong answer

"Must be a network blip." It is the default explanation for anything periodic and it is almost never right — networks fail irregularly, schedulers and TTLs fail on a period. The period is the strongest clue you have; use it.

seniorHow do you tell a slow query from pool exhaustion from lock contention?

Answer

Compare three durations that most teams only measure as one. Time to acquire a connection from the pool, time the database spends executing, and total client-observed time. If acquire time dominates, it is pool exhaustion — the database may be perfectly healthy and the fix is pool size, query duration, or fewer concurrent callers, not a bigger database. If execution time dominates and the query plan is stable, look at data volume, missing indexes and parameter sniffing; if execution time is bimodal — fast most of the time, occasionally very slow — suspect lock or latch contention and check the database's own wait statistics and blocking-session views. Trace spans give you the shape (many identical spans is an N+1, one long span is a genuine slow query), while the database's wait events tell you what it was waiting on. The instrumentation lesson to state: emit connection-pool acquire time as its own span or metric, because without it every pool problem looks like a slow database.

Example

span: db.query SELECT ...            1,790 ms
  ├─ db.connection.acquire           1,740 ms   <-- pool exhaustion
  └─ db.execute                         50 ms   <-- the database is fine

vs.

span: db.query SELECT ...            1,790 ms
  ├─ db.connection.acquire               2 ms
  └─ db.execute                      1,788 ms   <-- now look at the plan / waits

Follow-ups

  • Why is raising the pool size often wrong? — It pushes the queue into the database, where contention is worse and shared across every client. Fix the query or the concurrency first.
  • How do you spot an N+1 from telemetry? — A parent span with dozens or hundreds of near-identical short child spans; the total is large, each is trivial.
  • What about the ORM? — Check for lazy loading, unbounded result sets and missing pagination. Also check that the ORM's own instrumentation records the statement text (parameterised, never with values).

Common wrong answer

"The database is slow, we should scale it up." The most expensive wrong answer available: in a majority of these cases the database is idle and the wait is entirely in the client's connection pool. Interviewers ask this because the fix and the bill differ by an order of magnitude depending on which of the three it is.

principalHow do you set and enforce a latency budget across a request path?

Answer

Start from the user-facing target and decompose it, leaving margin. If the journey target is 500 ms at p99, allocate it across the hops — gateway 20 ms, checkout 80 ms, payments 200 ms, database 60 ms — and keep 20-30% unallocated for network, retries and variance, because a budget that sums exactly to the target is already broken. Each service owns its slice as its own latency SLO, and the allocation is a negotiation, not a decree: a team that cannot meet its slice either gets more budget (taken from somewhere) or the path is redesigned — made parallel, cached, or made asynchronous. Enforce it mechanically with deadline propagation: the caller passes the remaining time budget downstream so a service that receives 30 ms does not start a 200 ms operation, and everything below it fails fast rather than doing work whose result nobody will wait for. Monitor the decomposition continuously — per-hop p99 against its allocation, from span data — so a regression is attributed to a service rather than argued about, and review the allocations whenever the path changes.

Example

journey target: 500 ms p99 (checkout)
  gateway + TLS + routing         20 ms
  checkout own work               80 ms
  inventory (parallel)           120 ms  ┐ max, not sum
  pricing   (parallel)           100 ms  ┘
  payments                       200 ms
  ---------------------------------------
  allocated                      420 ms
  reserve (network, retries)      80 ms   (16%)

deadline propagation: gateway sends grpc-timeout / traceparent + budget;
each hop subtracts elapsed time and refuses work it cannot finish in time.

Follow-ups

  • What happens when a service exceeds its budget? — Same as an SLO breach: it is visible, owned, and either fixed or renegotiated. The point is that the conversation has a number in it.
  • How do retries fit in the budget? — They must fit inside it: a retry with the original timeout doubles the worst case. Retry only within the remaining deadline.
  • Where does deadline propagation break? — At async boundaries and at any hop that ignores the incoming deadline. Both are worth a contract test.

Common wrong answer

"Each service has its own latency SLO, so the total takes care of itself." It does not: independent per-service targets sum (or amplify) into a journey latency nobody owns, and the first regression becomes a cross-team argument with no shared number. Budget the journey, decompose it, and propagate deadlines.

10Distributed failure modes

This unit is where observability meets distributed systems: the failure patterns you must be able to recognise from telemetry before you can fix them. Interviewers usually deliver these as symptoms — "the dependency recovered but our service did not", "everything is timing out but nothing is broken" — and score whether you name the mechanism. Almost all of it is senior-level; a mid-level candidate is expected to know retries and timeouts, not metastable failure.

midWhy do retries make outages worse, and how do you retry safely?

Answer

Because retries multiply load exactly when the system is least able to serve it: a dependency slows down, every caller retries three times, offered load triples, the dependency gets slower, and the loop closes. It compounds through layers — three layers each retrying three times is 27× amplification at the bottom — and synchronised retries produce a thundering herd that keeps the dependency down even after the original trigger is gone. Safe retries need four things: exponential backoff with jitter, so clients spread out instead of arriving together; a cap on attempts, applied at one layer only, not at every layer; a retry budget — retry only while retries are under a few percent of total requests, which automatically stops retrying during a real outage; and idempotency, because retrying a non-idempotent operation is a correctness bug, not a resilience feature. And retries must live inside the remaining deadline, or the worst-case latency multiplies with the attempt count.

Example

// backoff with full jitter, capped attempts, inside the caller's deadline
var delay = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(
    Random.Shared.Next(0, (int)Math.Min(maxBackoffMs, baseMs * Math.Pow(2, attempt))));

// retry budget: stop retrying when retries exceed 10% of traffic
if (retryRate.Value > 0.10) throw new RetriesBudgetExhaustedException();

Follow-ups

  • Which layer should own retries? — One, usually the outermost that can still make a meaningful decision. Retries at every layer is the amplification bug.
  • How do you see amplification in telemetry? — Downstream request rate rising while upstream request rate is flat; and a retry counter, which every client should emit.
  • Which errors are retryable? — Timeouts, 429 and 503 with backoff, connection failures. Never 4xx (except 429), and never a non-idempotent write without an idempotency key.

Common wrong answer

"We retry three times with a one-second delay, which handles transient failures." Fixed delays synchronise clients into a herd, three layers of it is 27× load, and there is no budget to stop it during a real outage. Jitter and a retry budget are the two words interviewers are waiting for.

midTimeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads — what does each protect against?

Answer

Timeouts bound how long you wait, which is what stops a slow dependency from consuming your threads and turning their problem into your outage; every network call must have one, and it must be shorter than your caller's remaining deadline. Circuit breakers stop you from calling a dependency that is clearly failing: after a failure threshold they open, fail fast for a cooldown, then let a trial request through — this protects both sides, because it removes the load that keeps the dependency down and it removes the latency that would otherwise pile up in you. Bulkheads partition resources so one dependency's failure cannot exhaust everything: separate connection pools or concurrency limits per downstream, so a hung recommendations service cannot consume every thread and take checkout down with it. The observability requirement is the part candidates forget: each of these must emit — timeout counts, breaker state transitions, and per-bulkhead rejections — or during an incident you cannot tell whether you are seeing the failure or the protection working.

Example

timeout      100 ms per call, < the 400 ms remaining deadline
breaker      open after 50% failures over 20 requests; half-open after 10 s
bulkhead     max 20 concurrent calls to recommendations (of 200 total threads)

emit:  calls_timed_out_total, breaker_state{state="open"},
       bulkhead_rejected_total  -> otherwise the mitigation is invisible

Follow-ups

  • How do you choose a timeout value? — From the dependency's measured p99 plus margin, not from a round number; and always less than the remaining budget you were given.
  • What is the risk of a circuit breaker? — Opening on a transient blip and denying a healthy dependency, or a breaker per instance that never sees enough traffic to make a good decision.
  • What does an open breaker look like to users? — Fast failure, which is better than slow failure, but it should be paired with a fallback or a degraded response wherever possible.

Common wrong answer

"We use the default timeout from the HTTP client." Many clients default to 100 seconds or to no timeout at all, which means one slow dependency parks every thread you have. Interviewers ask because "no explicit timeout" is the single most common cause of an outage spreading beyond its origin.

seniorWhat is a metastable failure, and why did the service not recover when the dependency did?

Answer

A metastable failure is a state the system falls into under a trigger and then sustains by itself after the trigger is gone, because the failure mode generates its own load. The classic sequence: a brief dependency blip causes timeouts, timeouts cause retries, retries triple the load, the queue fills, everything now times out, and every request is retried — so when the dependency recovers it is immediately hit with three times normal traffic plus a backlog, and it fails again. The system has two stable states and it is stuck in the bad one. Other engines for it: a cold cache (the cache emptied, so every request goes to the origin, which is too slow to refill it), queue backlog with timeouts (you spend all capacity serving requests whose clients already gave up), and connection storms after a mass reconnect. The signature in telemetry is a sustained high load with no external cause and a recovery that does not happen when the trigger clears. The exit is almost always to reduce load — shed traffic, drain the queue, open circuit breakers, scale up first and let traffic back gradually — not to wait.

Example

trigger  : 30 s of database slowness
loop     : timeouts -> retries -> 3× load -> queue full -> all requests time out
                    ^                                                   |
                    +---------------------------------------------------+
database recovers at t+30s ... service does NOT recover.

exit: shed 80% of traffic (or stop the world), let queues drain,
      then ramp traffic back over minutes. Scaling alone often does not break it.

Follow-ups

  • Why doesn't scaling up fix it? — More capacity also means more concurrent retries at the still-saturated shared resource; without shedding, you often just amplify faster.
  • How do you make it unlikely? — Retry budgets, load shedding with admission control, deadline propagation so doomed work is dropped early, and bounded queues that reject rather than grow.
  • How do you recognise it live? — Offered load high, upstream user traffic normal, retry ratio very high, and the original trigger no longer present.

Common wrong answer

"We'd scale up until it recovers." Sometimes it works, often it feeds the loop and turns a five-minute dependency blip into an hour-long outage. The move interviewers look for is shedding load first and ramping back gradually.

seniorWhat is a gray failure and why is it the hardest kind to detect?

Answer

A gray failure is a component that is degraded but not down: it passes health checks, it answers, it looks alive to the systems that decide whether to route to it, but it is serving errors, serving slowly, or serving wrong data for some subset of requests. It is the hardest kind to detect because your automation is built on binary liveness — a health check that returns 200, a heartbeat that arrives — and there is a differential observability gap: the component's own view of itself ("I am fine") disagrees with its clients' view ("this one is timing out"). The detection strategy is therefore to trust the clients: measure success and latency per downstream instance from the caller's side, compare instances against their peers, and eject outliers automatically (outlier detection in a mesh, or client-side load balancing that de-prioritises slow endpoints). Make health checks meaningful rather than trivial — check dependencies, pool availability and the ability to do actual work, not just that the process is running — and split liveness (restart me) from readiness (send me traffic), because conflating them either kills healthy processes or keeps sending traffic to broken ones.

Example

# the client's view: is one instance materially worse than its peers?
(
  sum by (upstream_instance) (rate(client_requests_total{status=~"5..|timeout"}[5m]))
  /
  sum by (upstream_instance) (rate(client_requests_total[5m]))
) > 5 *
scalar(sum(rate(client_requests_total{status=~"5..|timeout"}[5m]))
       / sum(rate(client_requests_total[5m])))

Follow-ups

  • Why not just make health checks stricter? — Deep health checks can cascade: one dependency blip marks every instance unhealthy at once and you lose the entire service. Keep readiness shallow-ish and rely on client-side outlier ejection.
  • What are common gray failures? — A wedged thread pool, an exhausted connection pool, a full disk, a corrupted local cache, a NIC dropping packets, a node with a failing disk.
  • How does a service mesh help? — Outlier detection and per-endpoint success metrics for free, without every client implementing it.

Common wrong answer

"Our health checks would catch it." Health checks are exactly what gray failures pass — that is the definition. Answering with health checks tells the interviewer you have not met one; answering with client-side per-instance success rates tells them you have.

seniorTimestamps across services disagree. How much do you trust them, and what do you use instead?

Answer

Trust them for coarse ordering, never for fine causality. NTP-synced hosts are typically within a few milliseconds but can drift much further under load or with a bad NTP source, and virtualised clocks can jump; so a span that appears to start before its parent, or a child that appears to end after it, is usually clock skew rather than a bug — and every tracing backend has to cope with negative-duration artefacts for this reason. For causality, use the structure you already have: parent/span IDs express happens-before regardless of clocks, and sequence numbers or logical clocks do the same within a service. For cross-service ordering during an incident, prefer relative measurements — the client span's duration minus the server span's duration is a network estimate that does not depend on the two clocks agreeing — and corroborate onset ordering with several signals rather than one timestamp. Operationally: monitor clock offset as a first-class metric, because skew silently corrupts trace waterfalls, log correlation, certificate validation and any TTL-based logic.

Example

# treat clock offset as a monitored signal, not an assumption
abs(node_timex_offset_seconds) > 0.05

# and remember: the network estimate does not depend on clock agreement
# network ≈ client_span.duration - server_span.duration

Follow-ups

  • What breaks silently under skew? — Trace waterfalls (negative gaps), log correlation by time, token and certificate validity, cache TTLs, and any "did A happen before B" query.
  • What about ordering within one service? — Monotonic clocks for durations, wall clock only for display. Measuring a duration with the wall clock is a real bug when NTP steps.
  • Do cloud providers help? — Yes: high-accuracy time services with microsecond-class synchronisation exist and are worth using where ordering matters.

Common wrong answer

"All our hosts run NTP so timestamps are accurate." NTP bounds drift, it does not eliminate it, and it fails silently when the source is wrong or the host is saturated. If your causal reasoning depends on millisecond agreement between two machines, it is already unreliable — use trace structure instead.

seniorHow do you shed load and degrade gracefully — and how do you observe that you did?

Answer

Decide in advance what you will drop and in what order, because during an incident there is no time to design it. Admission control at the edge with priority classes is the mechanism: reject or queue low-value traffic first — background sync, bulk exports, analytics, unauthenticated browsing — while protecting the revenue path and anything already in flight; return 429 with Retry-After so well-behaved clients back off instead of hammering. Degrade rather than fail wherever the product allows: serve stale cache, drop the personalisation block, disable expensive search facets, switch to a cheaper model or a static fallback. The observability requirement is that shedding must be loud: emit a counter of shed requests by priority class and reason, put the current degradation mode on a dashboard, and make it an explicit state in the incident channel — because a service quietly serving degraded results looks healthy on an availability SLI while customers experience a worse product. That is also why your SLI definition should count a degraded response as what it is rather than as success.

Example

priority classes (shed from the bottom):
  P0  checkout, payment callbacks, auth          never shed
  P1  logged-in browsing, search                 shed above 90% saturation
  P2  recommendations, personalisation           shed above 75%
  P3  bulk export, analytics, prefetch           shed above 60%

emit: shed_requests_total{class,reason}, degradation_mode{mode="no_recs"}
SLI : a degraded response is not counted as a fully good event

Follow-ups

  • Where should shedding happen? — As early as possible — edge or gateway — so rejected work costs almost nothing. Shedding after the expensive part has run is not shedding.
  • How do you avoid shedding the wrong traffic? — Classify by business value, not by endpoint volume, and test it: a game day where you shed P3 in production is the only way to know it works.
  • What is the relationship to the SLO? — Shedding spends error budget deliberately, in a chosen direction. That is the point: choose who gets the failure rather than letting saturation choose randomly.

Common wrong answer

"We autoscale, so we don't need to shed." Autoscaling takes minutes and cannot help when the bottleneck is a shared database, a licence limit, or a third party — and it is useless in a metastable state. Shedding is the control you have in seconds; scaling is the control you have in minutes.

seniorWhat is a thundering herd, and where does it show up besides caches?

Answer

A thundering herd is many clients doing the same thing at the same instant because something synchronised them. The cache version is the famous one — a popular key expires and a thousand concurrent requests all miss and all hit the origin — but the pattern recurs everywhere: every pod restarting at once after a deploy or a node drain, and all reconnecting and re-warming simultaneously; every client refreshing a token that has the same expiry; cron jobs scheduled at exactly midnight across a fleet; every consumer reconnecting after a broker failover; and every retry firing at the same fixed interval. The general cure is jitter plus coalescing: randomise expiries, TTLs, schedules and backoffs so clients spread out, and deduplicate concurrent identical work so only one caller goes to the origin while the rest wait for its result (single-flight). Add serve-stale-while-revalidate so an expiry never converts into a synchronous miss, and stagger restarts and reconnections deliberately. In telemetry the fingerprint is unmistakable: a very sharp, very periodic spike in origin traffic with no corresponding spike in user traffic.

Example

// jittered TTL: 10 minutes ±10%, so a million keys do not expire together
var ttl = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(10) * (0.9 + Random.Shared.NextDouble() * 0.2);

// single-flight: one origin call per key, everyone else awaits the same task
var value = await cache.GetOrCreateAsync(key, async _ => await origin.FetchAsync(key));

Follow-ups

  • Why does a deploy trigger one? — Every new pod starts with a cold cache, empty connection pools and no JIT warm-up, and they all start within seconds of each other. Stagger the rollout and pre-warm.
  • What is serve-stale? — Return the expired value immediately while refreshing in the background, so an expiry never blocks a user request.
  • How do you find one after the fact? — Origin request rate at fine resolution: herds appear as narrow spikes on a fixed period that do not exist in the user traffic.

Common wrong answer

"We'd increase the cache TTL." Longer TTLs make each herd rarer and bigger, and they make the stale-data problem worse. The fix is desynchronisation (jitter) and coalescing, not duration.

11Profiling and runtime forensics

Profiling comes up when the interviewer wants to know whether you can get from "this service is slow" to a line of code. It is asked less often than tracing but it discriminates strongly: most candidates have never taken a production profile, and the ones who have can describe the flame graph, the overhead and the safety procedure. Continuous profiling is also the newest part of the OpenTelemetry story, so it is a good place to demonstrate that you follow the project.

midHow does a sampling profiler work, and how do you read a flame graph?

Answer

A sampling profiler interrupts the process at a fixed frequency — typically 100 Hz, so every 10 ms — captures the stack of each running thread, and aggregates identical stacks. Cost is proportional to the sampling rate rather than to the number of function calls, which is why it is cheap enough for production, and the statistical nature is fine: with enough samples the proportions converge. An instrumenting profiler, by contrast, wraps every call and produces exact counts with overhead that can be 10× or worse — a development tool, not a production one. A flame graph plots those aggregated stacks: the x-axis is proportion of samples, not time, so width equals cost; the y-axis is stack depth, with callers below callees. You read it by scanning for wide frames near the top — a wide plateau is where the CPU actually is — and you ignore the left-to-right order entirely, since it is alphabetical, not chronological.

Example

width = share of samples (cost).  depth = stack.  left-right order = meaningless.

[========================== HandleRequest ==========================]  100%
[====== Deserialize ======][============ Query ============][ Render ]
                           [====== ToLower ======][ Execute ]
                           ^ 34% of all CPU in a string comparison
                             -> a case-insensitive lookup in a hot loop

Follow-ups

  • What is a differential flame graph? — Two profiles subtracted, so you see what got wider between a good and a bad version. It is the fastest way to attribute a regression to a deploy.
  • What does a very deep, thin tower mean? — Recursion or a deep framework stack; it is not necessarily a problem, since only width is cost.
  • What sampling frequency? — 99 or 100 Hz is standard; 99 avoids locking in step with timers that fire on round numbers.

Common wrong answer

"The flame graph shows the timeline of execution left to right." It shows aggregated stacks sorted alphabetically; there is no time axis at all (that is a flame chart). Reading it as a timeline leads people to conclude that early-alphabet functions run first, which produces confidently wrong analysis.

midMemory grows steadily until the pod is OOM-killed. How do you find the leak?

Answer

First establish that it is a leak rather than a cache, a buffer or a heap that simply has not been collected: watch memory after a forced collection, or compare live-set size across GCs — a leak shows a rising floor, whereas a cache shows a rising but bounded plateau. Then take two heap snapshots minutes apart on an affected instance and diff them: the type whose instance count grows monotonically is your candidate, and the retention path from a GC root tells you who is holding it. In managed runtimes the usual suspects are stable and worth naming: a static or singleton collection that only ever gets added to; event handlers or subscriptions never unsubscribed; a cache with no eviction policy or no size bound; timers and long-lived tasks capturing large object graphs; connection or HTTP client instances created per request; and unmanaged handles not disposed. Also distinguish leaks from fragmentation and from a heap that is simply configured larger than the container limit — a container that OOM-kills at 2 GB while the runtime thinks it may use 4 GB is a configuration bug, not a leak.

Example

# .NET: two snapshots, then diff by type and find the retention path
dotnet-gcdump collect -p 1 -o /tmp/a.gcdump
sleep 300
dotnet-gcdump collect -p 1 -o /tmp/b.gcdump

# Java equivalent
jcmd <pid> GC.heap_info
jcmd <pid> GC.class_histogram | head -20     # instance counts by class
# Go
curl -s localhost:6060/debug/pprof/heap > heap.out && go tool pprof heap.out

Follow-ups

  • How do you tell a leak from a big cache? — Bounded versus unbounded growth, and whether the retained objects are reachable from a cache root with an eviction policy.
  • What about native memory? — Managed heap flat while RSS grows means native: unmanaged buffers, native libraries, thread stacks, or memory-mapped files. Different tools entirely.
  • Is restarting on a schedule acceptable? — As a temporary mitigation with an owner and a date, yes. As a permanent answer it hides a defect that will get worse.

Common wrong answer

"Managed runtimes have a garbage collector, so it can't be a leak." Managed leaks are extremely common — they are reachability bugs, not free bugs — and the GC is doing exactly its job by keeping objects your static dictionary still references. Say "unintended retention" and go looking for the root.

seniorThe CPU profile is flat but the service is slow. What are you missing?

Answer

You are profiling on-CPU time when the problem is off-CPU: the threads are not burning cycles, they are waiting. A standard CPU profiler samples running threads, so a service blocked on a lock, on a socket read, on disk, or on a connection pool shows almost nothing — the wide frames are missing precisely because nothing is executing. The complements are off-CPU profiling (sampling threads while they are blocked and attributing the wait to the stack that blocked, usually via scheduler tracing with eBPF), thread dumps (a single snapshot of what every thread is waiting on, which is often enough), and wait-oriented instrumentation: connection-pool acquire time, lock contention counters, thread-pool queue length. The rule of thumb is that a slow service with low CPU is a waiting problem, and a slow service with high CPU is a work problem — the two need different tools, and reaching for a CPU profile in the first case is a common dead end.

Example

# off-CPU: where are threads blocked, and for how long?
sudo offcputime-bpfcc -p $(pgrep -n dotnet) 30

# cheaper first step: what is every thread doing right now?
jcmd <pid> Thread.print | grep -A5 "BLOCKED"
dotnet-stack report -p 1

Follow-ups

  • What does a thread dump show that a profile does not? — Blocked states and lock ownership: which thread holds the monitor everyone else is waiting for.
  • Where does async complicate this? — Async waits release the thread, so thread dumps show an idle pool while thousands of continuations wait. You need task/state-machine level instrumentation or async-aware tooling.
  • What if CPU is high but the profile is spread thin? — Suspect GC, JIT, TLS, serialisation, or a framework overhead spread across many frames; group by library rather than by function.

Common wrong answer

"The profile is flat, so the code is fine — it must be the network." Sometimes true, but "flat CPU profile" is the signature of blocking, and blocking on a local lock or a local pool looks identical from the outside. Off-CPU analysis or a thread dump tells you which, and skipping that step is how teams blame networks for lock contention.

seniorWould you run continuous profiling in production? What does it cost?

Answer

Yes, and it is one of the highest-value additions of the last few years, because it turns "we need to reproduce it while profiling" into "look at what the process was doing at 02:41". Continuous profiling samples every process at a low frequency all the time — typically low single-digit percent CPU overhead at 100 Hz, and eBPF-based whole-machine profilers add roughly 1% with no application change and no language runtime support — and stores the aggregated stacks so you can query them by time, service and version. The killer feature is differential comparison: this hour against last week, this version against the previous one, this pod against its peers. In OpenTelemetry, Profiles is a first-class signal with its own data model, collector support from v0.148.0, and an eBPF profiler shipped as an official collector distribution — but it entered public alpha in March 2026 and the project explicitly says not to use it for critical production workloads, so the honest recommendation today is a mature vendor or OSS profiler with an eye on OTel Profiles as the destination once it stabilises. Cost is storage and, mainly, symbolisation.

Example

questions continuous profiling answers that nothing else can:
  "CPU is 40% higher since Tuesday"        -> diff this week vs last, by function
  "which service burns the most CPU/$"     -> cost attribution by stack
  "what was it doing during the incident"  -> profile at 02:41, retrospectively
  "is the regression in our code or a lib" -> the widened frames name it

Follow-ups

  • What is the hardest operational part? — Symbolisation: stripped binaries and containers make stacks unreadable, so you need to keep debug symbols and match them by build ID.
  • Does eBPF profiling need application changes? — No, which is its main advantage; the trade-off is no application-level semantics and a kernel-version dependency.
  • Can profiles correlate with traces? — That is the point of making it an OTel signal: attaching profile samples to the span context so you can profile one endpoint. Expect it to mature over the next releases.

Common wrong answer

"Profiling is too expensive to run in production." True of instrumenting profilers, false of sampling ones for the last decade. This answer signals that the candidate's mental model of profiling is a dev-machine tool from 2010 — and it forfeits the one signal that could have answered the incident.

seniorA service is hung: no CPU, no throughput, process alive. Walk me through it.

Answer

Capture before you restart — a restart destroys the only evidence and the incident will recur. Take a thread dump (or two, thirty seconds apart, so you can tell a stuck thread from a slow one) and read the states: if many threads are BLOCKED on the same monitor, find the owner and see what it is waiting on; if all worker threads are in a socket read, you are blocked on a dependency with no timeout; if the pool is empty and the queue is long, you have exhausted a bounded resource. Check the classic deadlock shapes: two locks acquired in different orders; a thread-pool starvation deadlock where blocking on an async operation consumes every pool thread so the continuation can never be scheduled; a connection pool where a request holds a connection while waiting for another connection. Corroborate with socket state (a pile of CLOSE_WAIT means you are not closing what the peer already closed) and with the dependency's own health. Then restart to restore service — after the dump — and fix the missing timeout or the lock ordering, because a hang is almost always an unbounded wait somewhere.

Example

"http-worker-42"  BLOCKED on 0x00000007d5a1  owned by "http-worker-7"
"http-worker-7"   RUNNABLE  in SocketRead(...)   <-- holds the lock, waits forever
   ... 198 more threads BLOCKED on 0x00000007d5a1

diagnosis: a network call with no timeout, made while holding a shared lock.
fix: never block on I/O inside a lock; set an explicit timeout on every call.

Follow-ups

  • Why two thread dumps? — One tells you what threads are doing; two tell you what has not moved. Progress is the distinguishing evidence.
  • What is thread-pool starvation? — Blocking synchronously on async work: every pool thread waits for a continuation that needs a pool thread. Classic in .NET and Node, and it presents exactly as a hang.
  • What should you have had in place? — Timeouts everywhere, a saturation metric on every pool, and an automatic dump-on-hang hook so the evidence is captured without a human.

Common wrong answer

"Restart the pod and see if it comes back." It will come back, and it will hang again tonight with the evidence gone. Interviewers ask this precisely to hear "capture a thread dump first" — that one sentence is most of the score.

seniorWhat is your policy for taking dumps in production safely?

Answer

Treat a dump as a controlled operation with a defined procedure, because it can pause a process for seconds and produce a file containing every secret in memory. The procedure I would write: take it from one instance, removed from the load balancer first so the pause is invisible to users; know the cost (a full heap dump stops the world for roughly a second per gigabyte and writes a file of comparable size, so check disk space or you cause a second incident); write it to ephemeral storage, then move it to an access-controlled bucket with a short retention, because the dump contains credentials, tokens and customer data and must be handled at the sensitivity of the most sensitive thing the process touches; record who took it and why. Prefer the lightest artefact that answers the question — thread dump, then targeted counters, then a CPU profile, and only then a full heap dump. Automate the capture on trigger (on OOM, on a hang detector) so the evidence exists without someone being awake, and rehearse it, because a procedure first attempted at 3am is not a procedure.

Example

# 1. take the instance out of rotation (stop readiness, keep the process)
kubectl label pod checkout-7d9f drain=true

# 2. lightest artefact first
kubectl exec checkout-7d9f -- dotnet-stack report -p 1        # threads
kubectl exec checkout-7d9f -- dotnet-counters collect -p 1    # counters
# 3. only if needed, the heavy one
kubectl exec checkout-7d9f -- dotnet-dump collect -p 1 -o /tmp/core

# 4. move off the node into controlled storage; treat as secret material

Follow-ups

  • What if there is only one instance? — Then the pause is user-visible: decide explicitly whether the evidence is worth it, and say so in the incident channel rather than doing it quietly.
  • Why is a dump a security artefact? — It contains keys, tokens, session data and personal data in plaintext. Many organisations require the same handling as a production database export.
  • How do you capture on OOM automatically? — Runtime flags that write a dump on out-of-memory, plus a sidecar or init container that ships it somewhere durable before the pod is recycled.

Common wrong answer

"Just take a heap dump on the affected pod and download it." Two failures in one: the stop-the-world pause on a serving instance can itself cause an outage, and downloading a memory image full of credentials to a laptop is a data-handling incident. The procedure — drain, lightest-first, controlled storage — is the answer.

12Telemetry cost and pipelines

Once you interview for lead or staff roles, someone will ask about the bill — because observability is frequently among the largest infrastructure line items and it grows with traffic rather than with value. This unit is about knowing the cost drivers per signal, having a structured reduction plan that does not destroy debuggability, and operating the collector tier as a real distributed system with its own failure modes.

midWhat actually drives observability cost?

Answer

Different things per signal, which is why a single "reduce telemetry" instruction never works. Metrics cost by cardinality — number of active time series and their retention — and are almost insensitive to request volume, so one badly-labelled metric can cost more than an entire service's traces. Logs cost by volume: bytes ingested, indexed and stored, so cost scales linearly with traffic and with how chatty each request is. Traces cost by spans retained, which is span rate times sampling rate, so both instrumentation granularity and sampling policy matter. Profiles cost by sample rate times retention, and are usually small. On top of all of that sit two costs people forget: egress, which can rival ingest when telemetry crosses cloud regions or providers, and per-seat licensing, which perversely discourages the broad access that makes observability valuable. Knowing which lever moves which line is the whole answer — and it is why the first step of any reduction is a breakdown by signal and by team.

Example

SignalCost driverBiggest lever
MetricsActive series × retentionDrop high-cardinality attributes
LogsBytes ingested/indexedDelete the top templates; move fields to spans
TracesSpans retainedTail sampling; fewer trivial spans
ProfilesSample rate × retentionUsually not the problem
AllEgress, seatsCollect locally; avoid per-seat pricing

Follow-ups

  • Which signal is usually the biggest bill? — Logs by volume in most estates, but metrics cardinality is the one that surprises people, because one label change can multiply cost overnight.
  • Why does egress matter? — Cross-region or cross-cloud telemetry is charged per gigabyte both ways; a local collector that aggregates and compresses before shipping often pays for itself immediately.
  • What is the trap in per-seat pricing? — It puts a price on the exact behaviour you want (everyone querying), so teams stop looking. Negotiate it away or budget for it deliberately.

Common wrong answer

"Storage is cheap, so observability cost is mostly noise." Storage is the cheapest part; ingest, indexing, cardinality and query are where the money goes, and the bill routinely reaches seven figures at mid-size companies. This answer marks a candidate who has never been asked to own the number.

seniorCut the observability bill by 40% without losing the ability to debug. How?

Answer

Measure first, in one place: cost by signal, by team and by metric name or log template. The distribution is always brutally skewed, so the top ten items usually contain the 40%. Then work in order of harm: kill cardinality bombs (attributes with unbounded values) — usually the single biggest win and it costs no debuggability at all; delete the top log templates that narrate control flow, and move their fields onto spans; introduce tail sampling if you are head-sampling uniformly, so you keep 100% of errors and slow traces while dropping routine ones; tier retention rather than deleting (hot 7-14 days, warm object storage, cold archive) and precompute the handful of series that need long history; and drop trivial internal spans that no one has ever queried. Then make it stick: per-team budgets with visible attribution, a cardinality guard in the collector, and an alert on volume growth — because otherwise you will do this again in six months. What I would not do is a flat percentage cut across everything, because it removes the same fraction of the useful data as of the useless data.

Example

baseline               $100k/mo   logs 55  metrics 25  traces 18  other 2
1 cardinality bombs     -12k      3 metrics with user_id / raw path labels
2 top-10 log templates  -18k      control-flow narration, never queried
3 tail sampling         -8k       100% errors + slow, 1% baseline
4 retention tiering     -6k       7d hot / 90d warm / 1y archive
                       -------
                        -44k      44% reduction, debuggability unchanged
guard rails: per-team budget alerts, collector cardinality limiter, volume growth alert

Follow-ups

  • How do you prove debuggability did not suffer? — Replay recent incidents against the reduced dataset and check that each question is still answerable; and track time-to-localise before and after.
  • What do you never cut? — Error traces, error logs, audit and security records, and SLI series. Put them in a protected pipeline.
  • How do you handle the team that objects? — Show them their own bill and their own top templates. Cost attribution converts an argument into a prioritisation exercise.

Common wrong answer

"Reduce retention from 30 days to 7 days across the board." It is the easiest lever and one of the worst: you lose the ability to compare against last month, to investigate slow-burn regressions, and to answer questions about an incident two weeks old — for a saving that is usually smaller than fixing three cardinality bombs.

seniorHow do you scale and operate the collector tier itself?

Answer

Split it by statefulness, because that determines how you scale. The agent tier is stateless — receive, enrich, batch, forward — so it scales with the node count as a DaemonSet and needs a memory limiter and modest resources. The gateway tier is where stateful processors live (tail sampling, span-to-metrics, delta-to-cumulative conversion, deduplication), and stateful processors require that related data reaches the same instance, so you put a trace-ID-aware load-balancing exporter in front and scale carefully — adding replicas redistributes trace IDs and can drop in-flight decisions. Size on memory rather than CPU, since buffering dominates, and autoscale on queue depth and refused-data counters instead of on CPU. Then monitor the collector as a first-class service using its own internal metrics — accepted versus refused versus dropped per signal, queue size against capacity, export failures — and route those to a different backend from the one it feeds, so a backend outage does not blind you to the collector. The failure mode to name is silent drop: a collector that is refusing 20% of spans looks perfectly healthy from the outside.

Example

# the four collector numbers worth alerting on
rate(otelcol_receiver_refused_spans[5m]) > 0
rate(otelcol_exporter_send_failed_spans[5m]) > 0
otelcol_exporter_queue_size / otelcol_exporter_queue_capacity > 0.8
rate(otelcol_processor_dropped_metric_points[5m]) > 0

Follow-ups

  • What breaks when you scale the tail-sampling tier? — Rebalancing changes which instance owns a trace ID, so traces in flight during the change can be judged on partial data. Scale gradually and prefer over-provisioning.
  • Should the collector be in the request path? — Never synchronously. Applications export asynchronously and drop on failure; a collector outage must not become a service outage.
  • What did the recent releases change here? — Collector v1.66.0/v0.160.0 (2026-09-02) made byte-size batching in the exporter helper O(1) instead of O(n²), which matters directly for gateway throughput, and moved to Go 1.26.

Common wrong answer

"It's stateless, so we just autoscale it on CPU." True for agents, false for any gateway doing tail sampling or delta conversion — and CPU is the wrong signal in both tiers, since the binding constraint is memory and queue depth. Naming the stateful/stateless split is what the question is for.

seniorHow do you attribute telemetry cost to teams, and should you charge back?

Answer

Attribute always; charge back carefully. Attribution needs one thing to be reliable: an ownership attribute on every piece of telemetry — service.name mapped to a team via a service catalogue, ideally enforced at the collector so unattributed data is either rejected or lands in a visible "unowned" bucket that someone has to explain. Then produce a monthly breakdown by team and by signal, and — more useful than the total — the top contributors within each team, because that is what makes the number actionable. On chargeback: showback (you can see your bill) changes behaviour almost as much as chargeback (you pay it) and costs far less political capital; full chargeback works when teams have real budget authority, and backfires when they do not, because the only lever a team without budget has is to stop instrumenting. The failure to avoid is making cost the dominant incentive: a team that turns off tracing to hit a number has saved money and cost you an outage, so pair the budget with a reliability expectation and the error-budget policy.

Example

# enforce ownership at the choke point: no owner, no ingest
processors:
  transform/owner:
    metric_statements:
      - set(resource.attributes["team"], "unowned")
        where resource.attributes["team"] == nil
  filter/unowned:
    error_mode: ignore
    metrics:
      metric:
        - 'resource.attributes["team"] == "unowned"'   # or route to a quarantine pipeline

Follow-ups

  • What about shared infrastructure telemetry? — Allocate by usage where you can measure it, and hold the rest centrally as a platform cost. Do not spread it evenly; it makes every number unarguable-with and therefore ignored.
  • How do you stop cost pressure destroying instrumentation? — Protect a floor: errors, SLI series and audit data are not part of the budget conversation.
  • What is the fastest behavioural win? — A monthly email with the team's top five cost contributors and a one-line explanation of each. Teams fix what they can see.

Common wrong answer

"We give each team a hard quota and cut them off when they exceed it." Cutting off telemetry mid-month blinds you exactly when a team is having a bad month — which is when they most need it. Alert, attribute and negotiate; do not build an automatic self-blinding mechanism.

seniorHow do you manage collector configuration and transformations as code?

Answer

Treat the pipeline like production code, because it is: it can drop your telemetry, corrupt your metric names, or leak personal data. Configuration lives in version control, is reviewed, and is deployed through the same progressive rollout as any service — canary a subset of collectors, watch accepted/refused/dropped counters, then proceed. Transformations use OTTL (the OpenTelemetry Transformation Language) in the transform, filter and routing processors, which is expressive enough to rename, redact, drop and route — and expressive enough to break everything, so it needs tests: the collector can be run against a recorded corpus of telemetry with expected outputs, and that test belongs in CI. Structure the config so ownership is clear — a platform-owned base (memory limiter, resource detection, redaction, cardinality limits) plus team-scoped overlays — and keep the base non-overridable. For fleet management, OpAMP is the emerging standard for remote configuration and health reporting of collectors, which matters once you have thousands of agents and cannot redeploy them all to change a sampling rate.

Example

processors:
  transform/normalise:
    error_mode: ignore          # a bad statement must not drop the pipeline
    trace_statements:
      - set(span.name, "GET /orders/{id}")
        where span.attributes["http.route"] == "/orders/{id}"
      - delete_key(span.attributes, "http.request.header.authorization")
      - set(span.status.code, STATUS_CODE_ERROR)
        where span.attributes["http.response.status_code"] >= 500

Follow-ups

  • Why does error_mode matter? — With propagate, one malformed record can fail a whole batch. ignore keeps the pipeline running and is almost always what you want in production.
  • How do you test a config change? — Replay a captured corpus through the new config and diff the output; then canary on a small collector subset before fleet rollout.
  • What is OpAMP? — An open protocol for remotely configuring, updating and monitoring agents, with a supervisor shipped alongside the collector releases. The answer for fleet-wide config without redeploys.

Common wrong answer

"It's just YAML, we edit it when we need to." That YAML decides what telemetry exists; an unreviewed edit can silently drop a signal and nobody notices until the next incident. Version control, tests against a corpus, and a canary rollout are the expected answer.

principalDesign the telemetry storage strategy: what do you keep, where, and for how long?

Answer

Work from the questions each horizon has to answer. Zero to two weeks is incident forensics, so keep everything at full fidelity and make it fast to query — raw metrics, all retained traces, all logs; this tier is where you spend on performance. Two weeks to three months answers "is this a regression, and how did we behave last quarter" — downsampled metrics (5-minute), sampled traces, logs in object storage with a light index; performance can be seconds rather than milliseconds. Three months to two years is SLO history, capacity planning and compliance: only precomputed SLI series, aggregate metrics, and whatever audit and security records your policy requires — everything else expires. Beyond that, archive only what a regulator or a contract compels. Two rules make it work: decide what to keep by the question it answers, not by the signal it belongs to; and make the long-tail data cheap by precomputing it with recording rules rather than by retaining raw data you cannot afford to query. Finally, put the policy in code next to the pipeline config, because a retention policy that lives in a wiki is an aspiration.

  • How do you decide the hot window? — From how long your incidents take to investigate plus a margin; if postmortems routinely query data from three weeks ago, 14 days is wrong.
  • What is the risk of aggressive downsampling? — Losing the tail: a downsampled average cannot reconstruct a p99. Downsample histogram buckets, never computed quantiles.
  • Where do audit records live? — Not here. Separate pipeline, separate store, separate retention and access, as in unit 04.

Common wrong answer

"Keep everything for a year — storage is cheap and you never know." The storage is affordable; the index, the query cost and the cardinality are not, and "you never know" is not a retention policy. Interviewers want to see retention derived from the questions each tier must answer.

13Platform, Kubernetes and eBPF

Asked when the role is platform, SRE or infrastructure-adjacent, and skipped entirely in some application loops — which is why it is "sometimes / nice to know" rather than critical. What is tested is whether you can debug the layer below the application: why a pod is being killed, what a mesh can and cannot see, and what eBPF actually buys you beyond the marketing.

midWhat do you monitor in Kubernetes that you would not monitor on a VM?

Answer

The orchestration layer, because in Kubernetes the platform is an active participant in your outages. Beyond the usual node and container resource metrics (cAdvisor via the kubelet), you want kube-state-metrics for the declared versus actual state — desired versus ready replicas, pod phase and reason, deployment rollout status, PVC status — because the interesting failures are "the platform will not give me what I asked for". You want Kubernetes events, which carry the why (FailedScheduling with the reason, OOMKilled, ImagePullBackOff, evictions) and are the fastest path to diagnosis, yet are commonly not collected at all — they expire after an hour by default. You want control-plane health, resource quotas and limit ranges, and PodDisruptionBudgets. And you want the container-specific signals that have no VM equivalent: CPU throttling from CFS quota, memory working set against the limit, and OOM kill counts — all of which produce user-visible latency while the pod still looks "Running".

Example

# asked for versus got
kube_deployment_status_replicas_available < kube_deployment_spec_replicas

# the container signals with no VM analogue
rate(container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total[5m])
  / rate(container_cpu_cfs_periods_total[5m]) > 0.25
container_memory_working_set_bytes / container_spec_memory_limit_bytes > 0.9

Follow-ups

  • Why collect Kubernetes events? — They explain scheduling and lifecycle decisions and they are ephemeral; ship them into your log or trace store (the collector has a receiver for them) or the evidence is gone within the hour.
  • What is the most common invisible problem? — CPU throttling: average utilisation looks low while the container is stopped repeatedly inside each quota period, producing regular latency spikes.
  • What about requests versus limits? — Requests drive scheduling, limits drive throttling and OOM kills; mismatches between them are behind a large share of "the platform is slow" reports.

Common wrong answer

"CPU, memory and disk per pod — same as a VM, just smaller." That misses the entire orchestration layer, which is where most Kubernetes incidents actually live: scheduling failures, evictions, quota exhaustion, throttling and rollout stalls. None of them appear in pod resource graphs.

midA pod is in CrashLoopBackOff. Walk me through it.

Answer

Get the previous container's logs first — kubectl logs --previous — because the running container has just started and knows nothing; the crashed one has the stack trace. Then read the pod's status and events: the termination reason distinguishes the families. OOMKilled with exit 137 means the container exceeded its memory limit, so it is a limit or a leak, not an application bug per se. Exit code 1 or 2 with an application stack trace is a startup failure — bad configuration, a missing secret, a dependency unreachable at boot. A liveness probe failure means the app started but the probe disagrees, and the classic cause is a probe whose timeout is shorter than a slow cold start, which kills the pod repeatedly and looks exactly like a crash. ImagePullBackOff and CreateContainerConfigError are not crashes at all. The trap to name is the log-loss one: if the container writes to a file inside the container rather than stdout, the crash takes the evidence with it, which is why stdout plus a node-level collector is the default.

Example

kubectl logs checkout-7d9f --previous          # the crashed container's output
kubectl describe pod checkout-7d9f | tail -30  # events + last state + exit code
kubectl get events --field-selector involvedObject.name=checkout-7d9f

# exit 137 = 128 + SIGKILL(9) -> OOMKilled or a failed liveness probe
# exit 143 = 128 + SIGTERM(15) -> graceful shutdown, usually eviction or rollout

Follow-ups

  • Slow start being killed by liveness — how do you fix it? — A startup probe, or a longer initialDelaySeconds/failureThreshold. Liveness should test "is it wedged", not "is it ready".
  • OOMKilled with plenty of headroom on the node? — Limits are per container, not per node. Also check whether the runtime's heap sizing is aware of the cgroup limit.
  • How do you keep the logs of a crashed container? — Write to stdout and collect at the node; a file inside an ephemeral container disappears with it.

Common wrong answer

"Check the logs" — with plain kubectl logs. That gives you the logs of the container that just started, which is usually empty, and candidates then conclude "there is nothing in the logs". The --previous flag plus the exit code is the whole diagnosis for most crash loops.

seniorWhat does a service mesh give you observability-wise, and what can it never see?

Answer

A mesh sits in the network path — sidecar proxy or, increasingly, a node-level and L7 split — so it gives you uniform RED metrics for every service-to-service call, TCP-level connection stats, mutual-TLS status, per-endpoint success rates for outlier detection, and spans for each hop, all without any application change. That uniformity is the real value: consistent naming and coverage across languages and teams, including services nobody will ever instrument. What it cannot see is everything inside the process: which function is slow, why a request took 400 ms once it arrived, business attributes like tenant or plan, database calls (unless they go through the mesh, and they often do not), and anything about cache hits, GC or queueing inside the app. It also cannot propagate trace context by itself — the mesh can start and stop spans per hop, but if the application does not forward the incoming traceparent, the mesh's spans will not join into a single trace, which is the number-one disappointment when teams expect distributed tracing "for free".

Example

mesh sees      : hop latency, status, retries, mTLS, per-endpoint success, connections
mesh cannot see: in-process time, DB queries, cache hits, GC, business attributes
mesh requires  : the app to forward traceparent/b3 headers, or its spans stay disjoint

Follow-ups

  • What is the overhead? — A sidecar adds latency (typically low single-digit milliseconds per hop) and memory per pod; sidecar-less/ambient modes reduce it. Measure per hop and multiply by your fan-out.
  • Mesh telemetry or application telemetry? — Both, and reconcile them: the mesh gives coverage and consistency, the app gives semantics. Discrepancies between them are themselves informative.
  • Does a mesh remove the need for the collector? — No: it produces telemetry that still has to be processed, sampled and routed.

Common wrong answer

"Install a mesh and you get distributed tracing for free." You get per-hop spans for free; you get a connected trace only if the application forwards the context headers. Teams that skip that step end up with thousands of single-span traces and conclude tracing is useless.

seniorWhat does eBPF actually enable, and what are its limits?

Answer

eBPF runs verified, sandboxed programs in the kernel attached to hooks — syscalls, network events, scheduler events, tracepoints, and user-space probes — so you can observe what a process does without modifying it, restarting it, or having any language support. That gives you zero-code instrumentation: L4/L7 network flows and latency, syscall-level tracing, off-CPU analysis, and whole-machine continuous profiling with roughly 1% overhead — which is why the OpenTelemetry eBPF profiler ships as an official collector distribution and why the spec's 1.60.0 release (2026-08-07) includes thread-context work for it. The limits matter just as much: it needs a recent kernel and privileges that many managed platforms restrict; it sees syscalls and packets, not application semantics, so it cannot tell you the tenant, the business operation or which code path was chosen; encrypted traffic requires uprobes into TLS libraries, which is version-fragile; and correlating kernel events into request-level traces requires stitching that is imperfect. The honest framing is that eBPF is superb for coverage and for the layer below your code, and complementary to — not a replacement for — application instrumentation.

Example

# things you can answer with no application change at all
sudo execsnoop-bpfcc                 # every process exec, with arguments
sudo tcpretrans-bpfcc                # TCP retransmits: is it really the network?
sudo biolatency-bpfcc 10 1           # block I/O latency histogram
sudo offcputime-bpfcc -p <pid> 30    # where threads block, and for how long

Follow-ups

  • Where does eBPF beat application instrumentation? — Coverage of things you cannot change: third-party binaries, legacy services, and the kernel-side truth about network and disk.
  • What is the security posture? — Programs are verified and sandboxed, but loading them needs elevated capabilities; treat the agent as privileged infrastructure and control who can deploy programs.
  • Can it give you distributed traces? — Partially: it can generate spans for network calls and even propagate context in some implementations, but without application semantics the traces are structural rather than meaningful.

Common wrong answer

"eBPF replaces instrumentation — you get everything with no code changes." It gives you coverage without semantics. No kernel probe can tell you which tenant, which feature flag or which business operation a request belonged to, and those are exactly the dimensions that end investigations.

seniorHow do you structure observability across multiple clusters and regions?

Answer

Collect locally, query globally. Each cluster or region runs its own collection and, for metrics, its own local store, so a network partition or a regional outage does not blind you exactly when you need visibility most, and so you are not paying egress on raw telemetry. Above that sits a global query layer — a federated or centralised long-term store — that can answer cross-region questions, plus global identity: every signal carries cloud.region, cluster and environment as resource attributes, or you cannot tell whether a spike is one region or all of them. Traces are the exception that needs care: a request that crosses regions produces spans in two places, so either ship spans to one trace backend or ensure both regions write to a store that can assemble them — otherwise cross-region traces are permanently broken. Two rules to state unprompted: the monitoring for region A must not live only in region A, and there must be at least one path — usually external synthetics plus a small out-of-region alerting stack — that survives losing an entire region, including its observability.

Example

per region : collector agents -> collector gateway -> local metrics store
                                                   -> trace/log backend (or central)
global     : long-term store federating all regions; dashboards query it
resilience : synthetics + alert evaluation OUTSIDE any single region
identity   : every signal carries cloud.region, k8s.cluster.name,
             deployment.environment.name

Follow-ups

  • Where do you evaluate alerts? — Close to the data for latency, but with at least a critical subset evaluated outside the region so a regional failure still pages someone.
  • How do you avoid duplicate alerts across regions? — Aggregate at the global layer for global conditions, keep regional alerts regional, and use inhibition so a region-down alert suppresses its children.
  • What about data residency? — Telemetry contains personal data; keep it in-region where required and federate only aggregates across the boundary.

Common wrong answer

"Ship everything to one central cluster so we have a single pane of glass." One region's outage or a cross-region network problem then takes your visibility with it, and you pay egress on every byte. Collect locally, federate for query, and keep an alerting path that survives losing any one region.

14Telemetry security and privacy

Rarely the focus of an observability interview, but it appears as a probe in regulated industries and it is disproportionately damaging to get wrong — an answer that treats telemetry as exempt from data protection marks you as a liability. Four questions cover the ground: what personal data leaks into telemetry, how the pipeline itself is secured, telemetry as a security signal, and what happens when someone asks you to delete their data.

midWhat personal data ends up in telemetry, and why does that matter legally?

Answer

More than teams expect, and mostly by accident: IP addresses and user agents in HTTP attributes; user, account and email identifiers used as labels or span attributes; full URLs containing identifiers, tokens or search terms in query strings; request and response bodies captured "for debugging"; headers including cookies and authorization; and free-text log messages containing names, addresses or support conversations. Under GDPR and similar regimes an IP address and an online identifier are personal data, so a telemetry store containing them is subject to lawful basis, purpose limitation, storage limitation, access control and data-subject rights — the same obligations as your production database, applied to a system that is usually far more widely readable and far less carefully retained. That is the point interviewers are checking: telemetry is not a technical exhaust exempt from data protection. The practical posture is to minimise at emission (pseudonymous IDs, templated routes instead of raw URLs, no bodies), redact in the pipeline as a second net, keep retention short, and document what is collected and why.

Example

http.url with query stringhttp.route plus explicit safe parameters
Email addressenduser.pseudo.id, a stable pseudonym
Full request bodyShape, size, and a hash if you need to compare
Client IP verbatimTruncated or geo-resolved, where the use case allows

Follow-ups

  • Is pseudonymisation enough? — It reduces risk but pseudonymous data is still personal data if re-identification is possible. It does not exempt you from retention or access obligations.
  • Where does the URL query string bite? — Password-reset tokens, session tokens and search terms end up in logs, in access logs and in span attributes. Use the templated route as the attribute.
  • What about cross-border transfer? — Sending telemetry to a backend in another jurisdiction is a data transfer like any other; it needs the same legal basis and sometimes in-region collection.

Common wrong answer

"It's just telemetry, not customer data." IP addresses, user identifiers and search terms are customer data wherever they are stored, and the observability store is usually the most broadly accessible system in the company. This answer is the one that ends interviews in regulated environments.

seniorHow do you secure the telemetry pipeline itself?

Answer

Treat it as a production data path with its own threat model. In transit: TLS everywhere, mutual TLS between agents, gateways and backends where you can, and authenticated OTLP — the collector supports auth extensions, so an open 4317 on a shared network is a defect. On ingress: never expose a collector receiver to the internet without authentication and rate limiting, because an unauthenticated OTLP endpoint is both a data-poisoning vector (an attacker injects fake spans and metrics to hide activity or trigger false alerts) and a denial-of-wallet vector against your ingestion bill. At rest and on read: role-based access to the query layer with tenant isolation, and audit of who queried what, because the observability store is a rich target — it contains identifiers, internal topology and sometimes credentials. On the agents: the collector runs privileged in many deployments (host metrics, eBPF), so its configuration is privileged material and its supply chain matters — pin versions and images. And redact before export, so secrets never reach a third-party backend in the first place.

Example

extensions:
  bearertokenauth/ingest: { token: "${env:OTLP_INGEST_TOKEN}" }
receivers:
  otlp:
    protocols:
      grpc:
        endpoint: 0.0.0.0:4317
        auth: { authenticator: bearertokenauth/ingest }
        tls: { cert_file: /tls/tls.crt, key_file: /tls/tls.key, client_ca_file: /tls/ca.crt }

Follow-ups

  • What is the risk of an unauthenticated collector? — Injected telemetry that pollutes dashboards and alerts (masking a real attack), plus unbounded ingestion cost. Both are real, and the second is immediate.
  • Should everyone be able to query everything? — Broad read access is valuable, so the compromise is broad access to redacted data and restricted access to anything sensitive, with query auditing.
  • Who can change collector config? — A small set, reviewed, because a config change can exfiltrate telemetry to a new exporter endpoint.

Common wrong answer

"It's internal traffic on a private network, so it doesn't need auth." Flat internal trust is exactly what lateral movement exploits, and an unauthenticated ingest endpoint lets anyone who reaches it forge telemetry or run up your bill. Authenticate and encrypt the pipeline like any other data path.

seniorHow does observability data support security detection, and where is the boundary?

Answer

Operational telemetry is genuinely useful for detection: authentication failure rates, sudden changes in access patterns, unusual outbound connections, spikes in 403s or in data-export endpoints, and anomalous latency or volume from one identity are all visible in data you already collect. Traces are particularly good at showing lateral movement, because they record who called what across service boundaries. The boundary is that security monitoring has different requirements: it needs completeness (sampling is unacceptable for audit-relevant events), long retention measured in months or years, tamper-evidence, and a much stricter access model — and it needs detections maintained by people who track adversary behaviour, not by the observability team. So the sensible architecture is one collection layer feeding two consumers: your observability backend for operations, and a SIEM or security data lake for detection, with security-relevant events routed on a protected, unsampled pipeline. Say clearly that you would not run detection off a sampled, 14-day, broadly-readable telemetry store — and that the observability store's own access should be audited, since it is itself a target.

Example

# route security-relevant records to the SIEM on an unsampled path
processors:
  routing/security:
    from_attribute: event.domain
    table:
      - value: security
        exporters: [otlp/siem]
    default_exporters: [otlp/observability]

Follow-ups

  • What can observability detect that a SIEM often misses? — Behavioural anomalies in application semantics: an internal service suddenly calling an endpoint it never called, or a tenant's query pattern changing shape.
  • Why can't you just sample security events? — A detection built on 1% of events misses 99% of a low-and-slow attack. Completeness is the requirement that separates the two systems.
  • Who owns the overlap? — Shared pipeline, separate detections. The platform team owns the transport; security owns the rules.

Common wrong answer

"We use our observability platform as our SIEM — it's all logs anyway." Sampling, short retention, no tamper-evidence and broad read access make it unfit for detection and for evidence, and it puts security-sensitive data in a widely readable store. One collection layer, two consumers.

seniorA user exercises their right to erasure. What happens to their telemetry?

Answer

It is in scope, and the honest answer starts by admitting that most telemetry stores are terrible at targeted deletion: they are append-only, immutable-chunk, time-partitioned systems designed for scan performance, not for finding and removing one user's rows. So the workable strategy is designed in before the request arrives. Minimise: do not store direct identifiers — use a pseudonymous ID whose mapping lives in a system that can delete, so erasing the mapping renders the telemetry effectively anonymous. Limit retention: a 14-30 day window means the data expires faster than most erasure processes complete, which is a defensible position when documented. Segregate: if you must keep identifiers, keep them in a store with deletion support rather than in the trace and log stores. And know what your backends actually support — some offer tenant- or filter-based deletion, many only offer whole-block expiry. In an interview, name the trade-off explicitly: full per-user deletability across telemetry is expensive and usually achieved by not storing the identifier in the first place.

Example

telemetry stores    enduser.pseudo.id = "u_88213"      (no email, no name)
mapping service     u_88213 -> user 4471                (deletable, access-controlled)

erasure request  -> delete the mapping row
                 -> remaining telemetry is no longer linkable to a person
                 -> and expires naturally within the 30-day retention window

Follow-ups

  • Is short retention a defence? — It is a strong mitigation and a documented storage-limitation position, but it is not a blanket exemption; get it reviewed rather than asserted.
  • What about backups and archives? — They are in scope too, which is another argument for not putting identifiers there. Document the retention of every tier.
  • Who should answer this question in your organisation? — Legal and privacy set the requirement; engineering states honestly what the systems can do. Overpromising deletability is worse than a documented limitation.

Common wrong answer

"We'd just run a delete query for that user across the logs and traces." Most trace and log backends cannot do targeted deletion at all, and promising it commits your organisation to something it cannot deliver. Design for it — pseudonymisation plus short retention — rather than claiming a capability that does not exist.

15Frontend and mobile observability

Asked when the role touches the client, and skipped otherwise — but it is a differentiator for a full-stack lead, because most backend candidates cannot describe how a browser trace joins a server trace. The four questions here cover real-user monitoring versus synthetics, connecting client to server, the constraints that make mobile genuinely different, and client-side error reporting.

midWhat does RUM tell you that server-side telemetry never can?

Answer

Everything between your response leaving the server and the user being able to use the page: DNS and TLS setup, CDN behaviour, network conditions on real devices, JavaScript execution and rendering, third-party scripts, and the long tail of device and browser diversity. That is where most perceived slowness lives — a 40 ms server response can still be an eight-second page on a mid-range Android phone on a congested network. Real user monitoring reports the Core Web Vitals from actual sessions — Largest Contentful Paint for loading, Interaction to Next Paint for responsiveness (which replaced First Input Delay as the responsiveness vital in 2024), and Cumulative Layout Shift for visual stability — plus client-side errors and failed requests the server never saw because they never arrived. It also captures the failures that are invisible server-side by construction: requests blocked by an ad blocker, CORS failures, a broken deployment of a static asset, or a client that timed out before your server finished. The counterpart is that RUM is noisy and only covers traffic you actually get, which is why you keep synthetics for consistent, controlled measurement.

Example

SymptomServer viewRUM view
"The site is slow"p99 = 80 ms, all greenLCP p75 = 4.2 s on Android
Checkout failingNo errorsCORS error on the payment iframe
Regional complaintsNothing unusualCDN edge in that region serving stale assets

Follow-ups

  • Which percentile for web vitals? — p75 by convention, segmented by device class and connection, because a single global number hides the mobile population entirely.
  • What is the cost and privacy angle? — RUM beacons are volume plus personal data (IP, user agent, URL); sample, and strip identifiers at ingest.
  • Do you still need synthetics? — Yes: controlled, comparable measurement and coverage of paths with little real traffic, plus they run when users are asleep.

Common wrong answer

"Our server-side latency is what matters; the front end is the front-end team's problem." The user experiences the sum, and in most products the majority of perceived latency is client-side. A lead who cannot reason past their own service boundary is exactly what this question is designed to find.

midHow do you connect a browser trace to the backend trace?

Answer

The browser SDK starts a root span for the document load or the user interaction and injects traceparent into outgoing fetch/XHR requests, so the server continues the trace instead of starting a new one. Three practical obstacles come up every time. First, CORS: traceparent is not a simple header, so cross-origin requests trigger a preflight and the server must list it in Access-Control-Allow-Headers, or the header is silently stripped and every trace splits in two. Second, the browser SDK must be told which origins to inject into — the default is same-origin only, and injecting into third-party origins is both useless and a small information leak. Third, transport: browsers cannot speak gRPC, so exports go over OTLP HTTP, ideally via sendBeacon so telemetry survives page unload. The complementary trick worth knowing is the Server-Timing response header, which lets the server hand the trace ID back to the page so client-side errors can reference the server trace even when the request itself was not sampled.

Example

registerInstrumentations({
  instrumentations: [new FetchInstrumentation({
    // without this, traceparent is never injected cross-origin
    propagateTraceHeaderCorsUrls: [/^https:\/\/api\.example\.com/],
  })],
});
// and on the server, or the preflight fails:
//   Access-Control-Allow-Headers: traceparent, tracestate
//   Server-Timing: traceparent;desc="00-4bf92f35...-00f067aa0ba902b7-01"

Follow-ups

  • Why is the browser sampling decision awkward? — The client decides at the root, so a head-sampled browser trace decides for your whole backend; and clients are untrusted, so a forced sampled flag from the browser is a cost risk. Re-evaluate server-side.
  • What about page unload? — Use sendBeacon (or fetch with keepalive), or you lose the telemetry of every user who navigates away — which is exactly the frustrated ones.
  • Bundle size? — Browser SDKs are not free; measure the added bytes and the startup cost, and load instrumentation lazily where you can.

Common wrong answer

"Install the browser SDK and the traces connect automatically." They connect same-origin. The moment the API is on another origin — which it usually is — the missing CORS allow-list silently breaks every join, and the symptom is thousands of one-span browser traces that nobody can explain.

seniorWhat makes mobile observability structurally different from backend?

Answer

Four constraints that have no backend equivalent. You cannot deploy a fix: a release goes through app-store review and then users update on their own schedule, so you must support many versions simultaneously for months and your telemetry has to be versioned and back-compatible — which also means instrumentation gaps are permanent for the installed base. The device is not yours: battery, CPU, memory and data plan are the user's, so telemetry must batch, compress, and respect background restrictions and metered connections. Connectivity is intermittent: events must be persisted locally and uploaded later, which means out-of-order and delayed arrival — telemetry timestamped hours before it arrives is normal, and your pipeline and dashboards must not treat it as clock skew. And the failure modes are different: crashes and ANRs need symbolication against the exact build, cold-start time is a first-class metric, and the OS may kill your process without warning, so the last thing you observed may be all you get. The practical consequence for a lead: remote configuration of sampling and log levels is essential, because you cannot ship a change to turn instrumentation up.

Example

backend                         mobile
deploy in minutes               app-store review + user update; N versions live for months
always connected                offline-first: persist, batch, upload later (hours late)
unlimited power                 battery/data budgets; background execution limits
you own the host                thousands of device/OS combinations
crash = restart + logs          crash/ANR needs symbolication per build; process may vanish

Follow-ups

  • How do you handle delayed events? — Keep the client event time and the ingest time as separate fields, and build dashboards on event time with a defined lateness window.
  • How do you control cost from millions of clients? — Client-side sampling driven by remote config, plus aggressive batching. Never let every device send every event.
  • What is the single most valuable mobile metric? — Crash-free session (or user) rate, segmented by app version and OS, because it is the one number that maps to user harm and to release quality.

Common wrong answer

"Same as backend, just with an SDK in the app." Ignoring the deploy latency and offline constraints leads to instrumentation you cannot change for months, telemetry that drains batteries, and a pipeline that silently discards late-arriving events from exactly the users with the worst connectivity — the ones you most need to see.

seniorHow do you make client-side error reporting actually useful?

Answer

Three things turn a firehose of client errors into something a team can act on. First, symbolication: upload source maps or debug symbols per build and keep them, or every stack trace is minified noise; tie them to a build ID that the client reports, and never serve source maps publicly. Second, grouping and deduplication: raw client errors are dominated by browser extensions, ad blockers, bot traffic and one user's broken network, so you need fingerprinting by normalised stack, a mechanism to ignore known-external frames, and counts by affected users rather than by event, because one user in a retry loop can generate thousands of events and look like an outage. Third, context: app or bundle version, release, device and OS, the user's route, breadcrumbs of the last N actions, and — the one that makes it debuggable — the trace ID of the failed request, so a client error links to the server-side trace. Then set the bar: alert on new error signatures in a release and on crash-free rate regressions, not on absolute error volume, which is a function of traffic and of the internet's general noise.

Example

group by  : normalised stack fingerprint + release
rank by   : affected users (not events)
enrich    : release, device, OS, route, breadcrumbs, backend trace_id
alert on  : new signature in a release; crash-free session rate drop > 0.5%
ignore    : known extension frames, bot user agents, offline network errors

Follow-ups

  • Why affected users rather than events? — Event counts are dominated by retry loops and by a few pathological clients; user counts map to impact and to whether it is worth fixing.
  • How do you keep source maps private? — Upload them to the error backend at build time and do not deploy them alongside the bundle; a public source map hands your code to anyone.
  • How much client telemetry should you sample? — Errors at 100% where volume allows, performance events sampled — and both controlled remotely so you can turn them down without a release.

Common wrong answer

"We log JavaScript errors to the console and collect them, then alert when the count spikes." Without symbolication they are unreadable, without grouping they are unrankable, and alerting on raw counts guarantees pages driven by bots and browser extensions rather than by anything you can fix.

16Judgment calls

These have no single right answer, and that is the point: the interviewer is watching how you frame the problem, which trade-offs you name without being asked, and whether you can hold a position under push-back while still updating on new information. A strong answer picks a side, states the conditions under which it would change, and names the cost of being wrong. Say "it depends" only if you immediately say on what.

principalYour observability bill is 15% of infrastructure spend. Is that too much?

What a strong answer shows

That you refuse the question as posed and reframe it around value: 15% is meaningless without knowing what it buys and what the alternative costs. A strong answer asks what the failure cost is — an hour of downtime for this business, the engineer-hours per incident, the customer churn from slow detection — and compares against the marginal reduction, then benchmarks internally rather than against an industry number. It distinguishes spend from waste: 15% that is mostly one team's cardinality bug is a defect; 15% that funds fast detection on a revenue-critical path may be cheap. It names the diagnostic questions — what is the distribution across signals and teams, what is the growth rate relative to traffic, and is cost per request flat or rising — because a rising cost-per-request is the actual alarm, not the absolute percentage.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Cost versus time-to-detect and time-to-resolve; every reduction has a debuggability price, and you should say which one you are paying.
  • The asymmetry: under-spending is invisible until an incident, over-spending is visible every month — which biases organisations towards cutting too far.
  • Per-seat licensing actively discourages the broad access that creates the value; it should be renegotiated rather than absorbed.
  • Waste is fixable without loss (cardinality bombs, log chatter); fidelity is not. Do all of the former before any of the latter.
principalShould every service own its own dashboards and alerts, or should a platform team standardise them?

What a strong answer shows

That you split the question into the layer that must be uniform and the layer that must be local. A strong answer standardises the skeleton — RED metrics, SLO panels, burn-rate alerts, resource attributes — generated automatically from a declarative spec, so every service has a competent baseline nobody had to build; and leaves domain-specific panels and alerts to the team that understands the domain. It grounds this in incident reality: during an incident you traverse services you have never seen, and if every team invented its own layout you cannot read them; conversely a central team cannot know that this service's queue depth means something different at month end. It also names the operating model consequence — the platform team owns the generator and the standards, not the content — and the failure mode of full centralisation: alerts nobody owns, and teams who feel no responsibility for their own signals.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Consistency speeds cross-team incident response; local knowledge catches domain-specific failure. You need both layers, not a winner.
  • Central ownership scales badly and creates a bottleneck; pure autonomy produces 200 incompatible dashboards and duplicated effort.
  • Generated-from-spec is the reconciliation: uniform where it matters, cheap to extend, and reviewable.
  • Ownership of the pager must follow ownership of the alert, or standardisation becomes an unfunded mandate.
principalA team wants to add a customer ID as a metric label. What do you do?

What a strong answer shows

That you engage with the underlying need rather than quoting the rule. A strong answer asks what question they are trying to answer — usually "is this specific customer affected" or "which customer is causing this" — and then routes it to the right signal: high-cardinality dimensions belong on spans and logs, where cost is per event, not on metrics, where cost is per series forever. It offers concrete alternatives: exemplars linking the metric to the customer's traces; a bounded label such as tenant tier or a top-N-customers label with an "other" bucket; per-tenant SLOs for the handful of customers that genuinely warrant them; and a separate, deliberately-scoped metric with a hard series cap. It also names the enforcement layer — a cardinality limiter in the collector — so the answer does not depend on this team's goodwill or on catching it in review, and it states the cost concretely (10,000 customers × existing label combinations = the actual number) rather than abstractly.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Per-customer visibility is a real business need, especially in B2B; refusing it outright is the wrong answer.
  • Metrics cost by cardinality permanently, and the damage is shared — one metric can degrade the store for everyone.
  • Top-N plus "other" preserves 95% of the value at 1% of the cost, and per-tenant SLOs are justified for the few customers with contracts.
  • Enforcement belongs at the choke point; a policy without a limiter will be violated again by a different team next quarter.
seniorWould you page on a symptom you cannot yet explain?

What a strong answer shows

That you separate "explainable" from "actionable". A strong answer says yes, if it is user-visible and someone can do something — mitigate, shed, roll back, escalate — because understanding is not a precondition for action, and requiring it delays every response. It then adds the discipline that prevents this from becoming noise: the page must have a defined action even if that action is "investigate and decide within N minutes", it must carry a runbook and the current burn rate, and if it fires repeatedly without anyone being able to act, that is a defect to fix, not a fact to live with. It also distinguishes severity: a symptom with no possible action at 3am should be a ticket for the morning, however alarming it looks. The underlying principle — page on symptoms, not causes — is the thing to state explicitly.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Waking people for unexplained symptoms is how you catch novel failures; waking them for unactionable ones is how you lose them.
  • The cost of a false page is measurable (sleep, trust); the cost of a missed page is measurable too (impact minutes) — compare them explicitly rather than defaulting to caution.
  • "Cannot explain" often means "insufficient instrumentation", so a repeated unexplained page is an instrumentation action item.
  • Escalation policy matters: an on-call who cannot act must be able to wake someone who can, without hesitation.
seniorAuto-instrumentation gives you 80% coverage with zero effort. Is manual instrumentation still worth it?

What a strong answer shows

That you know what the missing 20% is, and that it is not the same 20% everywhere. A strong answer accepts auto-instrumentation as the floor — nobody should hand-write HTTP client spans in 2026 — and argues that manual work should be spent only where it changes an outcome: business-meaningful span names, the attributes you have actually needed during past incidents (tenant, plan, experiment, provider), internal waits that auto-instrumentation cannot see (locks, queues, pool acquisition), and domain metrics that no framework can infer. It frames the decision economically: each manual attribute should be traceable to a question someone asked at 3am, which is a much stronger filter than "instrument everything important". And it names the maintenance cost — manual instrumentation rots, drifts from conventions, and must be reviewed like an API — which is the reason not to do more of it than you need.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Auto gives breadth and consistency cheaply; manual gives the semantics that end investigations. Neither substitutes for the other.
  • Manual instrumentation is code: it needs review, naming discipline and tests, and it decays when nobody owns it.
  • Over-instrumenting has a real cost in span volume and in noise, and it makes traces harder to read, not easier.
  • The right forcing function is postmortems: every "we couldn't tell which X" becomes exactly one attribute.
seniorSampling: is there ever a case for keeping 100% of traces?

What a strong answer shows

That you reason from volume and from what the data is for, not from principle. A strong answer says yes in three cases: low-traffic services where 100% is cheap and sampling only makes the data statistically useless; high-value, low-volume flows (payments, regulated transactions) where every case may need to be reconstructed; and short investigation windows where you deliberately turn sampling up. And no for the general case at scale, because the marginal value of the millionth successful trace is near zero while its cost is not. It then reframes the real question as policy rather than rate: keep 100% of errors and slow traces, keep a floor per tenant and per route so quiet-but-important paths are never dark, and sample the boring majority — which delivers most of the value of 100% at a small fraction of the cost. A strong answer also flags the statistics trap: never compute rates from a non-uniformly sampled trace store.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Complete data versus cost, but also versus query performance: a 100% store is slower to search, which costs you during incidents.
  • Tail sampling buys selectivity at the price of buffering memory, a decision window, and trace-ID-aware routing.
  • Sampled traces are useless for counting; RED metrics must come from the unsampled span stream.
  • Sampling policy is a governance artefact — it decides what evidence exists later — so it should be reviewed, not left to a default.
seniorWhen is "just restart it" the right answer?

What a strong answer shows

That you weigh evidence against impact explicitly, and that you always capture before you destroy. A strong answer says restarting is right when customer impact is ongoing, when you have already taken the cheap evidence (thread dump, counters, a profile if it is quick), and when restarting is genuinely reversible; it is wrong as a reflex, because the failure will recur tonight with no evidence and you will have paid the outage twice. It distinguishes cases: a wedged process with a known-good restart path, versus a crash loop where restarting achieves nothing, versus a state-corruption bug where a restart hides data damage that keeps propagating. It names the systemic follow-up — automatic capture on hang or OOM, so the evidence exists without a human deciding under pressure — and the anti-pattern of the scheduled restart as a permanent fix for a leak nobody investigated.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Restoring service now versus being able to prevent the next occurrence; both are real costs, and the right balance depends on impact and on recurrence rate.
  • A thirty-second dump usually costs less than the next incident; automate it so the trade-off disappears.
  • Restart-as-mitigation is legitimate; restart-as-explanation is not — the postmortem must say what was actually wrong.
  • Some failures survive restarts (metastable states, corrupted shared state), and restarting can even deepen them by cold-starting caches and pools.
seniorYour dashboards are green but customers are complaining. What does that tell you?

What a strong answer shows

That you treat the customers as correct and the instrumentation as the suspect. A strong answer enumerates the specific ways green-but-broken happens: the SLI is measured at the wrong place (application-internal, so it never sees requests that failed before arriving); the aggregate hides a segment (one region, one tenant, one client version, 2% of users — all invisible in a fleet average); the failure is client-side and no server signal exists; the system is serving successfully but wrongly (correctness failures look perfect on availability metrics); the telemetry pipeline itself is broken, so flat lines read as healthy; or the alert threshold was tuned until it stopped firing. It then commits to an action: reproduce as a customer, look at RUM and gateway data, split by every dimension, and treat the gap as a defect to fix in the SLI — the outcome of this incident should be that the same complaint would fire an alert next time.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Aggregation is what makes dashboards readable and what makes segments invisible; per-segment SLIs cost cardinality and alert surface.
  • Measuring closer to the user increases fidelity and noise simultaneously; you need both an internal and an edge/RUM view.
  • Availability SLIs cannot see correctness; if wrong-but-successful responses matter, you need a correctness SLI.
  • "No data" must never render as healthy — absence-of-signal alerting is the cheap fix for a whole class of this failure.
principalWould you let teams pick their own observability tools?

What a strong answer shows

That you separate the layers again: instrumentation and transport should be standard (OpenTelemetry plus a shared collector), because correlation across services is the entire value and it dies the moment two teams use incompatible conventions; the query and visualisation layer can tolerate some diversity, since a team that prefers a different UI over the same data costs little. A strong answer names the concrete costs of fragmentation — incidents that cross a tool boundary become unnavigable, on-call engineers cannot read another team's signals, volume discounts evaporate, and every integration is built N times — and balances them against the real benefit of autonomy: teams adopt tools they chose, and a mandated tool nobody likes gets worked around. It lands on "standard pipes, some freedom at the edges", with an explicit exception process rather than a blanket ban, and it notes that OTLP is what makes the compromise possible at all.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Autonomy versus cross-team debuggability; the cost of fragmentation is paid during incidents, which is when it is most expensive.
  • Standardising the wire format costs teams almost nothing; standardising the UI costs goodwill for a smaller benefit.
  • Purchasing leverage and integration effort scale badly with tool count, and both are invisible to the team making the choice.
  • An exception process with a named approver beats a ban, because bans produce shadow tooling you cannot see.
principalYou inherit a system with no observability and three months to improve reliability. Where do you start?

What a strong answer shows

Sequencing under constraint, and resisting the urge to build a platform. A strong answer starts by finding out what is actually breaking — read the last three months of incidents and support tickets, talk to on-call — because that tells you which of the missing pieces matters. Then it lands the cheapest high-value layer first: structured logs with trace IDs and RED metrics at the edge (gateway or load balancer), which gives an SLI for the main journey without touching a single service. Then symptom-based alerting on that SLI, so someone is told before customers are, followed by auto-instrumentation for tracing on the critical path, and only then the broader rollout. Throughout it names what it is not doing — no vendor migration, no 200-service mandate, no dashboard programme — and defines success as an outcome: "we detect our own incidents before customers report them, and we can localise a failure to a service in under ten minutes". It also budgets time for the incident process itself (severity scale, roles, postmortems), because that produces reliability improvements faster than tooling does.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Breadth versus depth: one instrumented critical path beats partial coverage everywhere, and it builds the political capital for the rest.
  • Buying a backend versus building one — with three months, you buy, and you keep the collector so the choice is reversible.
  • Process improvements (declaration, roles, postmortems) are nearly free and often outperform tooling in the first quarter.
  • Anything you enable must have a cost guard rail from day one, or the bill becomes the story instead of the reliability.

17Design exercises

Observability connects to system design in three recurring ways, and these are the three exercises interviewers actually set. Each gives you the prompt, the clarifying questions you should ask before drawing anything — asking them is part of the score — and then, in a collapsed block so you can attempt it first, a strong solution outline with the trade-offs you are expected to raise unprompted. Time-box each to 35-45 minutes as you would in the room.

Exercise 1 — Telemetry platform for 400 services across three regions

Prompt. "We run about 400 services in three cloud regions on Kubernetes, roughly 150k requests per second at peak. Today every team ships telemetry directly to a vendor and the bill has doubled year on year. Design the telemetry platform: collection, processing, storage, access and cost control. Tell me what you would build, what you would buy, and how you would migrate."

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • What is the current spend and its split across metrics, logs and traces? Which teams dominate it?
  • What are the actual reliability requirements — SLOs, regulatory retention, data-residency constraints per region?
  • What is the incident profile: how are incidents detected today, and how long does localisation take?
  • Which languages and frameworks, and how much is already OpenTelemetry-instrumented?
  • Is there an existing platform team, and what is my budget and headcount?
principalStrong solution outline and the trade-offs to name

Shape of the answer

Three tiers, one seam. Collection: OpenTelemetry SDKs with operator-injected auto-instrumentation as the default, exporting OTLP to a per-node agent collector that adds Kubernetes and cloud resource attributes and batches. Processing: a per-region gateway collector tier — stateless work at the agent, stateful work at the gateway (tail sampling behind a trace-ID load-balancing exporter, span-to-metrics before any sampling, redaction, cardinality limiting, ownership enforcement, routing). Storage: buy the backend, keep a hot 14-day tier per region and a federated long-term tier for SLI series and downsampled metrics; traces sampled by policy rather than by rate; logs tiered with only errors and audit protected from cost controls. The collector is the seam that makes the storage decision reversible, and it is where every cost and privacy control lives. Governance: semantic conventions enforced in the pipeline, ownership attribute mandatory, per-team showback with monthly top-contributor reports, and a declarative SLO spec that generates recording rules, burn alerts and dashboards.

Migration

Insert the collector under the existing agents first (no user-visible change), then dual-emit on a pathfinder set of services and compare span counts and latency distributions until they agree, then migrate service by service using auto-instrumentation with collector-side attribute renaming so existing dashboards keep working. Port dashboards and alerts as a tracked work item — it is the largest cost — and time-box the dual-emit window explicitly because it doubles ingest.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Build versus buy. Buy storage and query; build the pipeline. Self-hosting metrics/logs/traces at 150k rps is a permanent team, and it trades vendor lock-in for bespoke lock-in.
  • Central versus local collection. Collect and store hot data in-region for resilience, egress cost and residency; federate for global queries. A single central cluster is cheaper to operate and fails exactly when you need it.
  • Tail sampling costs. Memory, a decision window that must exceed your slowest trace, and trace-ID-aware routing that complicates scaling. Head sampling is simpler and blind.
  • Enforcement versus autonomy. A platform-owned non-overridable base config plus team overlays; total central control does not scale, total autonomy destroys correlation.
  • Cost control versus blindness. Budgets and alerts, never automatic cut-off — a team that exceeds its budget is often the team having an incident.
  • Failure of the platform itself. Collector internal metrics routed to a different backend, out-of-region alert evaluation, and external synthetics, so an observability outage is detectable.

Exercise 2 — SLOs and alerting for a multi-tenant payments platform

Prompt. "You own a payments platform: an API that authorises card payments, a webhook delivery system that notifies merchants, and a nightly settlement batch. Enterprise merchants have contractual availability terms. Design the SLIs, SLOs, alerting and dashboards, and tell me what you would page on."

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • What does the merchant actually experience when we fail — a declined payment, a retry, a delayed notification? Which is worst?
  • What do the enterprise contracts commit to, measured how, and over what window?
  • What is the traffic distribution across merchants — is it dominated by a few, and are there quiet-but-important tenants?
  • Which dependencies are outside our control (card networks, issuers) and what are their observed availabilities?
  • What is the tolerance for settlement lateness, and who is harmed by it?
principalStrong solution outline and the trade-offs to name

Shape of the answer

Three different systems need three different SLI families — that recognition is most of the score. The authorisation API is request/response: availability (non-5xx, and note that a legitimate decline is a success of the system, so the denominator excludes it) and latency (proportion under 500 ms), measured at the edge. Webhook delivery is asynchronous: the SLI is delivery within a target — proportion of events delivered and acknowledged within 60 seconds — plus a permanently-failed ratio, because "eventually delivered after four hours" is a failure the availability metric would call a success. Settlement is a deadline batch: proportion of settlement runs completed before the cut-off, plus a correctness SLI (reconciliation matches) because a settlement that completes with wrong numbers is worse than one that is late. Internal SLOs sit tighter than the contractual SLAs, and per-tenant SLIs exist for the enterprise tier so one large merchant's outage is not averaged away by everyone else's healthy traffic.

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Denominator definition. Excluding declines, malformed requests and one merchant's load test is necessary and is also the easiest place to accidentally define away real failures — so the exclusions are documented and reviewed.
  • Dependency composition. Your availability cannot exceed the card networks' and issuers'; either the SLO accounts for it or you measure "our fault" separately and publish both. Say which you chose and why.
  • Per-tenant SLIs. Real business value versus cardinality and alert-surface explosion; limit them to the enterprise tier and use a traffic floor to avoid tiny-denominator noise.
  • Latency versus availability on payments. A slow authorisation often becomes an abandoned checkout, so a latency SLO with real teeth matters as much as availability.
  • Correctness as a first-class SLI. Money systems can fail successfully; availability metrics are blind to it, and reconciliation is the only detector.
  • Error-budget policy under contract. Enterprise SLAs make the freeze conversation concrete — name the pre-agreed policy and the exception approver.

Exercise 3 — Debugging an event-driven data pipeline end to end

Prompt. "Orders flow from an API into Kafka, through three stream processors, into a warehouse used for merchant reporting. Merchants report that some orders are missing from reports and others appear hours late. Design the observability for this pipeline so that next time you can answer 'where is order 12345 and why is it late' in under five minutes."

Clarifying questions to ask first

  • What is the contractual or expected freshness — minutes, hours? Is lateness or loss the more serious failure?
  • Are the processors idempotent, and is there deduplication? Could "missing" actually be "deduplicated incorrectly"?
  • What are the partitioning keys, and is per-key ordering required?
  • Is there an existing dead-letter path, and does anyone look at it?
  • How large is the volume, and how long must we be able to look back?
principalStrong solution outline and the trade-offs to name

Shape of the answer

Give every record an identity and a trail, then measure the pipeline as a series of stages rather than as one box. Propagate trace context in message headers at every hop; each processor emits a CONSUMER span linked (not childed) to the producer span, so a batch consumer can link to many producers and a delayed record does not attach to a trace closed hours ago. Carry an order.id and an event.time on every record and every span, so "where is order 12345" is a single query and lateness is measurable as processing time minus event time. Instrument per stage: input count, output count, dropped count with reason, dead-letter count, per-partition consumer lag (maximum, never average), and end-to-end latency from ingest to warehouse. Then define the two SLIs that actually matter — freshness (proportion of time the worst partition is under the target) and completeness (records out over records in per window, with a tolerance) — and alert on those rather than on job success. Add a reconciliation job that compares source and warehouse counts per window and raises a discrepancy, because silent loss is the failure the streaming metrics will not show.

api -> kafka(orders) -> enrich -> kafka(enriched) -> aggregate -> kafka(agg) -> loader -> warehouse

per stage: in/out/dropped(reason)/DLQ counts, max partition lag, stage latency
per record: order.id, event.time, trace context in headers (linked spans)
SLIs     : freshness (max lag < 5 min), completeness (out/in ≥ 0.9999)
alerts   : freshness burn; DLQ rate > 0; reconciliation mismatch; consumer group stalled
answer   : "where is order 12345" -> query by order.id across stage spans
           -> last stage seen, its timestamp, and the reason it stopped

Trade-offs to name unprompted

  • Trace links versus child spans. Batch consumers and hours-long delays make child spans unbounded and misleading; links keep the causality and bound the trace.
  • Per-record identity costs. Indexing order.id makes lookup instant and is a cardinality decision — it belongs on spans and logs, never as a metric label.
  • Sampling in pipelines. Sampling traces breaks "where is this record"; the pragmatic split is sampled traces for shape plus a small always-on per-record audit trail for lookups.
  • Maximum versus average lag. One stuck partition is a total outage for the affected keys and is invisible in an average.
  • Exactly-once ambitions. Idempotency plus deduplication plus reconciliation is achievable; end-to-end exactly-once across a warehouse boundary usually is not — say so and design for detection.
  • Cost of reconciliation. It is a second pipeline with its own failure modes, but it is the only thing that catches silent loss; scope it to counts per window rather than full record comparison.

7Head versus tail sampling?Head is cheap, stateless and blind; tail buffers the whole trace so you can keep 100% of errors and slow traces, at the cost of memory and trace-ID-aware routing.
8How does trace context propagate?W3C traceparent/tracestate headers across the wire, an async-local context inside the process — and every broken trace is one of those two failing.
9Why did the service not recover when the dependency did?Metastable failure: retries and queued work sustain the overload after the trigger is gone, so you must shed load and ramp back rather than wait or scale.
10Why is a p99 more useful than an average?Latency distributions are long-tailed, so the mean describes nobody; and with N fan-out calls a per-call p99 becomes the typical page load.
