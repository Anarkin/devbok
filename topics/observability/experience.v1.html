01The p99 that only existed on the dashboard

tier · senior performance problem diagnosed

Example — replace with your own

Support kept escalating "checkout is slow" and I kept closing the tickets, because the latency panel said the checkout API's p99 was 240 ms and had not moved in a month. What finally made me stop trusting the panel was a screen recording from a support call: eleven seconds from tapping Pay to the confirmation. Nothing in our metrics could produce eleven seconds. So the metric was wrong, not the customer.

Two independent bugs, and both are the kind that survive for years because the graph stays flat and green. The first was aggregation: each pod exported a pre-computed quantile — a Prometheus summary, quantiles calculated inside the process — and the dashboard did avg by (service) over them. You cannot average percentiles. The mean of thirty pods' p99s is not the fleet p99; it is a number with no statistical meaning at all, and it is systematically optimistic, because the one pod that is on fire gets diluted by twenty-nine that are fine. The second was bucket design. When we later switched that endpoint to a classic histogram, someone had defined the buckets ending at le="1", so every request slower than one second landed in +Inf, and histogram_quantile — which interpolates linearly within a bucket — cannot interpolate inside an unbounded one. It clamps. Our p99 was mathematically incapable of exceeding one second no matter how bad the service got.

I proved it in twenty minutes with two queries side by side — the old panel, and the same window measured from spans, which have no bucket boundaries at all:

# what the dashboard showed: the mean of per-pod quantiles - meaningless
avg by (service) (http_server_request_duration_seconds{quantile="0.99"})

# what it should have shown: quantile over the merged classic histogram
histogram_quantile(0.99,
  sum by (service, le) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])))

# native histograms (stable since Prometheus 3.8.0): no le ladder to get wrong
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (service) (rate(http_server_request_duration_seconds[5m])))

The real p99 was 4.6 seconds and the p99.9 was over twelve. The slow path was a per-item tax-rate lookup that called a partner API once per basket line: a basket of one was fast, a basket of thirty was not, and average basket size had crept up after we launched bulk ordering. I found the offending span in four minutes once the histogram carried exemplars — an exemplar attaches a trace id to a specific bucket observation, so you click the tall bar and land in an actual slow trace instead of guessing which endpoint to open.

The fix came in three sizes. Same day: correct the dashboard and the alert to take the quantile over merged buckets, and add a burn-rate alert on the checkout SLO so an eleven-second experience would page someone. Same week: migrate the endpoint to native histograms, which store an exponential bucket layout at a configurable resolution instead of a hand-chosen ladder, so nobody picks an le ceiling again. Same month: batch the tax lookups and cache by jurisdiction, which took the p99 to 380 ms. What I would do differently is the ordering — I spent two weeks defending an optimisation backlog built on a number that was wrong, and I should have cross-checked metrics against traces on day one, because two signals disagreeing is the cheapest diagnostic in observability.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you debug the measurement or only the system. Anyone can say "I profiled it and found an N+1"; the signal here is that you noticed the instrument was lying and could say precisely why, in statistical terms. The mistake candidates make on this archetype is telling it as a pure optimisation win — "I made it 12× faster" — with no account of how they knew where to look, and no admission of the two weeks spent optimising the wrong thing.

(c) Recall prompts

When did a dashboard last disagree with a human being — and who turned out to be right?

Look for support tickets or a colleague's "it feels slow" that you dismissed because a panel was green. The story lives in the moment you decided to check the instrument instead of the system.

Where in your stack is a percentile computed inside the process rather than in the query?

Look for summaries, a client-side p95 field in logs, an APM agent set to pre-aggregate, or any dashboard applying avg() to something already named p99. Finding one is a story; explaining to the owning team why it is wrong is a better one.

Have you ever had to choose histogram buckets? What did you choose, and what did you get wrong?

A bucket ladder encodes an assumption about the SLO. If you picked 0.005 … 10 by copying an example from the docs, say so — that is the honest and common answer, and it sets up the native-histogram punchline.

What is the most expensive thing you optimised that turned out not to be the bottleneck?

This is the "what I'd do differently" half. A quantified wrong turn — two weeks, one sprint — makes the story credible in a way a clean win never does.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why can't you average percentiles? Explain it to a junior.

A percentile is a position in a distribution, not a quantity, so it has no additive structure — the p99 of a union is not a function of the p99s of its parts. Concretely: twenty-nine pods at 100 ms and one at 30 s average to about 1.1 s, but the true fleet p99 depends on how much traffic the bad pod took and could easily be 30 s. What is additive is the histogram: counts per bucket sum cleanly across pods, which is exactly why you aggregate buckets first and take the quantile last. The rule I give people is "sum then quantile, never quantile then average".

At what scale does the classic histogram approach break?

On cardinality. A classic histogram costs one series per bucket per label combination, so ten buckets across four hundred routes and three status classes is already tens of thousands of series for one metric — and people respond by deleting buckets, which destroys tail accuracy exactly where they need it. Native histograms fix the storage side, one sparse series with an exponential schema instead of a ladder, but they move the constraint rather than removing it, because every hop has to understand them. They have been stable since Prometheus 3.8.0 and the current 3.14.0 line is comfortable with them; if you remote-write to something older, or scrape with an agent that only speaks the classic text format, you get a silent downgrade rather than an error, and that is what to test before rollout.

You cross-checked with traces. Why not just use traces for latency?

Because sampled traces answer "why is this request slow" well and "how often are requests slow" badly. Under head sampling you keep a fraction chosen before the outcome is known, so rare slow requests are rare in the sample too, and any tail statistic carries sampling error on top of real variance. Metrics are counted on every request in-process, which makes them the right instrument for rates and thresholds. Exemplars are the bridge: they carry a trace id on a histogram observation, so you go from "the number is bad" to "here is one of the bad ones" without a join.

What did you trade away by moving to native histograms?

Compatibility and a little predictability. We gained resolution and dropped a large chunk of series, but took on a stack requirement — SDK, Collector, storage and Grafana all have to handle them — and gave up the way an SRE used to eyeball a specific boundary like le="0.5". Some third-party dashboards and a few recording rules had to be rewritten, and exposition through the text format is still being designed, so a scrape-based fallback is not free. For an endpoint with a stable, well-understood SLO threshold, a classic histogram with three well-chosen buckets around that threshold is still cheaper, and we kept several.

How would you stop this whole class of bug recurring across forty services?

Make the right thing the default and the wrong thing hard to express. Latency instrumentation went into the shared service template so nobody hand-rolls it; we standardised on the semantic-convention metric names so dashboards are generated rather than copy-pasted; and CI now fails any dashboard JSON or rule file that applies an aggregation function to a series whose name or label marks it as a quantile. Then the durable part: an SLO on user-visible latency with a burn-rate alert, because that would have fired regardless of which instrument was lying underneath. I also added "does the metric agree with a trace?" to the service readiness checklist, since that cheap cross-check is what actually caught it.

02Head-based or tail-based sampling

tier · principal hard architectural trade-off

Example — replace with your own

We kept 1% of traces, chosen at the root by a probability sampler, and every incident followed the same script: someone found the failing request in the logs, pasted the trace id into the UI, and got "trace not found". The 1% we kept was a random 1%, and the requests we cared about were never random. The obvious answer — keep everything that failed — is tail sampling, and the obvious objection is that tail sampling is not a config flag, it is an architecture.

The mechanism is what the decision turns on. Head sampling decides at the root: the first service flips a weighted coin, records the answer in the sampled flag of the W3C traceparent header, and every downstream service honours it — which is why a head-sampled trace is either whole or absent. It costs nothing at the edge, and it is decided before you know whether the request was interesting. Tail sampling decides after the fact, which means something has to hold every span of a trace until the trace is complete, and every span of one trace must reach the same decision-maker. In a Collector deployment that means two tiers: agents on each node forward to a gateway pool through a load-balancing exporter that routes by trace id, and only the gateway runs the tail-sampling processor. Get the routing wrong and you do not get an error, you get quietly fragmented traces.

# tier 1 (agent): route by trace id so every span of a trace lands on one gateway
exporters:
  loadbalancing:
    routing_key: traceID
    protocol: { otlp: { tls: { insecure: true } } }
    resolver: { k8s: { service: otel-gateway.observability } }

# tier 2 (gateway): the only place the tail sampler runs
processors:
  tail_sampling:
    decision_wait: 30s          # spans buffered in memory for this long
    num_traces: 200000          # bounded buffer; overflow is silent loss
    policies:
      - { name: errors,   type: status_code,   status_code: { status_codes: [ERROR] } }
      - { name: slow,     type: latency,       latency: { threshold_ms: 1500 } }
      - { name: baseline, type: probabilistic, probabilistic: { sampling_percentage: 1 } }

The number that decided it was memory, not money. At our span rate, thirty seconds of buffering was roughly 14 GB across the gateway pool — and decision_wait is a correctness constraint, not a tuning knob: a trace whose last span arrives after the window is judged on what arrived, so a slow async leg gets classified as fast. Our batch jobs had spans arriving ninety seconds apart, which meant tail sampling would have systematically discarded exactly the traces from the subsystem with the worst latency. That is the trade I put to the architecture group: head sampling loses randomly, tail sampling loses with a bias you have to enumerate.

What we shipped was neither, purely: consistent probability head sampling at 5% as the statistical baseline; tail sampling in the gateway for errors and slow requests on the synchronous request path only; and the long-running batch pipeline left at 100% head sampling with short retention, because its volume was small and its traces were the ones we needed whole. Trace volume rose 3.4× and the bill 1.9× — baseline retention dropped to three days to pay for it — and "trace not found" during incidents went from routine to rare. What I would do differently: I sized decision_wait from the p99 of span duration, which is the wrong input. The right one is the distribution of trace completion time, and I did not have that number until I built a metric for it in week three, after the first round of quietly truncated traces. The adaptive_tail_sampling processor in Collector contrib — whose adaptive-throughput algorithm had its cold-start and overflow handling fixed in v0.160.0, September 2026 — would take the "pick a percentage and hope" part off my hands, and it is the first thing I would evaluate today.

The classic mistake is presenting tail sampling as strictly better — it is not, and naming its bias is most of the signal.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does tail sampling force load balancing by trace id?

Because the decision is a function of the whole trace, and the whole trace only exists if every span is in one process's buffer. Spans from one request come from many services on many nodes, so with plain round-robin each gateway sees a random subset and makes a different, uninformed decision — you get fragments, not traces. Routing by trace id makes the assignment deterministic, which is why the load-balancing exporter exists and why the gateway tier is stateful in a way the agent tier is not. The corollary people miss is that this makes gateway scaling disruptive: changing pool membership reshuffles the mapping, and traces in flight across the change can be split.

At what scale does this break?

It breaks on memory before it breaks on throughput. Buffered spans scale with span rate times decision_wait, so a traffic spike and a latency spike multiply — precisely when you most want the traces — and past num_traces the buffer evicts silently. The second wall is trace shape: a long-running trace, a streaming connection or a batch job does not fit in any sane window, so it needs a different strategy rather than a bigger window. My rule is that tail sampling suits a synchronous request path with bounded trace duration, and is the wrong tool for anything whose trace can outlive a deployment.

How do you still compute correct error rates when you sample?

You don't compute rates from sampled traces at all if you can avoid it — rates come from metrics, which are counted on every request. Where you must use spans, you need consistent probability sampling so each kept span carries its sampling probability and can be reweighted; OpenTelemetry's consistent samplers record that in the tracestate and the Collector can propagate it. What you cannot do is take a tail-sampled dataset that keeps 100% of errors and 1% of successes and compute an error rate from it — a mistake I have seen shipped to an executive dashboard. Keeping a probabilistic baseline policy alongside the interesting-trace policies exists exactly so there is an unbiased subset to reason from.

What would you do differently now, in 2026?

Two things. I would evaluate the adaptive tail-sampling processor in Collector contrib rather than hand-tuning fixed percentages: it targets a throughput budget and adjusts, and its cold-start and overflow behaviour was fixed in v0.160.0, which was my objection to it a year ago. And I would treat the sampling policy as a product decision per route rather than a platform default — checkout keeps everything, a health endpoint keeps nothing — with the platform's job being to make that expressible in one place instead of choosing a global number.

How did you get teams to agree, given it cost them agent overhead and money?

I stopped arguing about sampling and started arguing about incidents. I pulled the last twenty postmortems, counted how many contained a "we could not find the trace" line — eleven — and put minutes lost against each. That reframed the spend as MTTR rather than as a telemetry bill. For the teams worried about agent overhead I ran a load test showing the added cost sat in the exporter, not on the request path, since the SDK hands spans to a batch processor asynchronously. And I gave every team an opt-out with a default: set your own policy, and if you set nothing you inherit the platform's. Nobody used the opt-out, but it turned a mandate into a default.

03The outage nobody was paged for

tier · staff production incident / outage

Example — replace with your own

Forty-three minutes of broken checkout, found by a customer on social media, with every alert green throughout. That is the incident I tell, because the interesting part is not the bug — a payment provider rotated a certificate and broke our client's chain — it is that we had built a monitoring system structurally incapable of noticing.

It was invisible for three compounding reasons. Our alerts were cause-based: CPU, memory, pod restarts, database connections, 5xx rate at the ingress. The payment client caught the TLS exception, logged it at warning, and returned a domain result object, so the endpoint answered 200 OK with a declined status. Zero 5xx. The health endpoint reported healthy because it checked that the process was up and the database answered SELECT 1 — deliberately not touching the payment provider, because two years earlier a dependency check inside a readiness probe had caused a restart storm. And the one metric that could have caught it, a counter of successful payments, existed but was alerted with a static threshold — "fewer than 10 in 5 minutes" — which never fired because the number was in the hundreds. It needed a comparison against expectation, not against a constant.

During the incident the fastest thing I did was ignore all of that and query the log stream for the decline reason, which took ninety seconds once someone thought to look and gave us the certificate error verbatim. The slow part was the forty-one minutes before anyone looked. So the postmortem action was not "alert on certificate expiry" — that is the cause-based reflex, and it would have caught this outage and no other. We defined an SLO on what the user experiences, a successful checkout, and alerted on error-budget burn rate with the standard multi-window pair: a fast window that pages when the budget burns roughly fourteen times faster than sustainable, and a slow window for the quiet bleed a fast window never sees.

groups:
  - name: checkout-slo
    rules:
      # page: ~2% of a 30d budget in 1h. The short window guards against one bad scrape.
      - alert: CheckoutBudgetBurnFast
        expr: |
          checkout:error_ratio:rate1h  > (14.4 * 0.001)
          and
          checkout:error_ratio:rate5m  > (14.4 * 0.001)
        labels: { severity: page }
      # ticket: the slow bleed that never trips a fast window.
      - alert: CheckoutBudgetBurnSlow
        expr: |
          checkout:error_ratio:rate6h  > (6 * 0.001)
          and
          checkout:error_ratio:rate30m > (6 * 0.001)
        labels: { severity: ticket }

We also made the failure visible where it happened: the payment client stopped folding transport errors into a domain result silently and started setting the span status to ERROR with an exception event, so the trace tells the truth even when the HTTP response is a 200. That is the general lesson I now repeat in design reviews — a caught exception that becomes a business outcome is an observability decision, not only an error-handling one. The fast burn-rate alert has paged three times since, each time correctly, each time before support noticed. What I would do differently: I had known about the static threshold for six months and had it on a backlog labelled "low", because writing burn-rate rules requires agreeing an SLO and I did not want the meeting. The forty-three minutes bought me the meeting.

What the interviewer is really probing. Incident-command judgment, and whether your postmortems produce systemic change or a list of symptom patches. They want detection, diagnosis and mitigation separated cleanly, customer impact quantified, and an action that generalises. Two mistakes are common: making the story about the clever root cause rather than the detection gap, and telling it with no ownership — the outage was "the vendor's fault", when the forty-one minutes were entirely yours.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why burn-rate alerts rather than a simple "error ratio above 1%"?

Because a static ratio has no notion of how much failure you can afford, so it is simultaneously too noisy for a brief spike and too quiet for a slow bleed. Burn rate normalises against the error budget: a 14.4× burn on a 99.9% target means a 30-day budget is gone in about two days, which is worth waking someone for, while 1% for ninety seconds on low overnight traffic is not. The two-window structure makes it usable — the long window decides significance, the short window makes the alert reset quickly once the fix lands, so you are not paged for an hour afterwards. The cost is that you must first agree what "good" means, and that conversation is the hard part, not the PromQL.

Should the health endpoint have checked the payment provider?

No, and that earlier decision was right. A readiness probe that fails on a downstream dependency converts a partial outage into a total one: the orchestrator pulls every pod out of the load balancer, the pods restart, and you lose the 80% of traffic that never touches that dependency. The correct split is that liveness and readiness answer "should traffic come here", while dependency health is a separate signal you scrape and alert on. In .NET terms that means tagging the payment check out of the ready tag and exposing it as a metric instead of wiring it into the probe path.

What did you trade away by moving to symptom-based alerting?

Diagnostic directness. A cause-based alert tells you what to fix; a symptom-based alert tells you only that users are hurting, so the first minutes of every page now go to triage. We paid that back by making triage fast rather than by keeping the cause alerts as pages: the SLO alert links to a dashboard whose panels are the top dependencies by error contribution and by latency contribution, with exemplars into traces. The cause-based rules still exist, demoted to tickets and to context in the incident channel. The honest trade is fewer, better pages in exchange for slightly more thinking per page.

At what scale does the multi-window burn-rate approach break?

At low traffic. Burn rate is a ratio, and at ten requests an hour one failure is a 10% error ratio, so the fast window becomes a random-number generator. Below a few hundred events per window you need a different instrument: an absolute-count condition alongside the ratio, a synthetic prober to manufacture a traffic floor, or a longer window and the acceptance that detection takes tens of minutes. It also breaks when one SLO covers heterogeneous traffic — mixing a cheap read endpoint at 10,000 rps with checkout at 20 rps means checkout can fail completely without moving the ratio, which is the argument for per-journey SLOs rather than per-service ones.

How do you keep the "200 with a failure inside" pattern from coming back?

By putting it in code everybody shares rather than in a guideline. Our HTTP and messaging clients live in an internal package, and that package now sets the span status to ERROR and records an exception event whenever it converts a transport failure into a domain result — you have to opt out explicitly, with a comment. We added an architecture test that fails the build when a catch block in the integration layer returns without touching the current activity. And in review I ask one question of any try/catch around an I/O boundary: "if this fires a thousand times an hour, what turns red?" If the answer is "a log line", it is not observable.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why send everything through a Collector instead of exporting straight to the backend?

Because it decouples applications from the destination, and the destination is what changes. With a Collector in the path, switching backends, adding a second one, redacting an attribute, dropping a noisy metric or changing sampling is a config change in one place instead of a redeploy of thirty services. It also gives you a buffer with retry and a persistent queue at the edge, which is what keeps telemetry alive through a bad ten minutes at the backend. The cost is a component you now operate, with its own capacity and its own monitoring — but the alternative is a hard-coded vendor in every binary, which is the position we were migrating out of.

What breaks when semantic conventions change under you?

Everything downstream that named an attribute: dashboard queries, alert expressions, recording rules, saved searches, any ETL into a warehouse. Nothing errors — a query for http.method simply returns no data, so alerts silently stop firing, which is worse than a crash. That is why the dual-emit mode exists and why the order is instrument, then consume, then de-duplicate, never the reverse. Schema URLs are the longer-term answer, with telemetry carrying the semconv version it was produced against so a backend can translate, but support is uneven, so I still treat a mapping table plus a dual-emit window as the practical path.

At what scale does the two-tier Collector topology become the bottleneck?

The agent tier scales with nodes and is rarely the problem; the gateway tier is, because that is where the stateful work happens — tail sampling, span metrics, heavy OTTL transforms — and where every service's traffic converges. The signal to watch is the exporter queue: otelcol_exporter_queue_size approaching capacity, then refused spans as the memory limiter starts shedding. We ran the gateway with a memory limiter set well under the container limit, autoscaled on queue depth rather than CPU, and kept expensive processors off the agent tier so a node agent could never become a per-node bottleneck. Past a certain size you split gateways per signal, because logs and traces have very different burst profiles.

The team that lost the query-plan attribute — how did that end?

I did not tell them to live without it. We looked at what they actually used it for, which was finding missing indexes after a schema change, and it turned out they needed it twice a quarter, not continuously. So we replaced a proprietary always-on attribute with a targeted workflow: the database's own slow-query log, plus a span attribute carrying a normalised statement fingerprint so they could correlate. It was a worse feature and a better trade, and saying that out loud to them mattered — the failure mode in these migrations is pretending nothing was lost, which costs you the credibility you need for the next one.

Would you do a big-bang cutover if you had to do it again?

No, but I would shorten the dual-run. Six weeks of double-emitting attributes and paying two backends is real money and real noise, and its length was driven by dashboard migration — exactly the part I later automated. With the rewrite script in hand I would run a two-week dual-run per wave, three or four waves grouped by team rather than by service, and set a hard deletion date for the old attribute names, because the failure mode of dual-emit is that it never ends and you are still paying for it two years later.

05"Keep 100% of the traces"

tier · senior technical disagreement

Example — replace with your own

A senior colleague on the platform team wanted metrics pushed over OTLP everywhere and Prometheus scraping deleted, and in the same design review he wanted 100% trace retention because "storage is cheap". I disagreed with both, we argued about it for two weeks in comments, and the reason I tell this story is that the resolution had almost nothing to do with who was right — it came from making the decision criteria explicit instead of trading preferences.

His case was real. Pull-based scraping does not work for short-lived jobs: a cron pod that lives forty seconds either never gets scraped or gets scraped once, and the industry answer — a push gateway — is a shared mutable blob of stale metrics that outlives the job that wrote it, which is worse than the problem. It also does not work across network boundaries you do not control, and it forces service discovery to know about every target. Push over OTLP fixes all of that, and since Prometheus 3.0 there is a native OTLP receiver so you do not even need a translation layer — though it ships disabled by default, deliberately, because Prometheus has no authentication of its own and accepting inbound writes is a decision you should have to make consciously.

My case was about what you lose. In a pull model the scrape itself is a health signal: Prometheus synthesises an up series per target, and a target that stops answering is instantly visible as up == 0. In a push model, a service that has crashed and a service that is healthy but quiet look identical — absence of data, and absence is the hardest thing to alert on without generating noise. Pull also gives you a natural rate limiter, since the server decides how often it asks, whereas a push firehose from a misbehaving service can take down the ingest path for everyone. That is not a theoretical worry; it is the most common way a metrics backend falls over.

What broke the deadlock was that I stopped defending scraping and wrote down the question instead: what property of the workload decides this? The answer was lifetime. Anything long-lived and in-cluster gets scraped, because the free liveness signal is worth more than the flexibility. Anything short-lived, out-of-cluster or bursty pushes through the Collector, which enforces the limits the server would otherwise enforce for us. We put that in a one-page decision record with the two failure modes named, and it ended the argument in a forty-minute meeting after two weeks of comment threads. On 100% trace retention I lost, partially and correctly: he was right that his service's traces were small and its debugging workflow depended on completeness, so it kept everything at seven-day retention while the estate default stayed sampled.

What I would do differently is the two weeks. I was arguing from experience — "I have seen a push firehose take down an ingest tier" — which is unfalsifiable and sounds like seniority-as-argument. The moment I converted it into a criterion the other person could apply themselves, the disagreement dissolved. I also should have said out loud, early, that his short-lived-jobs problem was genuinely unsolved in my preferred model, because holding that back made me look like I was defending a position rather than solving a problem.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can disagree without making it about status, and whether you update. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is absence of a metric so much harder to alert on than a bad value?

Because "no data" is ambiguous: it could be a crashed process, a network partition, a scaled-to-zero deployment, a renamed label, or a perfectly healthy service with nothing to report. In a pull model that ambiguity mostly collapses, because the scrape has a result whether or not the application had anything to say, and up == 0 distinguishes "I asked and got nothing" from "I did not ask". In a push model you have to manufacture the equivalent — a heartbeat gauge emitted unconditionally at a fixed interval — and then alert on its staleness, which needs care because most query engines return an empty result rather than a stale one, and an alert expression over an empty result does not fire. That last detail is what bites teams: the rule looks correct and is silently unfirable.

At what scale does push become a liability?

When a client can generate more than the ingest tier can absorb and nothing in between says no. A misconfigured service that starts emitting a per-request label pushes a cardinality bomb straight into storage, and unlike a scrape you cannot fix it centrally by editing a relabel rule — you either fix the client or drop at the Collector. That is the main reason I insisted every push goes through a Collector we operate: limits, redaction and attribute dropping live there, so the platform keeps a place to stand. At very large scale you also need per-tenant quotas, because otherwise one team's incident becomes everyone's ingest outage.

Prometheus can receive OTLP directly now. Why keep the Collector?

Because the receiver solves transport, not governance. Prometheus 3's OTLP receiver is genuinely useful — it removes a hop for teams already emitting OTLP — but it ships off by default precisely because Prometheus has no authentication layer of its own, so exposing it means putting something in front anyway. Once you have that something, you may as well have a Collector, where you also get redaction, attribute normalisation, cardinality limits, routing to more than one backend and a persistent queue. The version where applications push straight into Prometheus is the version where every future change is a redeploy of every service.

You conceded on 100% retention for one service. Wasn't that inconsistent?

It was a deliberate exception with a stated cost, which is different from inconsistency. His service produced a small fraction of the estate's spans, its debugging workflow genuinely needed complete traces, and seven-day retention bounded the spend — so the exception cost us a few percent of the bill and bought the platform's credibility with the team most likely to work around us. What makes an exception healthy is that it is written down, priced, and has an owner who will be asked about it at the next review. What makes it rot is granting it verbally and never revisiting it.

How do you argue with someone more senior than you about this?

The same way, with one addition: I make the decision reversible or bounded before I make it contentious. If I can propose a two-week experiment with a metric that will settle it — push one service, watch ingest and alerting behaviour — the conversation stops being about who is more experienced. Where that is not possible, I ask them to write down what would change their mind, and I write mine down first so it is not a trap. If we still disagree and they own the call, I disagree and commit visibly, because a team that can see me implement a decision I argued against trusts my next objection more, not less.

06The trace that stopped at the queue

tier · senior nasty debugging session

Example — replace with your own

Orders were occasionally confirmed twice, roughly one in nine thousand, and every trace ended at the same place: the API span that published the message, then nothing. The consumer's work appeared in the backend as a separate root trace with no parent, so the two halves of the story existed but could not be joined, and correlating them by order id across two systems took about twenty minutes per case. Three days of this before I found it, and the bug was two bugs.

The first was propagation across the broker. Trace context travels in the W3C traceparent header, and HTTP clients inject it automatically because the instrumentation owns the request object. A message broker has no such convention unless the producer explicitly injects the context into message headers and the consumer extracts it. Ours injected — but only on the path that used the shared publisher. One service published through a hand-rolled helper written before the shared library existed, and that helper simply did not carry context. Nothing errored; you just got orphan traces, and orphan traces look enough like normal traces that nobody had questioned them for a year.

// The producer must inject; nothing does it for you across a broker.
var props = new Dictionary<string, object>();
Propagators.DefaultTextMapPropagator.Inject(
    new PropagationContext(Activity.Current!.Context, Baggage.Current),
    props,
    (carrier, key, value) => carrier[key] = value);   // writes traceparent/tracestate

// The consumer extracts, then starts a CONSUMER span linked to the producer.
var parent = Propagators.DefaultTextMapPropagator.Extract(default, message.Headers,
    (carrier, key) => carrier.TryGetValue(key, out var v) ? new[] { (string)v } : Array.Empty<string>());

using var activity = Source.StartActivity(
    "orders process", ActivityKind.Consumer, parent.ActivityContext);

The second bug is the one I still tell juniors about, because it is invisible in code review. Inside the consumer, the handler offloaded a slow step with Task.Run onto a custom scheduler that had been configured with ExecutionContext suppressed, for performance reasons, years earlier. Activity.Current in .NET flows on the AsyncLocal mechanism, which rides on ExecutionContext — suppress the flow and the ambient current activity is silently null inside that continuation. So spans created there had no parent even when propagation had worked perfectly. The same class of bug exists everywhere: a thread pool that does not capture context, a queue of work items handed between threads, a JVM executor without context propagation, a JavaScript callback outside the async hooks scope. Context is ambient state, and any code that hands work to another thread without carrying it will silently break your traces.

Once the traces joined, the duplicate confirmations took forty minutes to explain: the consumer acknowledged after a slow downstream call, the broker's visibility timeout was shorter than that call's p99, and the message was redelivered while the first attempt was still running. That is a textbook idempotency bug, and the only reason it took three days is that I could not see one trace containing both deliveries. What I would do differently is stop treating orphan traces as cosmetic. I now alert on them: a ratio of root spans that ought to have parents, per service, which would have surfaced this a year earlier for the price of one recording rule. And I check context flow in the same review pass as thread handoffs — if a diff introduces a scheduler, a channel or a background queue, I ask where the activity goes.

What the interviewer is really probing. Debugging method, not the answer: how you narrowed a rare, non-reproducible fault, and whether you distinguished the bug from the reason it was hard to find. The strongest version separates those explicitly — "the defect was an ack-timing bug, the three days were a telemetry gap" — and ends with the detector you added. Candidates lose points by narrating every dead end in order; compress the search, keep the decisive step.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Parent-child or span links for a message consumer? Why?

It depends on the fan-in. For a single message consumed once, a consumer span parented to the producer's context gives you one readable trace, which is what you want. For a batch consumer that pulls fifty messages from thirty different traces, a single parent is a lie — you would be claiming one causal chain where there are thirty — so the batch span gets a link per message instead, and each link carries its own trace context. The practical consequence is that links are the right tool whenever a span has many causes, and the practical cost is that most UIs render links far less usefully than parentage, so people reach for a false parent because it looks better. That is a trade worth naming out loud rather than making silently.

How would you detect broken propagation before an incident does?

Measure it as a ratio rather than hunting for it. Every service knows which of its spans are roots, and for a service that only ever handles work initiated elsewhere, a root span is by definition a propagation failure — so a recording rule over the share of root spans per service, alerted when it exceeds a small threshold, catches the whole class. A second, cheaper check runs in CI: an integration test that publishes a message and asserts the consumer's span shares the trace id, which turns propagation from a property nobody owns into a test that fails. I would add both; the alert catches drift in production, the test stops it being reintroduced.

The team suppressed ExecutionContext flow for performance. Were they wrong?

They were not wrong when they did it, and they were wrong to leave it undocumented. Suppressing flow avoids capturing and restoring the context on every scheduled item, which was measurable on their hot path at the time; the cost is that everything ambient — the current activity, the logging scope, the culture, anything on AsyncLocal — silently disappears inside those continuations. Today I would measure again before assuming the win still exists, since the runtime has improved considerably, and if it does I would restore the trace context explicitly at the boundary rather than the whole context. The general rule is that suppressing ambient state is a local optimisation with a global blast radius, so it needs a comment naming what it breaks.

At what scale does adding trace context to messages become a problem?

When headers become a meaningful share of the payload. A traceparent is about fifty-five bytes and tracestate can grow, so on a topic carrying two-hundred-byte events at very high rate that is real network and storage cost, and some brokers charge per byte. Baggage is the sharper edge: it propagates arbitrary key-value data to every downstream service, so it grows without bound if teams treat it as a place to stash things, and it crosses trust boundaries — anything in baggage is visible to every service in the chain. My rule is trace context always, baggage rarely and with a documented key list, and never anything user-identifying in either.

What would you do differently now?

I would make the shared publisher the only way to publish, rather than the recommended way. The legacy helper survived because nothing forced the issue; an architecture test banning direct broker-client use outside the shared package would have deleted this bug class before it existed. I would also fix the ordering of my own investigation: I spent day one on the duplicate-confirmation logic and day two on the broker's redelivery settings, when the fastest move was to ask why the trace ended, because a missing edge in a trace is itself a defect. Now "can I see this end to end?" is the first question in an investigation, before any hypothesis about the bug.

Example — replace with your own

Our metrics backend fell over on a Tuesday afternoon, and it fell over because of a two-line pull request merged on the Monday. Somebody had added a label to a counter — the customer identifier, so a support team could see per-customer request rates — and within eighteen hours the active series count went from 380,000 to 4.2 million, the ingester hit its memory limit, got OOM-killed, replayed its write-ahead log, hit the limit again, and stayed in that loop. We lost metrics for the whole estate, during business hours, because of one dimension on one counter.

The mechanism is worth being precise about, because it is the single most useful thing to understand about metric storage. A time series is identified by its complete label set: metric name plus every label value. Add a label with 12,000 distinct values and you do not add 12,000 series, you multiply the existing combinations — that counter already had route, method and status labels, so the real multiplier landed us near four million. Each active series costs memory in the head block whether or not anyone queries it, and it costs again in the index. Worse, that customer id was not even stable: some of it was per-session, so we had churn as well as volume, and churn is more expensive than a large but stable set because the index keeps the old series until the retention window rolls past.

# the top offenders, right now
topk(10, count by (__name__)({__name__=~".+"}))

# how many distinct values does one label have on one metric?
count(count by (customer_id) (http_server_request_duration_seconds_count))

# churn: series created per hour is the number that predicts the OOM
sum(rate(prometheus_tsdb_head_series_created_total[1h]))

Mitigation took twenty minutes once we knew: drop the label at ingest and let the head block age out. The durable fix took a month and had three layers. At the Collector, an explicit allow-list of metric attributes, so a new label has to be added deliberately in platform config rather than accidentally in application code. At the backend, per-tenant series limits, so one team's mistake is now their own outage rather than everyone's — that change was more political than technical and I had to argue it twice. And in the applications, a rule with a rationale rather than a prohibition: a label is for something you group by, an attribute is for something you filter to. Customer id is a filter, so it belongs on spans and logs, which are indexed for high cardinality, not on a counter. The support team's actual question — "is this customer seeing errors?" — was answered better by a trace query anyway, and in about the same number of clicks.

What I would do differently is obvious in hindsight: the guard rails existed as documentation and should have existed as a limit. I had written the cardinality guidance six months earlier, and it did nothing, because a document does not have an opinion at merge time. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is cardinality the fundamental constraint in a metrics system?

Because the storage model is built for a bounded set of series sampled frequently, and it pays for every distinct series whether or not anyone asks about it. A series is the full label set, so dimensions multiply rather than add, and the in-memory index plus the head block scale with the number of active series, not with the number of samples. That is the opposite of a log or trace store, which is built for many distinct records queried by selective predicates and pays mostly at query time. Which is why the correct answer to "I need per-customer visibility" is almost always "then that dimension belongs on a signal indexed for high cardinality", not "then buy a bigger metrics cluster".

At what scale do per-tenant limits stop being enough?

When the aggregate of everyone's legitimate limits exceeds what the cluster can hold, which happens sooner than people expect because limits get set optimistically and never revisited. At that point you need either a real capacity model — series budget per team, reviewed like a cost budget — or a different architecture, typically sharding by tenant so noisy neighbours are physically separated. The other thing that breaks is the escape hatch: teams that hit a limit start aggregating away dimensions they actually need, and you get quiet loss of signal rather than a loud outage, so the limit needs an alert when a tenant is near it and a review path, not just a hard stop.

Do native histograms or exemplars change this calculus?

They help on two different axes. Native histograms, stable since Prometheus 3.8.0, collapse a bucket ladder into one sparse series, which removes a per-bucket multiplier that used to make latency metrics the most expensive thing in the system. Exemplars help more subtly: they let a low-cardinality metric carry a pointer into a high-cardinality store, so you keep the aggregate cheap while retaining a path to individual examples — which is exactly the workflow the customer-id label was reaching for. Neither changes the fundamental rule that label values multiply, so both are relief rather than escape.

What did you trade away with the attribute allow-list?

Speed for teams, and some goodwill. Adding a legitimately useful dimension went from a two-line pull request in their own repo to a change in platform config with our review, which is real friction and it slowed a few good ideas down. We mitigated it by keeping the turnaround under a day and by making the deny path informative — the Collector logs what it dropped and why, so a team discovers the rule at the moment they hit it rather than in a wiki. If I ran it again I would allow a per-team self-service budget instead of a review queue, so the platform sets the ceiling and teams spend it themselves.

How did you handle the person who merged the change?

Directly and privately first, and then not at all in public. In the incident channel and the postmortem I described the change, not the author, because the review had two approvals and the platform had no guard rail — the system permitted it, so the system is what failed. Privately I walked them through the cardinality mechanism, and they ended up writing the CI budget check, which is the outcome I actually wanted: the person closest to the mistake usually builds the best prevention. The thing I watch for in myself is the temptation to be visibly gracious, which is its own kind of blame; the goal is that the next person tells us early, not that everyone admires how I handled it.

I checked the neighbouring services and it was not one bug. Password-reset URLs were in url.full with the token in the query string. An HTTP client span captured an Authorization header on one internal call. A log line in the payments service printed a whole request object, and the object had a serialiser that did not know about the card's last four digits. Every one of those was searchable by roughly two hundred engineers, and the telemetry backend had a ninety-day retention.

The thing I had to make people understand — and it was the actual work, not the redaction — is that a telemetry store is a data store. It is subject to the same access control, the same retention rules, and the same erasure obligations as the production database, and it is usually more widely readable. Nobody would ship a feature that let all engineering query customer emails from the orders table; we had built exactly that by accident, one attribute at a time, and it had passed every security review because reviews look at applications, not at the exhaust.

The fix had a fast layer and a slow one. Fast, that afternoon: redaction in the Collector, because the Collector is the one place every signal passes through and a config change there needs no application deploy. OTTL made the rules explicit and reviewable.

processors:
  transform/redact:
    error_mode: ignore
    trace_statements:
      # never keep credentials, at all
      - delete_key(span.attributes, "http.request.header.authorization")
      # strip query strings; the path is useful, the token is a liability
      - replace_pattern(span.attributes["url.full"], "\\?.*$", "?[redacted]")
      # keep the shape of the query, drop the values
      - replace_all_patterns(span.attributes, "value",
          "[\\w.%+-]+@[\\w.-]+\\.[A-Za-z]{2,}", "[email]")
    log_statements:
      - set(log.body, "[redacted]") where IsMatch(log.body, "\\b\\d{13,19}\\b")

Slow, over the following quarter: fix it at the source, because Collector redaction is a net, not a policy. Parameterised statements got captured without their values — the semantic conventions are explicit that the statement attribute should not carry sensitive parameter values, and the .NET and JVM instrumentations expose a switch for that rather than requiring a regular expression downstream. The password-reset flow moved its token from the query string to a POST body, which was a genuine security improvement independent of telemetry, since query strings end up in proxy logs and browser history too. And we added a "what does this emit?" line to the design-review template, with three example spans, so new features declare their telemetry the way they declare their database schema.

The uncomfortable part I always include: we could not cleanly delete the historical data. Spans live in a columnar store optimised for append and range scans, not for "find every record mentioning this person and remove it", so the honest options were a full reprocess or waiting out retention. We shortened retention on the affected signal to thirty days, documented the exposure, and told the privacy team rather than quietly hoping. What I would do differently is not the discovery, it is the sequence: I built the redaction rules first because they felt productive, and only afterwards did the inventory of what we were actually collecting. The inventory should come first — you cannot redact a field you have not enumerated, and two of the four leaks were found by the inventory, not by me.

Candidates weaken this story by making it a clever-regex anecdote, or by implying they fixed everything with no residual risk.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why redact at the Collector rather than in the application?

Because of the time constant. A Collector rule ships in minutes across every service and applies to code you do not own — third-party instrumentation, a vendored library, a service whose team is on holiday — whereas a source fix needs a change, a review and a deploy per service. That makes the Collector the right place for the emergency stop and the wrong place for the permanent policy, because a central regular expression is a lossy guess: it can miss a format, it costs CPU on every span, and it silently mangles legitimate data when it over-matches. So I use both deliberately — the Collector as a backstop that stays in place, the source as the actual fix — and I treat any rule that is still catching things a quarter later as an open bug against that service.

What do the semantic conventions say about this, and does that help?

They help more than people expect, because the conventions are opinionated about sensitivity: the database conventions say the query text attribute should carry the statement without sensitive parameter values, the HTTP conventions make header capture opt-in per header rather than automatic, and the URL attributes distinguish the full URL from path and query so you can keep the useful part. Following them is not a compliance story, it is a design story — the conventions encode which fields are safe to keep, and instrumentation that follows them gives you a safe default. Where they do not help is your own attributes: anything you add yourself is outside the conventions and outside anyone's default, which is precisely where all four of our leaks lived.

At what scale does regex-based redaction stop working?

Fast, on two axes. Cost: pattern matching runs on every attribute of every span, so at high span rates a handful of expensive patterns is measurable gateway CPU, and people respond by loosening them. Correctness: personal data does not have a reliable shape — a name, an address line, a free-text support note, an internal identifier that is personal in one jurisdiction — so pattern matching gets the formatted things (emails, cards, tokens) and misses everything else. Past a certain size the only approach that holds is an allow-list at the source: services declare what they emit, anything undeclared is dropped, and redaction becomes a safety net for the declared set rather than the primary control.

You couldn't delete the historical spans. How did you justify that?

By making it a decision someone accountable took with the facts, rather than a technical limitation I absorbed quietly. I documented what was collected, over what window, who could read it and what deletion would cost — a full reprocess of a columnar store against the operational risk of that reprocess — and put shortening retention on the affected signal alongside it as the cheaper mitigation. The privacy team chose; that is their call, not mine. The general principle I would defend in any interview is that engineers should surface residual risk with options and costs, and should not be the ones deciding unilaterally that an exposure is acceptable.

How do you stop this recurring without slowing every team down?

Make the safe path the default path and put the check where the cost is lowest. Our shared instrumentation package sets the sensitive switches correctly and captures no headers unless a service opts in per header, so a team gets a safe configuration by doing nothing. The review gate is one line in a template — name the three most interesting spans this feature emits — which takes a minute and catches the "we added a custom attribute" case that no default can cover. And there is a scheduled scan against staging telemetry for the formatted patterns, so drift shows up as a ticket rather than as a discovery a year later. None of that is heavy; the leaks came from absence of any check at all, not from a check being too light.

09Deleting 1,400 log statements

tier · staff tech-debt paydown / refactor

Example — replace with your own

The order service emitted about 90 log lines per request. Not one of them was useless to the person who wrote it, and collectively they were close to worthless: to reconstruct what had happened you read a wall of "entering CalculateTotals" and "got 3 items" interleaved from forty concurrent requests, with no correlation identifier, and by the time you had grepped it into a shape you could read, the incident had moved on. Logs were also our largest telemetry line item, and roughly two-thirds of the volume came from three services doing exactly this.

What I proposed was not "log less". Log-reduction projects framed as cost-cutting fail, because the moment there is an incident someone turns everything back on and nobody ever turns it off. I framed it as a change of shape: one structured event per unit of work, plus spans for the timeline, instead of a narrative of print statements. The canonical event carries everything you would have needed to answer a question about that request — identifiers, sizes, decisions taken, durations, outcome — as fields you can query, so "show me every order over 20 items where the tax service was slow" is one query rather than a grep and a guess. The spans carry the timeline the interleaved logs were badly approximating, and because the log record and the spans share a trace id, the two are one click apart.

// before: nine lines, unstructured, uncorrelated, one level for all of it
_log.LogInformation("entering CalculateTotals");
_log.LogInformation("got " + items.Count + " items");
_log.LogInformation("tax service call starting");
// ... six more, then somewhere far away, the outcome

// after: the timeline is the span; the facts are one queryable event
using var activity = Source.StartActivity("orders.checkout");
activity?.SetTag("order.item_count", items.Count);
activity?.SetTag("order.currency", currency);

_log.OrderCompleted(orderId, items.Count, total, taxMs, outcome); // source-generated
// [LoggerMessage(Level = LogLevel.Information,
//   Message = "order completed {OrderId} items={ItemCount} total={Total} tax_ms={TaxMs} outcome={Outcome}")]

Two mechanical details did most of the work. First, source-generated logging: the LoggerMessage attribute produces a strongly-typed method with no boxing and no string interpolation on the hot path, and — the part people miss — the fields stay separate all the way into the backend instead of being flattened into a message string, which is what makes them queryable. Second, the OpenTelemetry log bridge: the logging framework's records are exported as OTLP log records that automatically carry the current trace and span id, so correlation is a property of the pipeline rather than something each developer remembers to add. That is the difference between structured logging as a convention and structured logging as a guarantee.

We did it service by service over a quarter, with a rule that made it reviewable: every deleted log line had to be answerable either by a span or by a field on the canonical event, and if it was answerable by neither, it stayed. About 1,400 statements went; roughly 60 stayed, mostly genuine warnings and a few debug lines behind a level nobody enables in production. Log volume fell 71%, the bill fell with it, and the number people actually noticed was that median time to answer a support question about a specific order went from about fifteen minutes to under two. What I would do differently: I let the first service take five weeks because I reviewed every deletion myself, which did not scale and made me the bottleneck. On the second service I wrote the rule down and reviewed samples instead, and it took nine days. I also under-communicated at the start — two engineers thought I was proposing we stop logging, and I had to spend credibility undoing an impression I created by leading with the cost number instead of with the query.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can pay down debt without a rewrite, and whether you sold it on the right axis. The cost saving is the weakest part of this story; the strong part is that you changed what questions the system can answer and had a reviewable rule for what could be deleted. Candidates trip by describing a mass deletion with no criterion — that reads as reckless — or by claiming a percentage saving with no operational outcome attached.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Wide events or traces — aren't you describing the same thing twice?

They overlap and they are not redundant. A span is a timing structure: it has a duration, a parent, and it is sampled, which makes it excellent for "where did the time go" and unreliable as a system of record. A canonical event is a fact about a completed unit of work, usually unsampled, which makes it the thing you count, aggregate and audit against. Where they genuinely converge is that a rich root span with thirty attributes is close to a canonical event, and if your backend keeps root spans unsampled you can collapse the two — I have done that, and it works when the backend supports it. What I would not do is keep both and let them disagree, because two sources of truth about the same request is a debugging tax, not a feature.

How do you handle the "we need debug logs during an incident" argument?

By making verbosity a runtime decision rather than a deploy-time one. Log level per category, changeable without a restart, plus a rule that anything enabled during an incident has an expiry — we used a configuration change with a timer, so it turns itself off. The second half is making the default good enough that the argument rarely comes up: a canonical event with thirty fields answers most of what people used debug logs for, and trace context means they can pull the timeline instead. Where verbose logging is genuinely the only tool, usually a third-party library's internals, I would rather enable it for one pod or a sampled slice of traffic than for the fleet.

At what scale does the canonical-event approach break?

When the unit of work is not well defined, or when the event grows without bound. Batch processing is the first case: a job that handles a hundred thousand records has no natural per-request event, so you end up choosing between one enormous event per job and one per record, and neither is right — the answer is usually per-record events at a sampled rate plus per-job aggregates. The second case is field creep: canonical events attract fields because adding one is easy, and a two-hundred-field event costs real money at volume and stops being readable. So the event needs an owner and a review, the same as a database schema, which is exactly the framing I use when someone wants to add their fifth field this month.

What did you trade away?

Serendipity. A wall of unstructured logs occasionally contains the one line that explains something nobody anticipated, and a designed event only answers questions someone thought of. I accepted that because the frequency was very low and the cost of the wall was paid continuously, but it is a real loss and I would not pretend otherwise. We hedged it two ways: exception logging stayed verbose and unabridged, since unanticipated failures are exactly where you want raw detail, and adding a field to the canonical event is deliberately a small, fast change so the answer to "we could not see X" is a one-line pull request rather than a project.

How did you get four teams to do this when it delivered no features?

I did the first service myself, with the team rather than for them, and then let the outcome do the arguing — the support-response number was the thing that travelled, because it was a pain those teams felt weekly. I also made adoption cheap: the shared logging package, the source-generated method templates and the deletion rule were done, so a team was buying a two-week job, not a design exercise. And I was explicit with the engineering manager about the trade — this quarter's capacity against a recurring cost and a recurring frustration — rather than smuggling it in as background work, because debt paydown done covertly poisons the well for the next one.

10Teaching a rotation to stop guessing

tier · principal mentoring / levelling up

Example — replace with your own

Our on-call rotation had eleven people and one of them was load-bearing. Every non-trivial page ended with someone messaging the same engineer, at any hour, and he answered — which is why nobody else ever learned. The visible metric was escalation rate: 64% of pages reached a second person, and about 80% of those reached him specifically. He was also, predictably, close to leaving.

The diagnosis I came to after listening to six handovers was that people were not missing knowledge of the system, they were missing a method. Faced with a page, most of the rotation would open a dashboard they liked, look at it, and then form a hypothesis based on the last incident they remembered — availability bias, dressed as intuition. The load-bearing engineer did something structurally different, and he could not articulate it because it was automatic: he narrowed by signal before he theorised. Symptom first, then which layer, then which instance, then which code path, and only then a hypothesis — and crucially, a hypothesis stated so it could be falsified in one query.

So I wrote his method down as four questions, and made it the first section of every runbook: What is the user-visible symptom, in one sentence? Is it everywhere or somewhere — which service, which instance, which tenant, which route? Did it start, or did it change — what does the same panel look like a week ago? What single query would prove me wrong? That last question is the one that changed people's behaviour most, because it converts "I think it's the database" into "if it's the database, connection wait time is up and it isn't, so it's not". Alongside it we taught the two aggregate lenses so nobody stares at a blank query bar: RED for request-driven services — rate, errors, duration — and USE for resources — utilisation, saturation, errors. Between them they cover almost every first move.

The teaching mechanism mattered more than the content. Weekly, thirty minutes, we replayed a real past incident from the telemetry: I gave someone the page text and nothing else, and they drove while narrating, with the rest of us forbidden to say the answer. Two rules made it work — the load-bearing engineer was not allowed to speak for the first ten minutes, and I deliberately drove a session badly myself, on a system I did not know, so the rotation could watch a principal engineer be lost and recover methodically. That single session did more for their confidence than the framework did; people assume seniority means knowing, and seeing that it means narrowing is the actual lesson.

Over two quarters escalation dropped from 64% to 21%, and the concentration on one person fell from 80% of escalations to 34%. He stayed. What I would do differently: I started with the framework and only added the replay sessions in month two, and the replays were where the learning actually happened — the document alone changed nothing for six weeks. I would also have measured earlier; I got the escalation numbers from a chat-history export after the fact, and having the baseline from day one would have let me show the rotation their own progress, which is a motivational tool I wasted.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you scale yourself. At principal level, "I fixed it" is a weaker answer than "I made eleven people able to fix it", and they want the mechanism, the measure, and evidence you did not become the new single point of failure. Two common failures: describing mentoring as a series of one-to-ones with no observable outcome, and quietly positioning yourself as the hero who knew the method all along — here the method came from a colleague, and saying so is the stronger move.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

RED and USE — when does that framing mislead people?

When the failure is not about volume or resources. RED is built for request-driven services and quietly assumes traffic exists, so it says nothing useful about a scheduled job, a stream processor or a queue consumer, where the interesting quantities are lag, age of the oldest unprocessed item and throughput against arrival rate. USE is built for resources and misses coordination failures entirely — a deadlock or a distributed lock held too long shows low utilisation, low saturation and no errors while the system does nothing. So I teach them as default opening moves rather than as a taxonomy, and I make sure the third lens, "what is queued and how old is it", is on the runbook for anything asynchronous.

How do you know they learned the method rather than memorising your incidents?

Because the improvement showed up on incidents that had nothing to do with the replays. That is the honest test, and I looked for it specifically: escalation fell on classes of page we had never rehearsed, and the reasoning in the incident channels changed shape — people started posting "if it were X, Y would be true, and it isn't" instead of "might be the cache again". I also watched who they escalated to: the fact that it spread across several people rather than concentrating on the next-most-expert person suggested they were escalating for context rather than for someone to think for them.

At what scale does this approach break?

It scales badly past one rotation, because it is transmitted by presence — thirty minutes a week with the same eleven people is not a thing I can do for eight teams. The version that scales is different in kind: the four questions go into the incident template so the tool asks them, the replays become a recorded library plus a facilitator guide so other teams can run their own, and I train facilitators instead of engineers. That is a real loss of fidelity, because the most valuable part was watching someone reason live and interrupting them, and a recording cannot be interrupted. My honest position is that method transfer is high-touch, so the leadership question is which teams get the high-touch version, not how to make it cheap.

What did the expert think about being told not to speak?

I asked him first, privately, and framed it as the thing that would get his weekends back, which was true and was what he wanted. He found the first two sessions genuinely uncomfortable — watching someone take twenty minutes over something he would do in two is hard, and once he broke the rule and I let it go rather than making a point of it in front of the group. What made it work was giving him a role rather than only a restriction: he wrote the replay scenarios and did the last ten minutes, where he would say what he would have looked at and why, which is the part people wanted anyway. Taking away someone's expert role without offering a better one is how you lose them.

How would you guide a team that has no telemetry at all through this?

The method comes first, and it survives poor tooling — narrowing by symptom, layer and time works with nothing but application logs, it is just slower. Then I would instrument in the order the method needs: a user-visible symptom signal first, which usually means RED metrics on the entry points and one SLO, because without it you cannot answer question one; then trace context propagation, because question two is "where", and traces answer it faster than anything; then structured errors. Dashboards last, which surprises people — a dashboard built before you know what questions you ask is decoration, and I would rather the team spent that week making the first three signals trustworthy.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is retrofitting instrumentation so much more expensive than adding it during build?

Because the expensive part is not the code, it is the coordination and the unknowns. Trace propagation is the clearest case: during build you are already touching every hop, so carrying context is an hour; afterwards it means a change per service, a deploy order, and the discovery that some hop — a legacy client, a third-party SDK, a serverless function — silently drops headers, which is a project rather than a task. Metrics are less asymmetric but still bad, because the meaningful ones need domain knowledge that has drifted out of people's heads within a month. And there is a compounding cost: without instrumentation you cannot see the behaviour you are trying to instrument for, so early production issues are debugged blind and the debugging itself takes longer.

How do you pick which four things, for a different launch?

I work backwards from the questions launch day will actually ask. Question one is always "is it working", which needs a user-visible signal — a rate, an error ratio, a duration on the entry point — and that is non-negotiable. Question two is "for whom", which needs a dimension you can segment by: the flag state, the tenant, the region, whatever the rollout is keyed on. Question three is "where", which needs the trace to survive every hop. Question four is "when did it start", which needs the signal to have existed before the change, so it has to ship at least a few days early. Anything that does not serve one of those four questions is deferrable by definition, and that is how I keep the ask honest rather than smuggling my full wish list in.

What if the product owner had said no anyway?

Then I would have taken the smallest subset I could do without permission and made the risk explicit and written. The RED metrics come from the shared template, so they are essentially free; the propagation is inside my team's code. What I could not do unilaterally is the SLO and the page, since that commits an on-call rotation. So I would have documented, in the launch plan rather than in a private message, that we would learn about a partial failure from customers rather than from an alert, with the expected detection time — not as a threat, but because an accepted risk should be accepted by name. I have done that; twice the risk was accepted and nothing happened, and once it was read by someone more senior who reversed the decision, which is not a mechanism I would rely on but is a reason to write things down.

At what scale does "instrument the new journey" stop being four days?

When the platform does not already exist. Everything above assumes a shared template, a Collector pipeline, an alerting path and an SLO tool that people know how to use — that is the leverage, and it is why platform investment shows up as launch speed rather than as its own metric. On a team with no pipeline, the same four items are three to four weeks, most of it deciding rather than coding: what the service is called, where it exports to, who gets paged. That is the case for building the platform between launches instead of during them, and it is the argument I would make to a manager who sees observability spend as overhead.

Nine minutes to mitigate — why not faster?

Four of those minutes were detection and five were human: reading the alert, opening the error events, confirming the flag correlation and deciding to turn it off. I would not have wanted it much faster with a human in the loop, and the way to actually improve it is to remove the human — a progressive-delivery setup that watches the SLO signal for the flagged cohort and rolls back on its own, which we did build later. The prerequisite for that automation is exactly the instrumentation we argued about, which is the honest sequencing lesson: you cannot automate a decision you cannot yet measure, so the four days were the price of every later improvement, not just of that day.

12Four hours of telemetry we never got back

Example — replace with your own

The observability backend had a bad afternoon — a storage incident on the vendor side, about four hours of degraded ingest — and we assumed we would simply catch up afterwards. We did not. When the backend recovered, roughly four hours of traces and a large share of logs were gone, permanently, and the reason was our own pipeline rather than theirs. The part that made it a real problem instead of an inconvenience: a production incident of our own started ninety minutes into that window, and we investigated it with almost nothing.

The mechanism is worth knowing precisely because it is counter-intuitive. The Collector's exporters have a sending queue with retry; when the backend rejects or times out, items stay queued and are retried with backoff. That works well for a blip. For a long outage the queue fills, and once full the exporter drops new items — correctly, because the alternative is unbounded memory growth. Two of our settings made it much worse than it had to be. The queue was in memory only, so when the gateway pods were restarted by the deployment we did mid-incident, everything buffered went with them. And the memory limiter, doing exactly its job, began refusing incoming data upstream as memory climbed, which pushed the drop point back into the agents and out of view. Nothing in this chain is a bug; it is a set of defaults that assume short failures.

extensions:
  file_storage/queue:
    directory: /var/lib/otelcol/queue    # survives a restart
    fsync: false                         # durability vs throughput: choose deliberately

exporters:
  otlp/backend:
    sending_queue:
      enabled: true
      storage: file_storage/queue        # the line we did not have
      queue_size: 100000
    retry_on_failure: { enabled: true, initial_interval: 5s, max_elapsed_time: 1800s }

service:
  telemetry:
    metrics: { level: detailed }         # meta-monitoring: watch the pipeline itself

The fixes were straightforward once we understood it. A file-backed persistent queue on the gateway, so a restart no longer discards the buffer, sized against a documented target of surviving a one-hour backend outage at peak rate — I insisted on writing that target down, because "bigger queue" is not a design. Meta-monitoring, which we genuinely lacked: alerts on the Collector's own metrics for queue size approaching capacity, send failures, and refused spans, routed to a channel rather than a page, plus one high-priority alert for "we are dropping telemetry", since that is the condition under which every other alert becomes untrustworthy. And an explicit priority order in the pipeline: if we must drop, drop debug-level logs and baseline-sampled traces before error traces and metrics, which we implemented with separate pipelines and separate queues per signal rather than one shared fate.

The uncomfortable conclusion I now say out loud in design reviews is that a telemetry pipeline is a lossy system by design, and the engineering question is which data you lose and when. Teams treat it as a database and are shocked when it behaves like a network. What I would do differently is the restart: deploying the gateway during a backend outage destroyed the one buffer that might have saved us, and it was my call, made quickly, without asking what was in memory. The runbook now says freeze telemetry-infrastructure deployments during a backend incident, which is a one-line lesson that cost four hours of data and a very bad incident investigation.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you understand that your monitoring is itself a distributed system with its own failure modes, and whether you own a decision you made under pressure that made things worse. The restart admission is the strongest part of this story — volunteer it. Candidates weaken it by blaming the vendor, or by describing the loss without saying what it cost, which is the concrete part: an incident investigated blind.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just make the queue enormous and never drop anything?

Because the queue is a buffer for a temporary failure, not storage, and sizing it for an arbitrary outage converts a telemetry problem into a capacity problem on the machines running your telemetry. A very large in-memory queue is memory you have taken away from the process's ability to receive; a very large disk queue is disk, plus the write amplification of persisting everything you were going to send anyway, plus a long recovery where you are pushing hours of backlog into a backend that has just come back and is probably fragile. There is also a freshness argument: telemetry that arrives four hours late is of limited value for alerting and of some value for analysis, so past a point you would rather drop it than delay live data behind it. The design question is how long an outage you intend to survive, stated explicitly, and then sizing to that.

At what scale does a persistent queue stop being the right answer?

When per-node disk cannot absorb your peak rate for a meaningful window, which arrives quickly at high span rates. Past that you need a real buffer between producers and the backend — a durable log such as Kafka in the middle of the pipeline, which is what large deployments end up doing and is exactly the direction the ecosystem has moved: Loki 4.0's rearchitecture put a Kafka-backed ingestion layer in front of storage for the same reason, to get durability, back-pressure and replay. The trade is that you have now added a distributed system to your observability stack, which needs its own operational competence, so I would only take it when the volume genuinely justifies it and would push sampling and filtering first.

How do you monitor the Collector without depending on the Collector?

You accept a small amount of duplication and keep one path independent. The Collector exposes its own metrics, and we scrape those with a Prometheus that is separate from the main pipeline and alerts through a different notification path, so a failure of the primary stack cannot also silence the alert about it. The rule I apply is that the alert about system X must not traverse system X, which sounds obvious and is violated constantly — we had alerts about the telemetry pipeline being delivered through the telemetry pipeline. There is a floor to this: something must be trusted, and for us that is the paging provider plus one small independent Prometheus, and I would rather have one clearly-named trusted root than a chain of mutual dependencies nobody has drawn.

What did you trade away by splitting pipelines per signal?

Simplicity and some efficiency. One pipeline is easier to reason about and shares a connection pool and batching across signals; four pipelines with their own queues use more memory, more file handles and more configuration, and there is now a matrix of failure states rather than one. We took it because shared fate was the actual defect — losing error traces because debug logs filled the queue is an outcome nobody would choose deliberately — and because it let us size and prioritise each signal against what it is worth. If I were doing it on a smaller system I would probably split two ways rather than four: things that must survive, and things that may be dropped.

You restarted the gateways. Talk me through that decision.

We had a config change that we believed would reduce load on the failing backend, and applying it meant a rolling restart, so I approved it about forty minutes in. It was a reasonable-sounding action taken quickly, and it was wrong for a reason I simply did not think about: the gateways were at that moment the only copy of ninety minutes of telemetry. What I take from it is not "never restart" but a question I now ask before any action during an incident — what state does this destroy? — which takes ten seconds and would have changed the call. It is also why the deployment freeze is written down: under pressure, a rule you read is more reliable than a question you remember to ask.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why include a dependency you cannot control in your own SLO?

Because the SLO describes the user's experience, and the user has no visibility into your supply chain. If you exclude the partner, your dashboard is green during an outage that is destroying the journey, which makes the SLO an instrument that lies at exactly the moment it matters. The objection — that you are held accountable for something you cannot fix — is only half true: you cannot fix their availability, but you can cache, degrade, fall back, retry, or ultimately replace them, and every one of those becomes fundable once the failure shows up in your budget. Where I would exclude a dependency is when there is genuinely no engineering response available and the contract is owned elsewhere; then it belongs in a separate, explicitly reported figure rather than hidden.

What is the error-budget policy, and did anyone ever honour it?

The policy is the part that gives the number teeth: what happens when the budget is exhausted. Ours said feature work pauses for reliability work until the trailing 28-day budget is back in surplus, with the director able to override in writing. It was invoked twice. The first time it worked as designed and produced three weeks of reliability work. The second time it was overridden for a commercial deadline — and the override being written down, with a named person and a reason, was more valuable than the pause would have been, because it made the trade visible instead of letting the budget be quietly ignored. An error-budget policy nobody ever invokes is decoration; one that is sometimes overridden in the open is functioning.

Server-side or client-side measurement — you chose server-side. Defend it.

I chose it as the SLI because it is complete: every request is measured, on infrastructure I control, with no dependence on the client being willing or able to report. Client-side is closer to truth and structurally biased — you only hear from sessions that loaded enough JavaScript to phone home, which systematically excludes the worst experiences, and it is polluted by the client's own device and network in ways I cannot act on. So I use client-side as a check on the SLI rather than as the SLI: if server-side is healthy and client-side is not, that divergence is itself an alertable condition and has caught a CDN problem and a front-end bundle regression for us. The wrong answer is to pick one and forget the other exists.

At what scale does one SLO per service stop working?

Almost immediately, once a service handles heterogeneous traffic. A ratio across a high-volume cheap endpoint and a low-volume critical one is dominated by the cheap one, so checkout can fail entirely without moving the number — which is the argument for SLOs per user journey rather than per service, even though journeys cut across team boundaries and are therefore politically harder. The scaling problem then becomes proliferation: forty journeys with four SLIs each is a maintenance burden nobody sustains, and stale SLOs are worse than none. My rule is that an SLO needs a named owner and a review date, and if nobody will own it, it should not exist — which in practice keeps the count to the handful of journeys the business would actually pause work for.

Eleven weeks is a long time. What would you have escalated sooner?

The decision structure itself. I spent about eight weeks assuming a well-run meeting would produce agreement, when the disagreement was not about facts but about who would be accountable for a number — and no amount of facilitation resolves that. I should have gone to the director in week two with the question "these four groups will not agree; will you decide, or will you delegate the decision to me?", which is a two-minute conversation I avoided because it felt like admitting failure. It is the opposite: recognising that a disagreement is about authority rather than information, and routing it accordingly, is the job. That is the piece of this story I actually think is worth telling in an interview.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does trace context need to be an explicit contract between teams?

Because it is ambient and therefore invisible: nothing fails when it is dropped, and the team dropping it sees perfectly healthy traces of their own. That asymmetry means it will never be discovered by the party responsible, only by the party downstream, which is a classic recipe for a defect that lives for years. Writing the headers into the interface definition — the same document that names the auth scheme — makes it a testable property, so a gateway change that strips it fails a contract test rather than silently degrading everyone's debugging. It also settles the format question in advance: W3C trace context is the standard and B3 is the legacy you will meet, and a composite propagator that accepts both is the migration path rather than a permanent state.

Their allow-list existed for security. Were you asking them to weaken it?

That was their first objection and it deserved a real answer rather than a dismissal. Allowing traceparent through does introduce something: an untrusted caller can set a trace id, which means they can join their spans to someone else's trace or flip the sampled flag to force sampling, and at the very edge of a public API that is a genuine, if minor, abuse vector. The proportionate answer is different at different boundaries — at an internal service boundary between two of our own systems, accepting it is correct and the risk is negligible; at the public edge, the gateway should generate a fresh trace and record the client's value as a link or an attribute rather than trusting it. We wrote exactly that distinction into the change, which is why the security reviewer approved it.

At what scale does manual cross-team correlation stop being viable?

It stops well before people admit it. With two teams and a shared user id you can correlate by hand and it costs hours; with six services on a path, retries, and asynchronous hops, timestamp correlation is not merely slow but wrong, because you cannot tell which of three concurrent attempts you are looking at. The failure is quiet: teams stop investigating the hard class of issue and start closing tickets as not-reproducible, which looks like an improvement in the ticket metrics. That is the argument I would use with a manager who thinks tracing is a nice-to-have — not the debugging time, but the category of problem that silently stops being investigated at all.

Should you have escalated to your manager after the first week?

No, and I would still say no, though it is a closer call than it looks. Escalating a nine-day, one-line change would have bought a faster fix at the cost of the relationship, and I needed that team for a dozen other things over the following year. What I should have done in week one is what I eventually did in week four — quantify and patch — so my mistake was slowness, not insufficient escalation. Where I would escalate is when the other team disagrees with the goal rather than the priority, or when the cost is accruing to users rather than to my own convenience; both change the calculus, and the second one I would raise the same day.

How would you prevent this across twenty teams rather than one?

By making the contract testable and the default correct. (d) Follow-up drill chain

Why work the levers in that order rather than by size of saving?

Because the levers differ enormously in what they cost you, and taking the painful ones first spends capability you may not need to spend. Deleting waste is free by definition; retention tiering costs latency on old queries, which is measurable and small; sampling costs statistical resolution on rare events, which is exactly the thing you cannot get back after an incident. Ordering by saving would have had me sampling traces in week one and discovering in month three that I could have hit the target with the free levers alone. There is also an organisational reason: starting with waste builds credibility for the later asks, because the first conversation is "we are paying for debug logs from 2024" rather than "I need you to accept less visibility".

Converting logs to metrics at the Collector — when is that a bad idea?

When you need the examples rather than the count. Aggregating a high-volume log stream into a counter is excellent for something you only ever graph — a rate-limit rejection, a cache miss — and destructive for anything you investigate individually, because you keep the shape and lose every instance. The failure mode is discovering that during an incident, when someone asks "which requests were rejected?" and the answer is a number. So I apply it only where a team can name the queries they run against that stream and none of them selects individual records, and I keep a small sampled passthrough alongside the counter where there is any doubt — a few per minute is nearly free and preserves the ability to look at one.

At what scale does per-team cost attribution stop working?

When ownership of telemetry does not match ownership of code, which happens more than you would like: shared infrastructure, a service inherited by nobody, a platform component every team's traffic passes through. Those become an unattributed pool, and if the pool grows the mechanism quietly stops working because the biggest line item has no owner. The fix is boring and organisational — every service has an owner attribute enforced at deploy time, and the platform's own consumption is a named budget defended like any team's. The other limit is that attribution creates a perverse incentive at the margin: a team under budget pressure can hit its number by under-instrumenting, so the budget conversation has to sit next to the SLO conversation, or you will save money by going blind.

How do you avoid cutting the data you need for the next incident?

By anchoring every cut to a question rather than to a data type. Before removing anything I ask which incident-time question it answers, and if nobody can name one, the data is a candidate; if someone can, it stays or moves to a cheaper tier that still answers it. In practice that means errors and their traces are close to untouchable, aggregate metrics on user-facing signals are untouchable, and the entire debate happens over successful-path verbosity — which is where the volume is anyway. I also kept a rollback path for the first month of each change, because a cut you cannot reverse quickly is a bet rather than an optimisation.

The fraud investigation needed the data you had aged out. Did that change your policy?

It changed one part of it. We had treated retention as a single operational question and it is partly a legal and investigative one, so the correction was to identify the small set of streams with an obligation or a plausible retrospective use — payment events, authentication events, admin actions — and give them a long, cheap archive tier separate from the operational retention. That costs very little because the volume is small; the mistake was applying one policy to everything. What it did not change is the operational retention for general application logs, because that was a genuinely good trade and the fraud case would not have been served by ninety days of debug output either.

17Continuous profiling, from toy to standard

Example — replace with your own

We could answer "which service is slow" in seconds and "which line of code is slow" only by rebuilding a suspect service with a profiler attached and hoping the behaviour reproduced. That gap cost us weeks a year, and it is precisely the gap continuous profiling fills: always-on, low-frequency sampling of stack traces across the whole fleet, so the question becomes a query over data you already have rather than an experiment you have to run.

Introducing it was mostly a credibility problem, not a technical one, because two previous tools had been introduced with enthusiasm and abandoned. So I did not ask for adoption. I asked for one service and one open question — a checkout service whose CPU had crept up 40% over two quarters with no obvious cause and no explanation anybody believed. Profiling it for four days gave an answer nobody had guessed: 22% of CPU was in JSON serialisation of a response field that had been added for an experiment, kept for nobody, and serialised on every request. Deleting the field recovered most of the drift. That is the demonstration I needed, and it was worth more than any presentation, because it answered a question the team had already failed to answer by other means.

The technical case is worth stating properly, because it is the part that gets misremembered. Profiling is a sampled statistical signal: an agent interrupts at a low frequency — tens of hertz — captures the stack, and aggregates. At those rates the overhead is small enough to run permanently, typically low single-digit percent, and the trade is resolution: you learn where time goes in aggregate, not what happened in one request. That is exactly complementary to tracing, which tells you which span was slow and nothing about what was executing inside it. The state of the ecosystem in 2026 matters here too: profiling has become the fourth OpenTelemetry signal rather than a vendor-specific add-on, with the Profiles signal entering public Alpha in March 2026, an OTLP representation compatible with pprof, and Collector support from v0.148.0 onwards — with GA targeted but not shipped, which is a fact I put in the rollout document rather than glossing. Alongside it, eBPF-based collection matured: OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumentation, the project donated from Grafana Beyla, reached v0.8.0 in April 2026 with a 1.0 planned for late 2026, and it profiles processes without touching the application at all.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is continuous profiling affordable when profiling used to be a special event?

Because it is sampled rather than instrumented. A traditional profiler that instruments every method call changes the program's performance enough to invalidate what it measures, which is why you ran it in a lab. Sampling at tens of hertz means the agent interrupts, walks the stack and aggregates — the cost is proportional to the sampling rate and the stack depth, not to how much work the program does — so overhead sits low enough to leave on permanently. The consequence is statistical: a rare code path may not appear at all in a short window, and you reason about proportions rather than about a specific request. That trade is nearly always right for production, and it is why the aggregation window matters more than the sampling rate when you are hunting something intermittent.

The OTel profiles signal is in alpha. Why build on it now?

Because the format is where the value is and the format is the part that stabilised first: an OTLP profiles representation compatible with pprof, so what I collect is portable even if the tooling around it changes. I would not build a production dependency on the alpha where a failure has user impact — this is a diagnostic path, so the failure mode of an alpha component is that I temporarily lose a debugging convenience, not that customers notice. The way I stated it in the rollout document was explicit: alpha, targeting GA, do not put it on the critical path, expect breaking changes at upgrade. What I would refuse to do is call it stable to make adoption easier, because the first breaking change would then cost me the credibility the pilot bought.

eBPF-based collection or in-process SDK — which and why?

Both, for different jobs. eBPF collection — OBI, formerly Beyla — needs no application change and covers everything on the node including things you cannot rebuild, which makes it the right way to get fleet-wide coverage quickly and the only way to see processes nobody owns. It costs you semantic depth: it sees system calls and stacks, not your application's own notion of a request, and it needs privileged access and a kernel new enough, which is a real constraint on older estates. The in-process SDK sees managed frames and application context properly and requires a deploy per service. My rule is eBPF for breadth and for the unowned surface, SDK for the services where you want profiles correlated with your own spans and attributes.

At what scale does continuous profiling get expensive?

On storage and on symbolisation rather than on CPU. Profiles compress well but they are dense, and keeping full-resolution stacks for every process for ninety days is a serious volume — so the pattern is short high-resolution retention with aggressive downsampling afterwards, which suits how the data is used, since almost every profiling question is about now or about a specific past week. Symbolisation is the operational cost people forget: you need debug information for every build in production, kept as long as the profiles, or your historical flame graphs turn into addresses. That means a symbol store with a retention policy tied to the profile retention, and a build pipeline that publishes reliably — which is exactly the three weeks I failed to scope.

How did you stop it becoming the third abandoned tool?

By removing every recurring cost of using it. It is on by default in the service template, so nobody has to decide; it is linked from the incident runbook at the step where people are already asking "why is this slow", so it is encountered in context rather than remembered; and there is a named owner with time allocated, because a tool with no owner degrades into a broken dashboard within a year. The other thing I did deliberately was to stop championing it after the first quarter. If the only reason a tool is used is that I keep mentioning it, it has not been adopted, and I would rather learn that early than sustain it artificially.

I look for agreement between two independent accountings. The runtime tells me the managed heap: generation sizes, large-object heap, allocation rate, time in collection — and a managed leak shows a gen 2 that grows and never comes down after a full collection. The operating system tells me the process total, and inside a container the cgroup tells me what the limit is enforced against, split into anonymous and file-backed. If the managed heap is flat while the container climbs, the growth is native — memory-mapped files, unmanaged buffers, thread stacks, a native library, or reclaimable cache. If both climb together, take a heap dump and compare two snapshots to see which type is accumulating. The cheap first check is the divergence, which takes a minute and eliminates half the search space.

Why does a container get killed for memory that the kernel could reclaim?

Because reclaim is not free and the limit is enforced against a total that includes reclaimable pages. Clean file-backed pages can be dropped cheaply, so in most cases the kernel reclaims and nothing dies — but reclaim costs latency, dirty pages must be written back first, and if anonymous demand spikes faster than reclaim can keep up, the out-of-memory killer fires. The practical consequence is that a workload doing heavy file I/O inside a tight container limit is living close to an edge that looks like a leak on a graph, and the fix is either headroom, less file traffic, or an explicit hint that the pages are not worth caching. It is also why alerting on total container memory produces false pages for I/O-heavy services, and why splitting anonymous from file-backed in the alert is the correction.

At what scale does the "restart it" runbook entry become dangerous?

As soon as restarting destroys state that is diagnostically or operationally valuable, and that threshold arrives earlier than people think: an in-memory cache whose cold-start costs the database a spike, a buffered telemetry queue, an in-flight batch, a leader election that reshuffles. At small scale a restart is a cheap probe; at scale it is an intervention with its own blast radius, and a rolling restart of a fleet under load can be the thing that turns a degradation into an outage. So I want runbooks to say what a step is for and what it costs — "restart to clear the symptom; this loses the local cache, expect a database spike for two minutes" — because that is the sentence that lets a tired person make a decision rather than follow an instruction.

You raised the limit rather than fixing it. Isn't that papering over the problem?

It is, deliberately, and the distinction I would defend is between mitigation and resolution. At 03:20 the goal is to stop customer impact with the smallest, most reversible action available, and a limit increase is reversible, quick and well understood; diagnosing a memory-accounting subtlety at that hour is how you make a second mistake. What makes it legitimate rather than lazy is that it was written down as a temporary mitigation with an owner and a follow-up, and it was actually reverted after the real fix. The failure mode is the limit increase that becomes permanent and unexamined, which is how services end up with 8 GB limits nobody can justify — so I treat every mitigation as debt with a due date.

How would you make this diagnosis unnecessary next time?

By putting the comparison in the signal rather than in someone's head. The dashboard now shows container memory and managed heap on one panel, so the divergence is visible without knowing to look for it, and the alert distinguishes anonymous growth — which is worth a page — from file-backed growth, which is worth a ticket. Beyond that I would push the check earlier: a load test that watches both numbers would have caught this a week before, when the file round-trip was introduced, and a review question of "what does this write to disk and who reads it back" would have caught it before that. The pattern I keep taking from incidents like this is that the durable fix is usually a change to what is visible by default, not a change to what people know.

19Making the pager trustworthy again

tier · staff reliability / test improvement

Example — replace with your own

The rotation received about 1,100 pages a quarter across eleven services, and when I sampled a hundred of them, 38% had led to any action at all. The rest were acknowledged and closed. That is not an alerting problem, it is a trust problem: once a majority of pages are noise, people stop reading them carefully, and the failure mode is not annoyance — it is the real page that gets acknowledged reflexively at 2 a.m. by someone who has learned that pages are usually nothing. We had one of those, three weeks before I started this work, and it is the example I used to get the work funded.

The measurement came first, because "too many alerts" is unactionable and a number per rule is not. For a month, every page got a one-click disposition when it was resolved: acted, auto-resolved, duplicate, or wrong. That gave me a table of rules ranked by uselessness, and the distribution was the familiar one — twelve rules produced 71% of the pages, and eight of those twelve had never once led to an action. Deleting those eight was the single largest improvement in the whole project, and it took an afternoon. The hard part was social: each of those rules was written by someone in response to a real incident, and deleting it feels like discarding a lesson. What made it possible was a rule I could state plainly — an alert that has never changed anyone's behaviour is not a safety net, it is a tax on attention — and a commitment that we would delete it and watch, rather than delete it and forget.

Then structure. Every remaining rule had to satisfy four conditions or it was demoted from page to ticket: it describes a symptom a user could feel; it is actionable right now, not tomorrow; it links to a runbook with a first step; and it has an owner. Alerting moved into version control with the services, reviewed like code, and — the part that surprised people — unit-tested. Rule tests are one of the most underused tools in the whole discipline: you write a series of samples and assert which alerts fire and when, so a rule that can never fire, or that fires one evaluation too early, is caught in CI rather than during an incident.

# promtool test rules - a rule you have not tested is a rule you have not written
tests:
  - interval: 1m
    input_series:
      - series: 'checkout:error_ratio:rate5m'
        values: '0+0x30 0.05+0x40'      # healthy for 30m, then 5% errors
    alert_rule_test:
      - eval_time: 45m
        alertname: CheckoutBudgetBurnFast
        exp_alerts:
          - exp_labels: { severity: page }
      - eval_time: 20m                   # must NOT fire while healthy
        alertname: CheckoutBudgetBurnFast
        exp_alerts: []

Two quarters later: 180 pages a quarter, 91% of them acted on, and median time to acknowledge down from just over nine minutes to under two — that last number is the one I care about, because it measures whether people believe the pager. The mechanism that keeps it there is a fifteen-minute item in the weekly team meeting where we look at every page from the past week and decide, for each, whether the rule should be kept, tuned or deleted. It is unglamorous and it is the whole thing; alert quality decays continuously, so it needs a continuous, cheap ritual rather than a project. What I would do differently is that I deleted the eight rules in one change, and although nothing broke, I could not have proved it if something had. Now I demote first — page to ticket for two weeks, then delete — which costs almost nothing and turns an argument about risk into an observation.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you improve a system people depend on by measuring rather than by opinion, and whether you understand that alert fatigue is a reliability risk rather than a comfort issue. The strong version has a baseline, a distribution, a criterion for deletion, and a ritual that stops it regressing. Watch for the trap of framing it as reducing on-call burden for its own sake — the reason it matters is the missed page.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

How do you know you did not delete an alert you needed?

I do not know it with certainty, and I would not claim to — what I can say is how the risk was bounded. Every deleted rule had a full history showing it had never preceded an action, which is evidence rather than proof; the SLO alerts sat underneath as a symptom-level backstop, so a genuine user-visible failure would still page even if a cause-level rule was gone; and I tracked incidents for two quarters afterwards specifically for one detected later than it would have been, and found none. The residual risk is the rare cause that only that rule could see and that has not occurred yet. That is why demoting to ticket before deleting is better: you keep the signal, lose the interruption, and get the evidence for free.

What actually goes wrong when a rule is never tested?

Usually one of three things, all silent. The rule cannot fire at all — a label typo, a metric renamed by a semantic-convention change, an expression whose empty result is never greater than a threshold — and silence looks exactly like health. Or it fires far too eagerly because the evaluation window and the rate window interact badly, so a single missed scrape looks like a failure. Or the severity routing is wrong and it goes to a channel nobody reads. Rule tests catch the first two directly by asserting fire and no-fire at specific times, and the third is caught by a routing test. The general principle is that an alert rule is production code with no user to complain when it breaks, which makes it exactly the kind of code that needs tests most.

At what scale does the weekly review stop scaling?

At around one team, which is the honest answer. Fifteen minutes covers a week of pages for one rotation; for an organisation you cannot centralise it without creating a queue and a bottleneck. What scales is the measurement and the standard: the platform publishes per-team page volume and actioned rate on a dashboard everyone can see, and the review itself happens inside each team, with the platform providing the tooling and occasionally noticing an outlier. Making the number visible across teams does most of the work, because nobody wants to be the team with a 30% actioned rate — though I would be careful with that, since a metric used for comparison invites gaming, and the gaming here is trivially easy: stop paging for real things.

What did you trade away by demanding a runbook link on every page?

Speed of adding an alert, and I nearly lost the policy over it. Requiring a runbook means a genuine new failure mode cannot be alerted on in the ten minutes after an incident, which is exactly when people want to add one, and that friction gets resented. The compromise was that a new page-severity rule can be added immediately with a placeholder runbook containing one sentence — what to check first — with a two-week expiry after which it demotes itself to a ticket automatically. That kept the barrier low at the moment of highest motivation while preventing the accumulation of rules with no instructions, which is the state we started from.

How would you guide a team that resists deleting alerts?

I would stop arguing about individual rules and get them to measure disposition for a month, because the resistance is almost always about an imagined future incident and the data is about the actual past. Then I would frame the decision as a trade they already understand — every page spends attention, and attention is finite, so keeping a rule that has never helped is choosing to have less attention available for the one that will. If they still resist, I take demotion rather than deletion, which nearly everyone accepts because it is reversible, and let two weeks of nothing happening do the persuading. What I would not do is override them: an on-call rotation has to own its own pager, and a change imposed by someone who does not carry it will be reverted the first time anything goes wrong.

20"Just give me one health number"

tier · principal pushing back on a stakeholder

Example — replace with your own

A senior executive asked for a single platform health score on the leadership dashboard — one number, zero to a hundred, green above ninety. It is a completely reasonable request from where he sat, and building it would have been about a week's work, and I said no. Saying no to that request without sounding obstructive, and while actually solving his problem, is the hardest thing in this story; the technical argument is the easy half.

The technical argument is that composite scores lose exactly the information that makes a number worth having. Any weighted average of forty services hides the one that is completely down, because it is one-fortieth of the weighting — so the score is least sensitive precisely when the situation is most severe. Weights are arbitrary and become political, since every team argues for its own service's weight. Worst, a score has no action attached: told the number is 87, nobody can do anything, so within a month people either ignore it or start managing it, and managing a composite means gaming its components. I have watched a team hit a health target by narrowing what counted as an error, which is not a hypothetical.

But the actual question underneath was legitimate, and I only found it by asking what he would do differently at 95 versus 80. His answer, once we got there, was that he wanted to know two things: whether to worry right now, and whether reliability was getting better or worse over quarters — the second because he was defending headcount and had no evidence. Neither needs a composite. So I proposed something narrower and shipped it in a week:

What he asked forWhat he neededWhat we built
One number, 0–100"Should I worry right now?"A single status line: any journey SLO currently in breach, named, with duration — usually "all six journeys within budget"
Green above 90"Is it getting better?"Error-budget consumption per journey, by quarter — a trend with a denominator that does not move
A weighted roll-up"Where should the money go?"The three journeys with the worst budget consumption, each with a named owner and the current investment

Six journeys is small enough to read in five seconds — which was his real constraint, and I had initially misheard "one number" as a preference for simplicity when it was a constraint on attention. The phrase that landed was that a single score would tell him things were fine on the day checkout was down, and he did not want that, he wanted to be told the truth quickly. I also conceded something real: he was right that forty service dashboards were unusable at his level, and that was our failure, not his misunderstanding. Eighteen months on, the journey view is still the thing leadership looks at, and it has twice driven investment decisions in the right direction. What I would do differently is that I said no before I understood the need — my first response, in the meeting, was a small lecture about composite metrics, and I had to repair that afterwards. The order that works is understand, then propose, then explain why not the original. Leading with the objection makes even a correct answer sound like resistance, and at this level being right is not sufficient; being adopted is the job.

What the interviewer is really probing. Whether you can decline a senior stakeholder's specific request while serving their underlying need, and whether you can be persuasive rather than merely correct. They want the reframing question you asked, a concrete alternative you shipped quickly, and evidence it was adopted. Two failure modes: building it anyway and grumbling, or refusing on principle without delivering anything — both read as an engineer who cannot operate above their own team.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why exactly is a composite health score harmful rather than merely imprecise?

Because its error is not random, it is systematically wrong in the direction that matters. Averaging is a low-pass filter: a total outage of one service among forty moves the score by a couple of points and looks like noise, so the metric is least responsive at the extreme where you most need it to shout. It also destroys actionability, since no one can respond to "87" without decomposing it, which means everyone keeps the underlying dashboards anyway and the score adds a layer rather than replacing one. And once it is on a leadership dashboard it becomes a target, and the cheapest way to move a composite is always to adjust its definition rather than the system. A small set of named, unaveraged indicators avoids all three problems and is barely harder to read.

Isn't an SLO also a single number that hides detail?

It is a single number, and the difference is that it is not an average across unrelated things — it is a ratio over one well-defined event stream for one journey, with a stated definition of failure and an owner. That makes it decomposable in a way a composite is not: when it moves you can ask which requests, which dependency, which cohort, and get an answer from the same data. It is also bounded in what it claims; an SLO on checkout says nothing about search and does not pretend to. Where the criticism does land is that an SLO across heterogeneous traffic within one journey can hide a small, badly-affected cohort, which is why I keep per-cohort breakdowns behind it and why enterprise-tier customers often warrant their own.

At what scale does "six journeys" stop working?

When the organisation genuinely has more than a handful of independent products, at which point a single leadership view has to become hierarchical — each product with its own small set, and the top level showing which products are in breach rather than which journeys. That is a roll-up, and it works only because it is a list rather than an average: the top level says "two of nine products currently in breach, here they are", which preserves the name of the failing thing. The moment someone converts that list into a percentage of products healthy, you are back to the composite problem. So my rule is that aggregation upward may reduce detail but must never average across things a person would act on separately.

What if he had insisted?

I would have built it, with two conditions I would have asked for explicitly: that the composite never appears without the list of currently-breaching journeys next to it, so it cannot be green while something is down, and that it is not used as a target for any team. That is a decent outcome — the harm in a composite is mostly in being alone and in being a target — and it is a much better position than refusing and being overruled, which loses both the argument and the conditions. I would also have said, once and in writing, what I expected to go wrong, not as a hedge but so that when it did we could fix it quickly. Being able to implement a decision you argued against, competently and without visible resentment, is part of the job.

How do you guide a team through this kind of request when you are not in the room?

I teach the reframing question rather than the objection, because the objection is easy to look up and the question is the skill: what would you do differently at 95 than at 80? It works on almost every "can you build me a dashboard" request and it converts a specification into a need, which is the only form you can actually design against. The second thing I teach is to come back within a week with something real, because credibility in these conversations is bought with speed, not with correctness — a proposal a week later beats a perfect argument in the meeting. And I tell people to concede the true part loudly: he was right that our dashboards were unusable at his level, and admitting that is what made the rest of the conversation possible.

These are the prompts that are not tied to one story — the ones that open a loop, close it, or arrive when an interviewer wants to see how you generalise. Deploy: story 15 (the backend I shut down) — a seven-month bet on a business case that omitted operating cost. They want: proportion. A mistake that is too small reads as evasion; one that is catastrophic and uncontained reads as risk. delivery read once

Ten sentences to read the night before and once more in the car park. They are about delivery, not content — by this point the content is either in your head or it is not, and delivery is where prepared candidates still lose.

  1. Open with the situation in one sentence, and put a number in it. "Forty-three minutes of broken checkout that none of our alerts noticed."   2. Say what you decided, not only what happened. Observability stories drift into system narration — the graph did this, then the service did that — and the interviewer is assessing judgment. Every ninety seconds, return to a decision you made and the alternative you rejected.
  3. Separate detection from diagnosis from mitigation. It is the vocabulary of the discipline and it instantly signals that you have run real incidents. "We detected it in four minutes, diagnosed it in twenty, mitigated in nine" is worth more than any amount of technical colour.
  4. Quantify the result, and prefer a rate over an absolute. Cost per million requests, percentage of pages actioned, time to answer a support question. Absolute numbers invite "compared with what?"; ratios carry their own denominator and hold up when the interviewer probes.
  5. Separate "I" from "we" deliberately and out loud. Say "the team migrated thirty-one services; my part was the topology and the equivalence proof". Interviewers are trained to notice a candidate who says "I" about work that obviously took eight people, and it costs you more than modesty ever will.
  6. Own the failure inside the win. Every story here has one — two weeks optimising the wrong thing, a restart that destroyed the buffer, three weeks of blame-shaped framing. Volunteer it before you are asked; the interviewer will find it anyway, and finding it themselves converts a strength into a doubt.
  7. Tie the technical decision back to a real trade-off. Head sampling loses randomly, tail sampling loses with a bias you must enumerate. Cardinality is the budget. A composite score is least sensitive when the situation is most severe. One sentence of "it depends, because…" does more than a paragraph of mechanism.
  8. Answer a drill-down in three to six sentences, then stop. The follow-up chains in this page are calibrated to that length. Silence afterwards is the interviewer's turn, not a gap you are obliged to fill, and continuing to talk is how a correct answer becomes an uncertain one.
  9. When you do not know, state the boundary and then reason. "I have not run the profiles signal in production — it is alpha as of March 2026 — but here is what I would test first, and why." Reasoning from a stated boundary scores higher than a confident guess and infinitely higher than an invented one.
  10. Close each story on the systemic change, not the fix. The limit instead of the guideline, the test instead of the document, the default instead of the campaign. Fixing the incident is the mid-level answer; making that class of incident impossible — or merely visible — is what they are hiring for.
