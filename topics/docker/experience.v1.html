01 The image that took 11 minutes to build

SeniorPerformance problem

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

I inherited a Java service whose CI build took 11 minutes for a one-line change, while the same Dockerfile built in 90 seconds on my laptop after the first run. docker buildx build --progress=plain showed why: the Dockerfile ran COPY . . before mvn -o dependency:go-offline, so every commit — even a comment change — invalidated the dependency-resolution layer's cache key, and CI's ephemeral runners never carried a warm cache between builds anyway. Locally the layer just happened to survive because Docker Desktop kept the same build cache around. I split the Dockerfile so pom.xml is copied and dependencies resolved in their own layer before COPY src ever runs, moved ~/.m2 into a BuildKit --mount=type=cache so it isn't baked into an image layer at all, and wired --cache-to type=registry --cache-from type=registry into the CI job so a cold runner could still pull a warm cache. CI dropped to about 2 minutes; incremental local rebuilds to under 10 seconds. What I'd do differently: I should have added a .dockerignore on day one — the build context was silently shipping 400MB of target/ and .git to the daemon before a single instruction ran, which is its own 15–20 second tax nobody had noticed because nobody had measured it.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you understand cache invalidation as content-addressed (instruction + the files it touches), not folklore ("just put COPY last"), and whether you measured before/after instead of asserting an improvement. The common failure here is describing the fix correctly but with no numbers, or claiming sole credit for a fix a teammate's flame graph actually found.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does moving COPY after dependency install help, mechanically?

BuildKit keys each layer by the instruction plus the content it touches. If src/ changes but the earlier layer only ever touched pom.xml, that layer's key is unchanged and BuildKit reuses it verbatim instead of re-running mvn. It's not about instruction order for its own sake — it's about minimizing what each layer's key depends on.

What if the slow part is pulling the base image, not your own build steps?

Then the fix is upstream of your Dockerfile: a smaller base (distroless or alpine over a full OS), a regional pull-through registry mirror, or pinning to a digest you've already warmed on every node. I'd check docker system df and pull timings before assuming the build steps are the bottleneck.

At what scale does registry-backed cache-to/cache-from stop paying for itself?

For a tiny, already-fast build, the network round-trip to push and pull cache layers can cost more than it saves. It earns its keep on large multi-stage builds shared across many CI runs — a monorepo with a heavy dependency-resolution stage is the sweet spot, not a 20-second build that ran fine before.

What did you trade away by moving to a BuildKit cache mount?

Portability. A mount cache lives on the builder machine unless you explicitly export it, so a genuinely fresh runner still starts cold. I accepted that because our runners were a stable, warm pool — I'd revisit it if we moved to fully ephemeral, one-shot runners.

What would you do differently now, given mount type=image went stable in 29.7.0?

I'd look at mounting reference data directly from a prebuilt image at build time instead of copying it into context at all, which shrinks what the daemon has to receive before the build even starts. I'd pilot it on one service first, since it changes how the build graph reads and not every CI runner's buildx version supports it yet.

02 Swarm, Compose, or Kubernetes

StaffArchitectural trade-off

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Eight services ran on Compose in staging, and I owned the call on what runs them in production, for a six-person team with no dedicated platform engineer. I timeboxed a two-week spike rather than debating it in meetings: the same stack, deployed once onto Docker Swarm (`docker stack deploy` from a lightly extended `compose.yaml`, native overlay networking, rolling updates, secrets) and once onto a managed Kubernetes cluster. Swarm got us to a working production topology in days — it's built into the Engine, and our Compose files needed only a `deploy:` stanza. But it had no autoscaling primitive equivalent to an HPA, weaker native ingress routing, and overlay networking that, per Docker's own release notes, has needed repeated reliability work release over release. Kubernetes gave us horizontal autoscaling and a much deeper hiring pool who already knew the tooling, at the cost of about three weeks of ramp-up and a genuinely bigger surface to operate and patch. I chose Kubernetes — not because Swarm was worse for our current traffic, but because our roadmap had a 10x growth number in it and I didn't want to re-platform under pressure a year later. I wrote the decision down with the actual spike numbers so nobody would relitigate it from memory. What I'd do differently: I'd have scoped the spike to include a failure-injection test (kill a node mid-deploy) on both, since that's exactly the scenario that later validated the choice during a real node loss.

The mistake is dismissing Swarm as a toy without acknowledging it was the technically correct call at that scale, or picking Kubernetes and not owning the ongoing operational cost you took on.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why not just stay on Compose with a reverse proxy in front?

Compose alone has no cross-host scheduling or failover — losing the one box takes everything down. It's the right call for a single low-stakes service, but not for eight services a team depends on for uptime.

What if the team had stayed at three engineers instead of growing?

I'd likely have stayed on Swarm — its operational cost is much lower, and three engineers can't absorb Kubernetes's ramp-up on top of feature work. The decision is a function of team capacity as much as traffic.

At what scale does Swarm's overlay networking start to hurt?

In practice it's connection-count and node-churn driven more than raw request volume — high-cardinality service-to-service traffic and frequent rolling updates are where its reliability has needed the most upstream work. A stable, moderate-node deployment can run it comfortably for a long time.

What did you trade away by choosing Kubernetes?

Three weeks of team ramp-up we could have spent on features, plus a permanently larger operational surface — more moving parts to patch, secure, and understand deeply enough to debug at 3am.

What would you do differently now?

Run the failure-injection test in the spike itself rather than discovering the answer during a real incident, and set an explicit revisit date on the decision so it doesn't calcify past the point it's still correct.

03 The night the payments API OOM-looped

SeniorProduction incident

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Paged at 2am: the payments API container was restarting every 40–60 seconds, and each restart briefly exhausted the database connection pool as it reconnected, which is what actually paged us — not the restarts themselves. docker inspect showed exit code 137 on every cycle: OOM-killed by the cgroup, not crashed. The container had a --memory=512m limit set months earlier, and the JVM's -Xmx was set to 480m — leaving almost no headroom for off-heap memory, thread stacks, or metaspace, all of which live inside the same cgroup limit the JVM heap does. A traffic bump that day pushed metaspace and thread count up just enough to tip it over, and the kernel's OOM killer took the whole process, not just "the memory it asked for." I raised the container limit to 1Gi, dropped -Xmx to 640m to leave real headroom, added --memory-reservation=768m as a soft ceiling that shows up before a hard kill, and added a Prometheus alert on cgroup memory usage approaching the hard limit instead of only alerting on restarts after the fact. What I'd do differently: I'd have caught this in load testing if we'd tested with the same memory limit as production instead of an unlimited local run — the limit itself was never exercised until real traffic did it for us.

What the interviewer is really probing: incident-command judgment under pressure and whether you understand the OOM-kill mechanism, not just "we bumped the memory." A weak answer stops at "we gave it more RAM"; a strong one explains why the JVM's own limit and the cgroup limit have to be set in relation to each other, and names the monitoring gap that let it get this far before anyone knew.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does exit code 137 mean OOM-kill specifically?

137 is 128 + 9 — SIGKILL — and the cgroup's OOM killer sends SIGKILL, not SIGTERM, because it needs the process gone immediately to reclaim memory. A graceful shutdown (SIGTERM, 143) is a different failure entirely and points away from memory.

What if raising the memory limit had just delayed the same failure at a slightly higher traffic level?

That's exactly why I added reservation-based alerting instead of only raising the ceiling — a limit increase without headroom tracking just moves the cliff edge, it doesn't remove it.

At what scale does this class of failure change shape?

At higher replica counts, a shared root cause (a bad deploy, a traffic spike) OOM-kills many pods near-simultaneously, and the real risk shifts from one container's restart to a thundering-herd reconnect storm against the database — which is the exact mechanism that paged us here, just at a larger blast radius.

What did you trade away with a higher memory limit?

Density — fewer instances fit per node at the same total memory budget, so it's a real cost decision, not a free fix, and I'd frame it that way to whoever owns the infra budget.

How does cgroup v1 versus v2 change this diagnosis?

v2's unified hierarchy gives cleaner memory-pressure signals (memory.pressure) than v1's separate controllers, so on a v2 host I'd expect to catch the approaching limit earlier from PSI metrics alone. cgroup v1 is deprecated in Engine 29 with support continuing to at least May 2029, so I'd flag any host still on v1 as a migration item, not just accept it.

What would you do differently now?

Bake the production memory limit into the load-testing environment as a hard requirement, not an afterthought — the limit itself is part of the system under test, and it never was, until real traffic tested it for us.

04 Cutting over to the containerd image store

StaffMigration / upgrade

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Since Engine 29.0.0, fresh installs default to the containerd image store instead of the legacy graph-driver stack (overlay2 and friends), but an existing host keeps whatever it already had until you opt in. I owned rolling our fleet from legacy overlay2 storage onto the containerd store ahead of a deprecation deadline. The upside was real: better multi-platform image handling, and image data that's directly usable by containerd-native tooling instead of Docker's own bespoke format. The migration itself is effectively one-way in practice — once a host's images live in the content store, going back means re-pulling everything on the old driver, so I staged it host-by-host behind a canary rather than as a fleet-wide flag flip. The change that actually bit us wasn't the storage engine — it was that docker image ls was redesigned in the same 29.0.0 release to show a collapsed tree view and hide untagged images by default. A cleanup script that counted docker images | wc -l to decide whether pruning was needed suddenly under-counted, because it silently stopped seeing untagged layers it used to count. I caught it in staging because I diffed script output before and after on the same host, not because anyone read the release notes line about it. Fixed by switching the script to docker image ls --all --format with explicit fields instead of parsing the human table at all. What I'd do differently: treat "redesigned output" as a breaking change to anything parsing it, and grep the whole fleet's tooling for docker images | or docker ps | pipelines before any Engine major bump, not after.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you read release notes for behavioral changes, not just feature announcements, and whether you have a rollback plan for something that's structurally hard to roll back. The mistake candidates make is describing the shiny new feature and skipping the boring tooling breakage that's what actually causes migration pain in practice.

  • Have you ever staged an infrastructure migration behind a canary because rollback was expensive?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why is the containerd image store hard to roll back from?

Images get re-materialized into containerd's content-addressable store rather than the old graph driver's layout, so reverting means the old driver no longer has the data in its expected shape — practically, you re-pull rather than convert back.

What if a host had no room to re-pull the whole image set during a canary rollback?

I'd pre-stage disk headroom as a canary precondition, or narrow the canary to hosts with spare capacity first — a rollback plan that can't actually execute isn't a rollback plan.

At what fleet size does host-by-host canarying become impractical?

Past a few hundred hosts I'd move to a percentage-based rollout with automated health gating rather than hand-picking canaries, but the principle — verify tooling against the new output before trusting it fleet-wide — doesn't change.

What did you trade away by staging it slowly?

Time — the deprecated path stayed live longer than ideal, which is a real cost if the deprecation carries its own risk (an unpatched legacy driver, say), so I'd weigh migration speed against that risk explicitly next time.

What would you do differently now?

Build a small compatibility-check script that runs common fleet tooling against both output formats before the migration starts, turning "we found it in staging" into "we knew before we touched a single host."

05 The rootful vs. rootless standoff

SeniorTechnical disagreement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

A security-minded colleague pushed hard for rootless Docker fleet-wide: no root daemon means a container-breakout bug has a much smaller blast radius, since the daemon itself isn't running as root on the host. I didn't disagree with the security case — I pushed back on doing it fleet-wide in one move, because rootless historically paid for that safety with real operational friction: slirp4netns networking was measurably slower than the default bridge, binding privileged ports under 1024 needed extra plumbing, and resource limits needed cgroups v2 delegation that not every one of our older hosts had configured. We compromised: rootless for anything internet-facing and high-risk first, rootful (with seccomp/AppArmor defaults intact) for internal batch jobs where the friction wasn't worth it yet. That compromise aged well — Engine 29.5.0 replaced slirp4netns with gvisor-tap-vsock as the default rootless network driver specifically to close the performance gap, and 29.8.0 shipped RootlessKit v3.1.0 with a new port driver, so the operational case against rootless has kept getting weaker exactly as we assumed it would. What I'd do differently: I'd have written the compromise down as a dated decision with a re-evaluation trigger tied to specific Engine releases, instead of it living only in a Slack thread that's now hard to find.

What the interviewer is really probing: how you handle a disagreement where the other person is right on the merits but wrong on timing or blast radius, and whether you can land on "yes, and" instead of a binary win/lose. The mistake is either capitulating without a plan or refusing to move at all — both read as poor judgment for this level.

    hint

  • Have you ever proposed a staged compromise instead of an all-or-nothing rollout?

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does rootless meaningfully reduce blast radius specifically?

The daemon and container processes run as an unprivileged user mapped via user namespaces, so a container-escape bug lands the attacker in an unprivileged context on the host rather than as root — it doesn't prevent escapes, it caps what one is worth.

What if the internal batch jobs you left rootful turned out to process untrusted input?

Then my risk classification was wrong and they should have been in the first wave — this is exactly the kind of assumption I'd want a second pair of eyes on before finalizing the split, not after.

At what scale does a fleet-wide rootless flip become worth the remaining friction?

Once the performance gap is close enough that nobody notices, which the gvisor-tap-vsock default and newer RootlessKit port drivers are actively closing — I'd re-run the original benchmark on current Engine before assuming the old numbers still apply.

What did you trade away with the staged compromise?

Consistency — two operational models running side by side for longer, which is its own maintenance cost and a source of "which mode is this host in" confusion if it isn't tracked explicitly.

What would you do differently now?

Write the compromise as a dated ADR with an explicit re-evaluation trigger tied to Engine release notes, so the decision updates itself on schedule instead of needing someone to remember a Slack thread.

06 DNS that only failed under load

MidNasty debugging session

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

One service intermittently failed to resolve another service's name — only under load, only for a few seconds at a time, and never reproducibly on demand. Logs showed getaddrinfo timeouts against Docker's embedded DNS server on the bridge network. I eventually caught it with tcpdump inside the container during a synthetic load spike: UDP DNS packets were being dropped under connection-storm conditions, and the client's resolv.conf had the default ndots:0-adjacent search behavior triggering extra lookups for what should have been a single internal query, multiplying the DNS load exactly when the network was already busiest. The fix had two parts: reduce unnecessary lookups by using fully-qualified internal names so the resolver didn't need multiple attempts, and move the highest-QPS service-to-service calls off ad hoc DNS lookups onto a cached service-discovery layer that only re-resolved on a TTL, not per request. The intermittent failures disappeared under the same load test that used to reproduce them in minutes. What I'd do differently: I burned almost a full day assuming it was an application-level connection-pool bug before I thought to look at the network layer at all — I'd start with a packet capture under load much earlier next time, since "works most of the time" is a strong hint to look at something probabilistic like packet loss, not application logic.

What the interviewer is really probing: systematic debugging under an intermittent, load-dependent symptom, and whether you know where the embedded DNS server's real limits are rather than treating it as an opaque black box. The mistake is jumping straight to "add a retry" without understanding why the failure happens, which just hides the same problem at slightly higher load.

(d) Follow-up drill chain

Why does UDP DNS traffic degrade under exactly this kind of load, mechanically?

UDP has no built-in retransmission or congestion control, so when the network path is saturated, DNS packets simply get dropped rather than queued or slowed — and a client that doesn't retry aggressively just sees a timeout with no other signal.

What if fully-qualifying names hadn't been enough on its own?

I'd have moved to TCP for DNS if supported end to end, or put a local caching resolver (like a per-node dnsmasq or a sidecar) between the container and the embedded DNS server to absorb the burst instead of hitting it directly every time.

At what scale does relying on ad hoc per-request DNS lookups become a systemic risk rather than an edge case?

Roughly wherever service-to-service call volume starts to rival the DNS server's own throughput ceiling — past that point, every additional request is also an additional resolution, and the two scale together until one of them breaks first.

What did you trade away moving high-QPS calls to a cached discovery layer?

Freshness — a TTL-based cache means a just-changed endpoint isn't picked up instantly, so I sized the TTL against how often endpoints actually changed in practice, not against a theoretical worst case.

What would you do differently now?

Reach for a packet capture under synthetic load on day one of an intermittent, load-correlated bug, instead of spending most of a day on application-layer theories that intermittency alone should have made me suspicious of.

07 Docker Hub throttled us at 3am

SeniorScaling challenge

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

An autoscale event during a traffic spike meant dozens of new nodes pulling the same base images from Docker Hub within a few minutes, and midway through, new containers started failing to start with pull errors. We'd hit Docker Hub's pull-rate limit — our nodes were pulling anonymously or on a low-tier authenticated account, and a burst scale-out looks, from the registry's side, exactly like the abusive traffic the limits exist to stop. The immediate fix was ugly but fast: fail over pulls to a mirrored copy of the same images in our own private registry so new nodes stopped depending on Hub entirely during the incident. The durable fix was a pull-through cache/registry mirror sitting in front of Docker Hub for all base-image pulls, so steady-state traffic hits our own infrastructure and only cache misses go external, plus switching CI and node bootstrapping to authenticated pulls with a plan tier sized for our actual burst pattern, not our average one. What I'd do differently: I'd have load-tested the autoscale path against realistic pull volume before it ever needed to handle a real spike — nobody had modeled "fifty nodes pull the same image in the same sixty seconds" as a load scenario at all, because it's an infrastructure dependency, not application traffic, and it fell through the gap between two teams' test plans.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you understand a dependency's failure mode from its owner's perspective (a registry protecting itself, not "being unreliable"), and whether you separate the fast mitigation from the durable fix in how you tell it. The mistake is treating a third-party rate limit as a bug to route around forever rather than a capacity problem to plan for.

Situation

16 The registry bill that tripled

StaffCost optimisation

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Our private registry storage bill roughly tripled over two quarters with no corresponding growth in the number of services we ran. docker system df on a sample of build hosts and a scan of the registry itself showed the actual driver: every CI build pushed a new tag, nothing ever pruned untagged or superseded images, and multi-arch builds were producing and storing full images for platforms almost nothing actually deployed to. What the interviewer is really probing: whether you connect infrastructure hygiene to a real dollar number, and whether you fix the systemic cause (retention policy, build matrix) rather than doing a one-time manual cleanup that just regrows. The mistake is a story that ends at "we deleted a bunch of old images," with no mechanism preventing the same regrowth.

18 Crashloop backoff at 3:14am

MidOn-call firefighting

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Paged for a service stuck in a restart loop, backing off further each cycle — 10 seconds, then 20, then 40 — which meant it was serving less and less traffic the longer the incident ran. docker inspect showed a growing restart count and, critically, an exit code of 1 with a clean-looking log line right before each restart — not 137, so not an OOM-kill, and not 143, so not a graceful shutdown signal from the orchestrator either. The actual cause was a `HEALTHCHECK` misconfigured with too short a `start_period`: a real, slow-but-legitimate startup dependency (a cache warm-up step) hadn't finished by the time the health check began failing it, and the restart policy dutifully killed and restarted a container that would have become healthy on its own within another 15 seconds if left alone. Immediate fix: manually restart with the health check's start grace period temporarily disabled to break the loop and restore service. Root-cause fix: extend `start_period` to comfortably exceed the real warm-up time, and add a distinct alert on restart count itself so the next instance of this pattern pages before the exponential backoff has fully throttled traffic to near zero. What I'd do differently: I'd have load-tested the health check's timing against the actual cold-start path before this ever reached production — the health check had been tuned against a warm-cache dev environment where the timing looked fine.

What the interviewer is really probing: methodical triage under pressure — distinguishing exit codes, checking the restart policy's own behavior, not just the app logs — and whether you both restore service and prevent recurrence. The mistake is a story that stops at "we restarted it and it was fine," with no explanation of why it was looping in the first place.

Why does exponential backoff make this worse the longer it runs, specifically?

Each cycle serves traffic for a shrinking fraction of the growing interval, so total availability during the incident window keeps degrading even though nothing about the underlying problem is getting worse — the backoff policy itself becomes part of the outage's shape.

Any time the startup dependency it's waiting on changes — a bigger cache to warm, a slower migration — the grace period is only correct for the startup profile it was tuned against, and it drifts stale silently otherwise.

19 Docker-in-Docker was our flakiest test

SeniorCI / reliability improvement

(a) Worked example EXAMPLE — replace with your own

Our CI's most-retried job, by a wide margin, was the one that built and ran a container inside a container using privileged Docker-in-Docker — somewhere around one in eight runs failed for reasons that looked unrelated to the actual code change: storage-driver conflicts between the outer and inner daemon, or the privileged container simply not being available yet when the job's first step tried to use it. Rather than adding more retries, which treats the symptom, I moved the build step off DinD entirely onto a remote Buildx builder that CI connects to over the network instead of spinning up a nested daemon locally — no privileged mode, no nested storage driver, and a warm shared cache across every run instead of a cold one per job. The image-testing step that still genuinely needed to run a container was separated out and given a scoped, non-privileged sidecar instead of a full nested daemon. Flake rate on that job dropped from roughly 12% to under 1%, and it got noticeably faster too, since the remote builder's cache was warm from other jobs. What I'd do differently: I'd have measured *why* DinD was flaky before assuming retries were the answer — the team had been quietly living with the retry-and-hope pattern for months, and it took someone actually reading the failure logs closely to see the same two or three root causes recurring instead of random noise.

What the interviewer is really probing: whether you fix the actual reliability mechanism instead of papering over flakiness with retries, and whether you understand why DinD specifically is fragile (nested storage drivers, privileged mode) rather than just "it's known to be flaky." Why is privileged Docker-in-Docker specifically prone to this kind of flakiness?

22 Night-before cheat sheet

final review

Ten sentences to read once, out loud, the night before. Not new content — a compression of everything above into what you actually need to remember walking in.

  1. Open every story with the situation in one plain sentence before any detail — the interviewer is oriented in the first five seconds or not at all.
  2. Every story needs a number: a percentage, a time, a dollar figure, a count. If you don't have one, say the honest qualitative version instead of inventing a precise-sounding fake.
  3. Always answer "what would you do differently" with something specific, not "communicate better" — name the concrete check, default, or policy that should have existed.
  4. Say "I" for what you actually did and "we" for what the team did — conflating them either steals credit or hides your own contribution, and interviewers notice both.
  5. Every technical decision had a trade-off; name what you gave up, not just what you gained — a story with no downside sounds rehearsed, not real.
  6. Name the actual constraint you were under (deadline, headcount, an inherited system) — it's what makes a decision legible instead of arbitrary.
  7. For an incident or security story, state what changed in your process afterward, not just that the immediate issue got fixed — a fix without a process change invites the same question twice.
  8. Keep any version numbers or specifics you cite plausible for the current stable release you'd actually be running — a wildly outdated detail undercuts an otherwise strong story.
  9. If asked "at what scale does this break," answer with the actual mechanism (a lock, a queue, a cache-hit ratio) — not a guess dressed up as an estimate.
  10. Never fake a story you don't have — say plainly you haven't hit that situation yet and reason out loud how you'd approach it; that reads as more senior than a fabricated example.
